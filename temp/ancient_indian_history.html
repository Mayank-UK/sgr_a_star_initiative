- People who study past

        - Scientists who study the past by examining material remains left behind by humans, such as tools, pottery, buildings, and other artifacts
        - Their primary goal is to understand human history and culture, from prehistoric times to more recent periods
        - They involve excavation, surveying, and dating methods
        - Reconstructs past lifestyles, economies, and environments
    - Historians
        - Historians are scholars who study, analyze, and interpret the past through written records, such as manuscripts, documents, and inscriptions
        - They involve research, analysis, and interpretation of texts
        - Produces narratives and interpretations of historical events
- Dating convention
    - BC and AD System
        - BC (Before Christ)
            - Refers to the years before the traditionally recognized birth year of Jesus Christ
        - AD (Anno Domini)
            - Latin for “In the Year of Our Lord,” referring to the years after the birth of Christ
            - 1 AD was 2025 years ago from today (2025 AD)
    - BCE and CE System
        - BCE (Before Common Era)
            - Equivalent to BC but avoids religious connotations
        - CE (Common Era)
            - Equivalent to AD but is a secular alternative
            - 1 BCE was 2025 years ago from today (2025 CE)
    - Other dating systems
        - Indian Calendar 
            - Saka Era
                - Officially used in India alongside the Gregorian calendar
                - Starts in 78 AD
            - Vikram Samvat
                - Begins in 57 BC, commonly used in northern India
        - Islamic Hijri Calendar
            - Based on the lunar cycle
            - Dates are counted from 622 AD, the year of Prophet Muhammad's migration (Hijra) to Medina
        - Chinese Calendar
            - Based on lunar and solar cycles
            - Years are associated with the 12-animal zodiac system
        - Jewish Calendar
            - A lunisolar calendar starting from the biblical creation year (3761 BCE)
        - Mayan Calendar
            - Includes the “Long Count” system to track extensive cycles of time
    - How Historians Interpret Dates
        - Circa (c. or ca.)
            - Used when the exact date of an event is uncertain, e.g., c. 300 BCE
        - BP (Before Present)
            - Used in archaeology and science to date objects, with “present” set as 1950
        - Epoch Markers
            - Specific events used as reference points, such as the founding of Rome in the Roman calendar
            - Epoch
                - An epoch refers to a distinct period in the timeline of history, often defined by significant events, developments, or changes that mark a shift in the course of history
                - Example
                    - The Victorian Epoch (Time during Queen Victoria's reign in the UK (1837 - 1901))
                    - The Classical Epoch (Period of ancient Greece and Rome)
- Sites
    - These are places where remains of things (tools, pots, buildings, etc) were found
    - Earlier and later levels

- Eras
    - Prehistoric era
        - The time before written history, including periods like the Stone Age, Bronze Age, and Iron Age, where early humans lived and evolved without written records
        - It includes
            - Paleolithic (Palaeolithic) Era (Old Stone Age): 2.5 millions years ago to 10,000 BCE, it's end is recognized by climate change (to warmer sides)
            - Mesolithic Era (Middle Stone Age): 10,000 BCE to around 8,000 BCE (varies by region)
            - Neolithic Era (New Stone Age): 8,000 BCE to around 3,000 BCE (varies by region)
    - Ancient era
        - The time period where the first civilizations began to develop, usually from the invention of writing (around 3000 BCE) to the fall of the Roman Empire in the West (around 476 CE)
    - Medieval era
        - Also called the Middle Ages, this period lasted from around 476 CE (fall of the Western Roman Empire) to the 15th century (the start of the Renaissance)
    - Modern era
        - The period from the late 15th century to the present day, marked by events like the Renaissance, Industrial Revolution, and the rise of modern nation-states
    - Contemporary era
        - The most recent period of history, often defined as the 20th century and beyond

- Hunter gatherers
    - Who were they
        - They were early people who lived a nomadic lifestyle, they hunted animals and gathered fruits, seeds, etc
    - Why hunter gatherers were on the move
        - Food and resources
            - As the resources in one area became depleted, they would move to new areas where food was more abundant
            - Hunting large animals or gathering seasonal plants required them to follow animal migrations or seasonal growth patterns of plants
        - Climate and environmental changes
            - Changes in the landscape, such as droughts, floods, or the freezing and thawing of certain regions, could drive movement to ensure the group could sustain itself
            - Shifts in the climate or the seasons often made certain areas less habitable or resource-rich, forcing groups to move to more favorable environments
        - Social and cultural factors
            - Avoid conflicts with other tribes or groups
            - Some groups practiced a form of social structure where they would split and rejoin, ensuring genetic diversity and access to new resources
        - Technological limitations
            - Without permanent shelter or advanced agriculture, they lacked the means to store large quantities of food for long periods, so they had to continue moving to find fresh sources
    - How do we know about these people and their lifestyle
        - Archaeological evidence
            - Artifacts, cave paintings, rock art, bones and fossils
        - Genetic evidence
            - DNA analysis, mitochondrial DNA
        - Anthropological studies
            - Studying contemporary hunter-gatherer groups, who still exist in some parts of the world
        - Paleo-environmental evidence
            - Plant and animal remains
        - Linguistic evidence
            - Studying the evolution of modern languages and linguistic patterns

- Early cities
    - Indus valley civilization / Harappan civilization
        - Harappa was the earliest city to be discovered so it's also called Harappan civilization, and Harappan cities, these cities developed 4700 years ago
        - Story of Harappa city
            - Harappa was discovered during the laying of railway lines, it was not given much importance and it's remains were used as source of ready made materials like bricks during the railway construction
        - Special about Harappan cities
            - Division of city into two or more parts
                - City was divided into two or more parts larger and lower and smaller and upper, upper part was citadel and used for special buildings
                    - Citadel
                        - Fortified area or stronghold within a city at its highest point for defense, administration, urban organization
                - Planned houses, drains and streets
                    - One or two story tall houses with courtyard, wells and separated bathing area , drainage system with inter-connected network, planned streets
                - Special buildings
                    - Mohenjodaro's Great Bath: Probably used by important people for special occasions
                    - Kalibangan and Lothal: Fire altars for sacrifices
                    - Granaries: Found in Harappa, Mohenjodaro, and Lothal
        - Life in the Harappan city
            - Busy city with rulers who planned
            - Remains found like crafts, terracotta toys, materials gathered from different locations like precious stones, metals, etc indicating diverse city lifestyle
        - Crafts in the Harappan city
            - Remains found include stone items, shells, metals (copper, bronze, gold and silver) used for weapons, ornaments, vessels. Beautiful pots with designs indicating they were specialist
            - Seals and designed pots, cotton remains from cloth, copper objects, spindle whorls made of terracotta and faience in Mohenjodaro
            - Terracotta
                - Terracotta means baked earth. Clay based ceramic metal to create durable porous objects
            - Faience
                - A gum was used to shape and sand a powdered quarts into an object like beads, bangles, earrings, tiny vessels, etc
        - Raw materials for these cities
            - Harappans probably got copper materials from Rajasthan or Oman, Tin from Afghanistan and Iran, Gold from Karnataka, etc
        - Food for people in these cities
            - Supply of fruits, crops and animals from countryside (includes domestication, hunting, gathering)
            - Grew wheat, barley, pulses, peas, rice, sesame, mustard and reared buffalo, goat, etc
            - Use of plough and irrigation using stored water (because region has low rainfall)
        - Major harappan cities
            - Harappa
                - Location
                    - Present-day Punjab, Pakistan, along the Ravi River
                - Key Features
                    - The first city discovered (1921) gave the civilization its name
                    - Large granaries, citadel, and residential areas
                    - Evidence of seals, beads, and terracotta figurines
            - Mohenjo-Daro
                - Location
                    - Present-day Sindh, Pakistan, near the Indus River
                - Key Features
                    - Famous for the Great Bath, used for ceremonial purposes
                    - Advanced drainage system and grid-patterned streets
                    - Artifacts like the “Priest-King” statue and “Dancing Girl” bronze sculpture were found here
            - Dholavira
                - Location
                    - Present-day Gujarat, India, located on Khadir Beyt near the Rann of Kutch
                - Key Features
                    - One of the largest Harappan sites, with a unique water management system (reservoirs and dams)
                    - Divided into three sections: citadel, middle town, and lower town
                    - Stone inscriptions in the Harappan script were found here
            - Lothal
                - Location
                    Present-day Gujarat, India, beside a tributary to Sabarmati near the Gulf of Khambhat
                - Key Features
                    - Known for its dockyard, indicating maritime trade
                    - Evidence of bead-making, shell-work, and jewelry production
                    - Houses with bathrooms connected to a drainage system
            - Kalibangan
                - Location
                    - Present-day Rajasthan, India, along the Ghaggar River
                - Key Features
                    - Evidence of fire altars, suggesting religious practices
                    - Houses with mudbrick foundations
                    - Plowed field remains, indicating advanced agricultural practices
            - Rakhigarhi
                - Location
                    - Present-day Haryana, India
                - Key Features
                    - One of the largest Harappan settlements
                    - Evidence of multi-roomed houses and a drainage system
                    - Artifacts like beads, bangles, and pottery were discovered
            - Chanhu-Daro
                - Location
                    - Present-day Sindh, Pakistan
                - Key Features
                    - Focused on manufacturing beads, bangles, and seals
                    - No citadel, indicating it was likely a craft production center
        - Mysterious end of these harappan cities
            - Around 3900 years ago, people stopped living in many of these cities
            - Study showed that in later levels, raw materials became rare, garbage piled up, drainage choked, unplanned construction
            - Reasons may include river dried up, deforestation, rulers lost control, flooding, etc

    - Vedas : 
        - The Vedas are the oldest and most authoritative scriptures of Hinduism. They are a collection of hymns, rituals, and philosophical teachings composed in Sanskrit between around 1500 BCE and 500 BCE
        - Types of Vedas
            - Rigveda
                - Date 
                    - The oldest of the Vedas, dating back to around 1500 BCE
                - Content 
                    - It consists of hymns (suktas) dedicated to various deities such as Agni (fire), Indra (king of gods), and Soma (a plant used for rituals). These hymns describe the creation of the world and offer praises to the forces of nature
                - Significance 
                    - It is considered the foundation of Vedic religion and contains important philosophical ideas about life, cosmology, and the gods
            - Yajurveda
                - Date
                    - Developed around the same time as the Rigveda
                - Content
                    - Primarily a guide to performing rituals and sacrifices. It is composed of mantras and prose. The Yajurveda is divided into two main parts: Shukla (White) and Krishna (Black)
                - Significance
                    - It focuses on the practical aspects of religious life, particularly the correct performance of Vedic rituals and ceremonies
            - Samaveda
                - Date
                    - Compiled around 1000 BCE
                - Content
                    - Known as the “Veda of melodies,” it is a collection of chants and musical notes for reciting the hymns in the Rigveda during rituals. It emphasizes the musical aspect of hymns
                - Significance
                    - The Samaveda is closely tied to the performance of sacrificial rituals and is considered the foundation for the classical Indian music tradition
            - Atharvaveda
                - Date
                    - Compiled around 1000 BCE
                - Content
                    - A combination of hymns, spells, and incantations, the Atharvaveda deals with more practical aspects of life, such as healing, magic, and protection. It also includes philosophical discussions
                - Significance
                    - Unlike the other Vedas, which focus more on rituals, the Atharvaveda is considered a more practical and secular text, dealing with issues like health, prosperity, and everyday concerns
        - Structure of the Vedas
            - Each Veda is composed of four main parts
                - Samhitas
                    - These are the collections of hymns and mantras that form the core of the Vedas. They are chanted or recited during rituals
                - Brahmanas
                    - These texts explain the meaning of the hymns and provide detailed instructions on the performance of rituals and sacrifices
                - Aranyakas
                    - These texts are associated with meditation and spiritual practices and were often written by hermits or ascetics living in forests
                - Upanishads
                    - The final and most philosophical part of the Vedas. The Upanishads explore deep philosophical questions about the nature of existence, the self (Atman), and the universe (Brahman). They mark a transition from ritual-based to more meditative and metaphysical thought

    - Varna system
        - Mentioned in the Vedic texts, especially in the Purusha Sukta of the Rigveda
        - Evolution
            - Early Vedic Period (Work-Based Varna System)
                - The Rigveda refers to the varnas in the Purusha Sukta, where the four varnas are described as originating from different parts of the cosmic being (Purusha)
                    - This was primarily a functional and skill-based classification
                        - Brahmins
                            - Those who pursued knowledge, performed rituals, and preserved spiritual texts
                        - Kshatriyas
                            - Those skilled in governance and warfare
                        - Vaishyas
                            - Those engaged in trade, agriculture, and wealth generation
                        - Shudras
                            - Those who provided labor and services
            - Later Vedic Period (Birth-Based Varna System)
                - As society became more structured and stratified, varna gradually became hereditary
                - The Dharma Shastras (like the Manusmriti) reinforced this hereditary model, emphasizing that one's varna was determined by birth
                - Social mobility between varnas became restricted
                - Manusmriti also had rules to prevent shudras from reading veda
                - Women were often grouped with shudras
                - Later added classification of untouchables : hunters & gatherers, people wo did burials & cremations

    - Burial spots
        - Megaliths
            - Megaliths are large stone structures or monuments associated with burials or memorials, primarily found in the Neolithic, Chalcolithic, and Iron Age periods
            - Practice of marking burial sites started around 3000 years ago prevalent through many parts of India (deccan, south, north-east, kashmir)
            - Purpose
                - Often associated with burial practices and rituals
                - Served as commemorative markers for the dead
            - Types
                - Cist Burials
                    - Stone slabs forming a box-like burial chamber
                - Dolmens
                    - Large flat stones placed on upright stones, forming a table-like structure
                - Menhirs
                    - Single, upright standing stones, often marking graves
                - Stone Circles
                    - Circular arrangements of stones, sometimes enclosing burial sites
                - Sarcophagi
                    - Stone coffins or containers for the dead
            - Cultural and Historical Significance
                - Social Practices and differenes
                    - Indicates complex burial rituals and a belief in the afterlife
                    - Some megaliths contain more valuable objects than others indicating social differences between rich, poor, chiefs, etc
                    - Some megaliths contain more than one skeleton probably of same familty
                - Material Culture
                    - Associated grave goods like pottery, iron tools, beads, and ornaments suggest economic and technological advancements
                - Iron Age Context
                    - Many megaliths date to the Iron Age (1200 BCE - 600 BCE), reflecting the transition to settled life
                - Community Organization
                    - Construction required collective effort, hinting at early forms of social organization
            - Important megalithic sites in India
                - Brahmagiri (Karnataka)
                    - Excavations revealed megalithic burials and artifacts, such as iron implements and pottery
                - Hire Benakal (Karnataka)
                    - Known for hundreds of dolmens and considered a UNESCO World Heritage Site
                - Adichanallur (Tamil Nadu)
                    - Rich in grave goods like bronze and iron objects, beads, and pottery
                - Jorwe (Maharashtra)
                    - Chalcolithic and megalithic remains found here
                - Nartiang (Meghalaya)
                    - Famous for its menhirs and stone monoliths

    - Janpadas
        - Charactericts
            - Early Formation
                - Initially, Janapadas were small, clan-based regions where tribes settled as they transitioned from pastoralism to agriculture
                - The tribal chiefs or kings governed these regions
            - Agricultural Basis
                - With the advent of agriculture, people began to settle in specific regions, leading to the growth of Janapadas
                - These areas became centers of economic activity due to farming, trade, and crafts
            - Social Structure
                - Society in Janapadas was divided into varna (class) and jati (caste)
                - A strong system of governance and law emerged to regulate social and economic life
            - Political Organization
                - Each Janapada was independent and ruled by a raja (king) or tribal leader
                - Assemblies like the Sabha and Samiti often played a role in governance
            - Boundaries
                - Janapadas were marked by defined territorial boundaries, often fortified to protect from invasions
                - They were distinct from earlier tribal communities that lacked permanent settlements
            - Cultural Identity
                - Each Janapada developed its own culture, language, and traditions, which were influenced by geography and the people inhabiting the area
        - Significance of Janapadas
            - Marked the transition from a tribal to a territorial society.
            - Laid the foundation for urbanization and the formation of states in ancient India.
            - Represented the shift towards more organized political and social systems.
        - Important Janapadas Mentioned in Ancient Texts
            - Kuru (modern Haryana and Delhi)
            - Panchala (modern western Uttar Pradesh)
            - Kosala (modern eastern Uttar Pradesh)
            - Videha (modern northern Bihar)
        - Mahajanpadas (means Great Janapada)
            - Over time, small Janapadas merged or grew in size, giving rise to the Mahajanapadas (greater realms), around 6th century BCE. These were larger kingdoms or republics with advanced political systems
            - Important Mahajanapadas
                - Magadha (present-day Bihar)
                    - First important mahajanpada, very powerful, capitals were Rajagriha -> Pataliputra
                    - Had great river system for transport, fertile land, water supply, dense forest for wood and war animals (elephants)
                    - Had iron mines for tools and weapons
                    - Rulers = Bimbisara -> Ajatasattu
                - Kosala (present-day Uttar Pradesh)
                - Vrijji/Vijji/Vrajji (northern part of modern-day Bihar)
                    - Another important mahajanpada, capital was Vaishali
                    - No one king, many kings ruled together through republic of gana/sangha
                    - Regular assemblies to take decisions (women and dasas were not allowed)
                    - Budha and Mahavira belonged to sanghas
                    - Later sangha was conquered by Gupta empire
                    - Story of Ajatasattu and Budha
                        - Ajatasattu wanted to attack vajji and asked buddha for his advice on the matter
                        - Budha asked if assemblies were held regularly in Vajji to know it's internal stability to which he said yes, and budha replied it will be difficult to defeat if so
                - Lichhavi (Located in present-day Nepal and parts of Bihar)
    - Taxes

        - Earlier taxation included different types of taxes from different occupations
            - Herders = animals
