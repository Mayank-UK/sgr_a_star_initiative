    - Causes of Decline of Mughal Empire
        - Aurangzeb's misguided policies
            - His reimposition of Jizya in 1679 alienated Rajputs, Marathas, and Sikhs; prolonged Deccan campaigns from 1681-1707 drained treasury and military resources.
        - Weak successors
            - Post-Aurangzeb rulers like Bahadur Shah I, Jahandar Shah, and Muhammad Shah lacked the military prowess or administrative skill to maintain centralized control.
        - Wars of succession & Role of kingmakers
            - Frequent succession conflicts, such as Jahandar Shah vs. Farrukh Siyar, empowered nobles like Zulfiqar Khan and Sayyid Brothers to manipulate emperors, weakening imperial authority.
        - Nature of mughal state
            - A centralized feudal system dependent on loyal governors crumbled as provincial loyalty eroded after Aurangzeb, leading to fragmentation.
        - Degeneration of Mughal nobility
            - Factionalism between Irani and Turani nobles, coupled with corruption—e.g., revenue farming under Jahandar Shah—undermined governance and military cohesion.
        - Jagirdari Crisis
            - Over-allocation of jagirs—land grants—by the early 18th century reduced imperial revenue, as nobles hoarded income, crippling the state financially.
        - Rise of Regional powers
            - Marathas, Sikhs, and successor states like Hyderabad exploited Mughal weakness, asserting autonomy and challenging imperial dominance by the 1730s.
        - Invasions of Irani & Durrani Kingdoms
            - Nadir Shah’s 1739 sack of Delhi and Ahmad Shah Abdali’s seven invasions—e.g., 1748, 1757—looted wealth and annexed territories like Punjab, exposing Mughal vulnerability.
        - Advent of European Powers
            - East India Company’s victories, like Plassey in 1757 and Buxar in 1764, shifted power to the British, reducing Mughals to pensioners by Shah Alam II’s reign.

    - Rise of Regional States
        - 
        - Successor States
            - Hyderabad
                - Hyderabad founded in 1724 by Qlich Khan (Nizam-ul-Mulk)
                - He fought in the Battle of Shaker-Kheda against Mubariz Khan - Viceroy of Deccan at that time.
            - Carnatic
                - In the 18th century, the Mughal sub of Carnatic came under the authority of the Governor of Deccan (Nizam of Hyderabad).
                - Following the lines of the Governor of Deccan, they also declared Independence.
                - Accordingly, the Nawab Saadatullah of Carnatic, appointed his nephew Dost Ali as his successor, without the prior approval of his superior, the Nizam.
                - However, in 1740, Marathas invaded Carnatic, killed Nawab Dost Ali and took his son-in-law Chanda Sahib as prisoner to Satara.
                - Thereafer the affairs of Carnatic deteriorated and this proved an opportunity to European counterparts to interfere in the affairs of Carnatic which eventually led to Carnatic Wars.
            - Bengal
                - In Bengal, Murshid Quli Khan started the process of autonomy in about 1717.
                - He was appointed as Diwan of Bengal along with the position of Subahdar of Bengal given later on. (An Exception).
                - Murshid Quli Khan is also known for his new revenue system introduced in Bengal known as Malzamani System.
                - The Dynastic Rule of Quli Khan was challenged by Alivardi Khan (Battle of Gheria) who assumed power through a coup and killed Sarfaraz Khan.
                - Thus under Alivardi's rule the areas of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa were essentially established as a sovereign state in Eastern India.
                - In 1765, Alivardi Khan died and nominated his grandson Siraj ud Daula as his successor.
            - Awadh
                - Saadat Khan (popularly known as Burhan-ul-Mulk) became the Subahdar of Awadh in 1722, aiming to establish Awadh as an independent power center and decline of the Mughal Empire – provided a perfect opportunity for this.
                - Successors faced numerous rebellions and challenges while at the same time enlarging their empire.
                - Safdar Jung and later on Shuja ud Daula, further solidified Awadhʼs autonomous political framework by 1764.
        - New States
            - Marathas
                - 
                - Chhatrapati Shivaji established the Maratha Kingdom in the areas of western Deccan near about the 17th century.
                - Internal conflicts among Maratha chiefs led to the establishment of a new Maratha Governance system under Balaji Vishwanath Peshwa (Prime Minister to King Shahu).
                - First Peshwa – Balaji Vishwanath made the post Hereditary making his son Baji Rao next Peshwa.
                - Under the Baji Rao Maratha confederacy, emerged supreme represented Gaekwads at Baroda, Bhosle at Nagpur, Holkar at Indore, Scandia at Gwalior and Peshwa at Poona.
                - He also captured Bassein and Salsette ports from the Portuguese.
                - Marathas fought the Third Battle of Panipat (1761) against Abdali as a protectorate of the Mughal Empire under Peshwa Balaji Baji Rao, famously known as Nana Saheb.
            - Sikhs
                - Sikh confederacy emerged in response to intense Mughal suppression in the later half of 18th century.
                - Sikhs initially organized themselves under 12 Misls (Groups) each under a local chief.
                - This confederation transitioned into the Sikh empire following the accession of Misldar Ranjit Singh Sukerchakiya (Originally a Governor under the Afghan King) as the sovereign Maharaja of Punjab, marking the beginning of a new Era of Sikh domination in north India.
        - Independent Kingdoms
            - Mysore
                - Mysore historically was not under the Mughal Empire; Mysore was originally a viceroyalty of the Vijayanagar Empire.
                - Mysore was transformed from Viceroyalty to an independent state under Wodeyar Dynasty.
                - Haidar Ali and Tipu Sultan overthrew the Wodeyar rulers in the late 18th century.
            - Kerala
                - Martanda Varma established an independent state of Kerala with Travancore as his capital.
                - He extended the boundaries of his state from Kanyakumari to Cochin.
            - Jats
                - Churaman and Badan Singh succeeded in setting up the Jat Kingdom of Bharatpur in the 18th century,
                - But it was under Suraj Mal that Jat power reached its zenith.
            - Rohilkhand
                - Ali Muhammad Khan took advantage of the collapse of authority in North India following Nadir Shah’s invasion to set up a petty kingdom, Rohilkhand

- Advent of Europeans in India
    - Overview of European Arrival
        - 
        - Introduction
            - In the 15th century, the Ottoman Empire seized control of the sea route to India through the Mediterranean , making it difficult for Europeans to reach India by their traditional route.
            - This prompted European countries to compete with one other from the 15th century onwards to discover new maritime routes to India.
            - This quest for new routes was fuelled by a strong desire to do trade with India, which was regarded as the land abundant with superior goods.
            - By the 17th century, all European nations had established commercial empires in India.
            - Their operations were primarily focused on coastal port cities in India and did not infringe upon the sovereignty of indigenous polities.
            - They utilised existing trade networks to exchange goods for bullion, shifing the balance of foreign trade in Indiaʼs favour and feeling economic growth.
            - However, conflicts emerged among these European powers as they sought to secure trade monopolies and territorial control.
            - By the 1750ʼs British East India Company emerged as a dominant force in India, surpassing all other European trading companies.

            - By 1600 they had established an elaborate system of Eastern Trade from the Persian Gulf to Japan and traded mainly in redistribution of goods to Northern Europe.

    - The English (1608)
        - Establishment of the English East India Company
            - In 1599, a company to trade with the East was formed under the auspices of the group of merchants known as Merchant Adventurers, the Company popularly came to be known as East India Company.
            - In 1600, EIC was granted a royal charter by Queen Elizabeth giving it the exclusive privilege of trading east of the Cape of Good Hope for a period of 15 years.
            - Initially, their main area of interest was Spice Islands in Indonesia, however due to the overwhelming presence of Dutch, they shifed their preference towards India and thus in 1608, Surat in Gujarat was established as the trade transit point and the company ships were docked there.
            - The Company now planned to open up a factory in Surat and sent Captain Hawkins for the same.
                - He was the first Englishmen to set foot on Indian soil.
                - He could speak Turkish and came in a ship called ʻHectorʼ.
                - On arrival, he went to Jahangir's court in Agra.
                - In 1609, the English were given the royal Farman to set up factories on the west Coast.
                - However, Hawkins had to leave India at that time due to Portuguese intrigues.
        - Early English Factories
            - In 1612, The English, under the command of Captain Thomas Best, defeated the Portuguese at Swally near Surat.
            - Impressed by this triumph, Emperor Jahangir granted the English a Royal Farman in 1613 allowing them to open their first permanent factory at Surat in 1613 and trade in Cambar, Ahmedabad and Goa.
                - Imp - First temporary factory at Masulipatnam in 1611.

            - In 1616, English opened their first factory in the south at Masulipatnam. (First one on in South East Coast)

            - 2. In 1639, Francis Day was able to obtain Madras on lease from the Raja of Chandragiri and shifed the centre of their activity to Madras.

        - English Factories in Bengal
            - On the SOUTH-EASTERN coast, the first English factory was set up in Orissa in 1633
            - In 1651, at Hugli, the first English factory was set up in Bengal upon receiving permission from Sultan Shuja.
            - In 1658, all establishments of the Company in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa and Coromandel Coast were brought under the control of Fort St. George.

            - In 1690, Job Charnock established a factory at Sutanati which was fortified in 1697 and called Fort Williams.

            - Later In 1717, Company secured from Mughal Emperor Farukh Siyar a Farman confirming the privileges granted EARLIER and extending them to Gujarat and Deccan. (Mission led by John Surman)

                - In Bengal, Taxes on the Companies import and export were waived off for an annual payment of Rs. 3000.
                - Company was permitted to issue dastaks for the transportation of such goods.
                - Company was permitted to rent more lands around Calcutta.
            - FARMAN OF 1717 - MAIN PROVISIONS -
                - In Hyderabad, the Company retained its existing privileges of freedom from duties in trade and had to pay the prevailing rent only for Madras.
                - In Surat, for an annual payment of Rs. 10000, the EIC was exempted from the levy of all duties..
                - It was decreed that the coins of the Company minted at Bombay were to have currency throughout the Mughal Empire.
            - The original company faced opposition to its monopoly, which led to the establishment of a rival company and the fusion (1708) of the two as the United Company of Merchants of England trading to the East Indies.
            - The United Company was organized into a court of 24 directors who worked through committees.

- Rise of British Supremacy: Carnatic Wars - The Anglo-French Rivalry
    - Overview of British Ascendancy
        - Introduction
            - The mid-18th century saw the gradual transformation of the English East India Company from a trading company into a political power.
            - During the period 1600 to 1744, the slowly expanded its trade network in India, simultaneously easing out the rivals though a strategy combining diplomacy and war.
            - Though the rise of British supremacy in India is usually traced to 1757 with the British victory in the Battle of Plassey, the ground for the victory was laid in South India where the British might was successfully tested out against the French Company.

    - Anglo-French Struggle for Supremacy - The Carnatic Wars
        - 
        - Reasons for Political Power Acquisition
            - Why did these foreign powers start gaining Political Power ?
                - When the decline of Mughal Authority (Nadir Shah Invasion of 1740) and the weak regional powers (Marathas - Nizam of Hyderabad) proved incapable of safeguarding the interests of European companies in India, they saw the necessity to empower themselves militarily.
                - Further, they were commercial entities, seeking to maximize their profit margins, which necessitated the elimination of competition and establishment of monopoly.
                - Not that they only wanted to monopolize their trade and sell dear, but they also wanted to acquire power, necessitating the acquisition of some political control over the country they traded with.
        - Emergence of Rivalry in South India
            - Why this rivalry emerged in the South India
                - In this context, the emergence of hostilities between the English and the French was but natural.
                - South India emerged as the arena for Anglo-French rivalry as it had become the main centre for French activities with Pondicherry as capital.
                - The French had also begun to wield influence in the neighbouring states of Hyderabad and Mysore.
                - Besides, there was not much scope in western India because of powerful Marathas, while Eastern India was under the direct control of Alivardi Khan, therefore it was only in Southern India that conditions became favourable, especially after the death of Nizam-ul-Mulk of Hyderabad in 1748.

    - First Carnatic War (1740-1748)
        - Background of Hostility - Austrian War of Succession
            - 
            - The immediate context for conflict between two European rivals in India was provided by the War of Austrian Succession in Europe which broke out in Europe in 1740 between Austria and Prussia.
            - In this war, Britain and France joined opposite camps.
            - Perhaps conscious of their relatively weaker position, the French tried hard to prevent the extension of war to India and Dupleix (The French General) advised his English counterpart to maintain neutrality.
                - (Dupleix - Introduced the innovative concept of Subsidiary Alliance in India)
            - But the English did not agree, instead deliberately adopting an aggressive stand and seized some French ships off the south-eastern coast of India to provoke France.
            - The French had no fleet in India, hence Dupelix messaged La Bourdonnais (The French Governor of Mauritius) for reinforcements .
            - He immediately siphoned 3000 men towards the Coromandel Coast.
            - As soon as the help arrived, French attacked the English and captured the British possessions in Fort St. George, Madras and nearby areas, and Robert Clive was taken as prisoner of War, and thus the First Carnatic War had begun.
        - Battle of St. Thomas (River Adyar)
            - Anwarrudin, the Nawab of Carnatic, disapproved of the hostilities in his kingdom, thus when the English appealed to him for protection, Nawab asked the French to quit Madras, but the French were in no mood to retreat and neglected the advice.
            - The Nawab now sent a large army to fight the French.
            - A large Indian Army of 10,000 men met a small French army of 230 European and 700 Indians soldiers on the bank of River Adyar near St. Thomas in 1746.
            - But to everyone’s surprise, a handful of properly trained soldiers of the French army routed Anwaruddin’s vast army.
            - This highlighted the superiority of the European army and exposed the weakness of the military of the Indian rulers.
        - End of the War - Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle
            - Thereafter in 18th century political chaos, there was no dearth of Indian princes who would invite Dupleix’s assistance to turn the scales in their favour.
            - In this way, it was Duplex who first showed the way of intervening in disputes of the Indian rulers and thereby acquiring political control - a technique which was later perfected by the English company.
            - Even as the French continued the siege, the War of Austrian Succession came to an end by the Treaty of Aix la Chapelle on the basis of mutual restitution of conquests.
            - This ended the first round of Anglo French rivalry in India too.
            - Madras was restored to the English and the French got Louisburg in North America, and thus the first war ended in a draw.

    - Second Carnatic War (1749-1755)
        - Background - Succession Disputes in Carnatic and Hyderabad
            - Dynastic feuds in South India proved the content for the second round of Anglo-French conflict in the South.
            - The succession disputes at both Carnatic and Hyderabad provided the French Governor General Dupelix an opportunity to intervene in Indian Politics.
            - In 1740, The Marathas had invaded the Carnatic and killed Nawab Dost Ali (Ruler of Carnatic) and took his son-in-law Chanda Sahib as prisoner to Satara.
            - In 1743, The Nizam of Hyderabad (Nizam ul Mulk) felt prompted to come to the Carnatic and appoint Anwarrudin Khan as the Nawab of Carnatic.
                - But in 1748, the Chanda Sahib was freed by the Marathas and the same year, Nizam-ul-Mulk died.
                - His son Nasir Jung succeeded him but his grandson, Muzaffar Jung laid claim to the throne.
            - Muzaffar Jung now allied with Chanda sahib to gain their respective seats in Hyderabad and the Carnatic.
        - Intervention by Dupleix
            - He was able to intervene in the wars of succession that started after the death of Nizam ul Mulk.
            - He pledged support to Muzaffar Jung and Chanda Sahib, thus a tripartite struggle was developed.
            - In August 1749, the three allies ambushed and killed Anwaruddin at the Battle of Ambur, near Vellore.
            - His son Muhammad Ali fled to Trichinopoly and Chanda Sahib became the Nawab of Carnatic and thus began the second phase of the Anglo-French War (2nd Carnatic War).
            - After this the allies marched to Deccan, The French army surprised and killed Nasir Jung and installed Muzaffar Jung as the Nizam of Hyderabad.
            - He rewarded Dupleix by appointing his honorary Governor of all the Mughal Dominions south of the River Krishna and other territories were ceded to the French.
                - In return, Dupleix placed at the disposal of Muzaffar Jung the service of his best officer named Bussy.
            - Soon afterwards, Muzzafar Jung was killed in an accidental skirmish.
            - However, Bussy maintained French influence in the Deccan by quickly placing his nominee Salabat Jung on the throne.
                - In this way, Bussy went on to control Deccan's political fortune for 7 years.
                - Thus the French power in the South was now at its heights from where it was going to fall badly.
        - Emergence of Robert Clive
            - Meanwhile, Muhammad Ali had taken refuge in the fortress of Trichinopoly and the repeated attempts of Chanda Sahib and the French to capture the fortress had failed.
            - The British, realising that the situation implied a serious threat to their position in Southern India, now decided to put their entire strength behind Muhammad Ali.
            - Robert Clive saves the day for the British, He suggested that French pressure on Muhammad Ali could be released by attacking Arcot, the capital of Carnatic.
            - Thus, attack upon Arcot was to be used as a diversionary tactic and thus Chanda Sahib would have to divert an effective part of his army for the protection of Arcot.
        - Siege of Arcot
            - Clive stormed Arcot with a small force of only 210 men and captured it, Thus Chanda Sahib alarmed by the attack on his capital, sent the best troops from Trichinopoly which comprised 4000 men to recapture Arcot.
            - Clive successfully sustained the famous siege for 53 days.
            - In the meantime, Clive received help in the form of reinforcements from Madras and some Indian allies (Mysore, Tanjore and Marathas).
            - Clive then came out of the besieged town, attacked and defeated Chanda Sahib (Beheaded), Muhammad Ali then became the undisputed Nawab of Carnatic.
            - The fate of French Governor Dupleix was sealed after the defeat of Arcot, The Directors of the French Company did not appreciate his political ambitions and disproved of the expenses they involved and recalled him.
            - On 1 August 1754, Godeheu succeeded Dupleix as French Governor General in India.
        - Treaty of Pondicherry
            - Godeheu signed a peace treaty, The Treaty of Pondicherry with the English in 1755.
            - As per this treaty, both the companies agreed not to interfere in the quarrels of the Indian princes.
            - After this treaty, French continued to maintain their hold over Hyderabad for some time, but they had lost much of their prestige and their predominant position in the Deccan had been definitely undermined.
            - On the other hand, English influence had been established in Carnatic, with their candidate Mohammad Ali installed as the Nawab of Carnatic.

            - Lally’s attack on Madras ended in failure as he could not get naval support in time.

        - Battle of Wandiwash (1760)
            - The battle still continued and Lally was eventually defeated decisively at the Battle of Wandiwash (Tamil Nadu - 22 January 1760)
            - The English army led by General Eyre Coot totally routed the French army.
            - Under Lally, Bussy was taken as prisoner.

            - The French capital of Pondicherry finally surrendered on 16 January 1761.
            - The fall of Pondicherry sounded the death knell of French dominions in India.
            - Shortly thereafter Jinje and Mahe on the Malabar Coast also surrendered to the English, leaving the French without even a toehold in India.
        - Peace of Paris (1763)
            - The Third Carnatic War ended officially in 1763 with the conclusion of the Peace of Paris, at the end of Seven Years’ War.
            - As per the treaty, Pondicherry and some other French settlements were no doubt returned to France, but these were never to be fortified again and could only function as mere trading centres.
            - The worst fate awaited Lally, He was detained as a prisoner of war for two years, after which he was allowed to return to his home country, where far from receiving any favourable treatment, he was first imprisoned and then executed.
            - Thus the Third Carnatic War brought down the curtain to the Anglo-French rivalry in India and cleared the way for the establishment of the British Empire.

    - Reasons for Defeat of the French Against the English
        - Continental Ambitions of France
            - France in the 18th century remained preoccupied with its continental ambitions, which considerably strained its resources.
            - The French Monarchs of the time were fighting for the ‘natural frontiers’ of their country, which meant acquisition of territory towards the Rhine and towards Italy.
            - Such an expansionist policy of France kept it perpetually trapped in the wars of Europe and diverted its energies away from colonial acquisition.
            - In the end, the French gained almost nothing on the continent and even lost their colonial possessions.
            - On the other hand, England did not covet an inch of European territory but followed its single minded objective of colonial expansion, an objective in which it achieved considerable success.
        - Difference in the Organisation of Two Companies
            - The French Company had been formed with a majority of capital contributed by the Monarchs with a guarantee of dividend to the shareholders, thus the directors of the company nominated by the king himself, took very little interest in the progress of the company.
            - Many times, when the company faced financial constraints, it was revived by the Government subsidy only.
            - Such a body was ill equipped to support Dupleix's ambitions and his costly wars.
            - The English Company, was an independent commercial corporation with a court of Directors who were greatly interested in the welfare of the Company.
            - The board maintained good financial health and was at one time seem as the milch cow of the British Government.
        - Different Systems of Government
            - The French Government was autocratic and suffered from corruption and inefficiency.
            - The English Government was run by an enlightened oligarchy, under which it was making immense progress towards a constitutional set up.
        - Other Reasons
            - Seats of Power
                - The French centered their power in Pondicherry, a single coastal enclave vulnerable to siege, while the English diversified their strongholds across Madras, Bombay, and Calcutta, giving them strategic resilience and broader influence over Indian rulers.
            - Naval Strength
                - British naval superiority, exemplified by control of the seas during the Seven Years’ War, allowed them to disrupt French supply lines and reinforce their own positions, whereas the French navy, often delayed or under-resourced, failed to support Lally effectively, as seen at Madras and Wandiwash.
            - Lack of Enthusiasm and Enterprise
                - French efforts in India lacked consistent enthusiasm from the home government and company directors, who prioritized European wars over colonial ventures, contrasting with the English East India Company’s proactive and profit-driven leadership, which eagerly backed military campaigns like Clive’s at Arcot.
            - Personal Incompetence of Commanders and Generals
                - French commanders like Lally, despite initial vigor, displayed arrogance and poor coordination—e.g., alienating subordinates and failing to secure Hyder Ali’s aid—while English leaders like Clive and Eyre Coote demonstrated adaptability, strategic foresight, and effective alliances with Indian powers.


- Rise of British Supremacy (2): Conquest of Bengal (1757-1765)
    - Overview of British Conquest
        - Introduction
            - The Beginning of British conquest in India may be traced back to the Battle of Plassey in 1757, in which the English forces defeated the Nawab of Bengal Siraj-ud-Daulah.
            - In fact, the history of British conquest in Bengal is the history of gradual transfer of power from Nawabs to British during the short period of eight years, from 1757 to 1764, the main cause of conflict being the commercial rivalry between the British and the Bengal Nawabs.
        - Why All Eyes on Bengal?
            - High agricultural production particularly rice.
            - Great Water Transport system for moving goods.
            - By mid 1700ʼs, Bengal was top producer of silk and cotton textiles in India.
            - Bengal was also a big supplier of saltpeters, needed for making gunpowder in Europe.
            - Therefore in the initial years of settlement, British, Dutch and French all had settlements in Bengal -
                - British in Calcutta
                - Dutch in Chinsurah
                - French in Chandernagore

        - Early European Presence
            - Since the 17th century, Bengal had emerged as a happy hunting ground of the Dutch, the English and the French company who were attracted to Bengal mainly owing to its rich resources.
            - In 1651, at Hugli The first English factory was set up upon receiving permissions from Sultan Shuja, the subahdar of Bengal.
            - Then he also granted the English East Indian Company the privilege of free trade in Bengal, Bihar and Orissa in return for lump sum payment of 350 pounds at a time when Companyʼs exports from Bengal were worth more than 50,000 pounds a year.             - In 1700, the Bengal factories were placed under Fort William.
            - Soon the villages grew into a city known as Calcutta.
        - Rise of Independent Nawabs
            - In 1717, Emperor Farrukhsiyar appointed Murshid Quli Khan, the Diwan of Bengal and also at the same time Subahdar of Bengal.
            - Taking advantage of his position, Murshid Quli Khan now declared himself as the Nawab of Bengal and became the first independent Nawab of Bengal. (F.S Farman of 1717)
            - In 1740, Alivardi Khan, the Deputy Governor of Bihar, deposed and killed Nawab Sarfaraz khan (Grandson of Murshid Quli).
            - Alivardi Khan rightly did not allow fortification of French and English factories at Chandernagore and Calcutta.
            - In 1755, Alivardi Khan dies nominating his grandson Siraj-ud-Daulah as his successor.

    - The Battle of Plassey (23 June 1757)
        - Battle Details
            - On 23 June 1757, both armies met at Plassey, 30 km south of Murshidabad.
            - The English force comprised of 950 European infantry and 2100 Indian sepoys, while that of the Nawab comprised of 50,000 men commanded by the treacherous Mir Jafar.
            - Only a small group of Nawabʼs soldiers fought bravely, they were led by Mir Madan and Mohan Lal.
            - Afer Mir Madan was killed, Mir Zafar played upon the fears of Nawab and advised him to retire from the battlefield, leaving the command in the hands of his generals.
            - The trick was well played, once the Nawab retired with 2000 men, a large part of Nawabʼs army led by Mir Jafar and Rai Durlabh merely looked upon, as Clive routed the rest of Nawabʼs forces.
            - As a result, the Nawab had to flee but was captured and put to death by an assassin, Muhammad Ali Beg.

            - It was a mere skirmish, resulting in 65 casualties on the English side and 500 on the Nawabʼs side.

                - Zafar was completely dependent on the English for maintenance of his position in Bengal.

                - The victory in Plassey brought about a great transformation in the position of the English Company in Bengal.
                - Until now the English Company was just one of the European companies trading in Bengal.
                - Afer Plassey, the Company emerged supreme and established its monopoly over Bengal trade by marginalising the French and the Dutch companies.

                - The victory in Plassey marked the beginning of British conquest of Bengal and later of India.
                - It helped the company and it servants to amass unimaginable wealth at the cost of the people of Bengal.

            - Mir Qasim secretly promised the necessary funds if the the English Company agreed to support his claim to the Nawabship.
            - Mir Zafar was ultimately forced to abdicate in favour of his son-in-law, Mir Qasim.
            - In this way, Mir Jafar sowed as he had repeat, got betrayal in return for betrayal.
            - Mir Qasim rewarded his benefactors by granting the Company the Zamindari of Burdwan, Mindapore and Chittagong.
            - He also promised to give the Company half of the share in chunam trade of Sylhet.
            - The first few months of Mir Qasimʼs reign went very well, but soon the relationship with the British embittered due to several reasons, But Mir Qasim belied English hopes.
            - He was able and efficient ruler and wanted to free himself from foreign control.
        - Changes Introduced by Mir Qasim
            - He moved the capital from Murshidabad to Monghyr in Bihar, away from the influence of the Company at Calcutta and court intrigues at Murshidabad.
            - Completely overhauled the bureaucracy by Men of his choice and sought to improve the finances of the state.
            - Sought to modernise his army along European line and preparations were made for the manufacture of guns at Monghyr
        - Conflict Between Mir Qasim and Company
            - The misuse of Companyʼs dastaks for private trade was perpetual cause of tension between the English Company and the Nawabs.
            - The Companyʼs servants were not paying any duty on their goods, whereas the local merchants had to pay duty with the following consequences -
                - The Nawab lost tax revenue
                - The local merchants faced unequal competition
                - Further, the Companyʼs servants even sold the dastaks to Indian merchants for a commission.
        - Frustration Increases Between Mir Qasim and Company
            - The Companyʼs servants were not even constant with duty free trade and used coercive methods to get things at cheaper rate.
            - They ignored the officials of the Nawab and forcibly took away the good and commodities of the peasants and merchants for a fourth part of their value.
            - They also used violence and oppression to force the peasants into buying goods at prices many times higher their value.
        - An Attempt at Settlement
            - In such conditions, though Mir Qasim did not seek independence from the British, he did seek to limit the fast expanding encroachments of the English on his jurisdiction, which were driving him to desperation.
            - Afer he appealed to British, A compromise was made that the Nawab would give the English traders a share in the inland trade provided they paid 9% duty.
            - It was also agreed that the Nawab alone would be competent to grant dastaks and his authority would be ultimate in the resolution of disputed regarding trade.
            - Unfortunately, the agreement was disproved by the Calcutta Council, as most of the Council members themselves participated in Inland trade and they rather favoured the deposition of Mir Qasim, which would provide them with yet another opportunity for receiving bribes.
        - A Masterstroke or Blunder by Mir Qasim
            - Out of desperation, Mir Qasim abolished all inland duties to bring the Indian merchants on the same footing as the English, provoking hostilities by an attack on Patna town.
            - In this way, war between the Nawab and the English broke out in 1763 and the Nawab was defeated in a series of battles that year.
            - Subsequently, he fled to Awadh and formed an alliance with Shuja-ud-Daulah (Nawab of Awadh) and Shah Alam II (fugitive Mughal Emperor) in a final bid to overthrow the English, which will ultimately lead to the Battle of Buxar (1764).

    - Battle of Buxar (22 October 1764)
        - Battle Details
            - The three allies closed with the Companyʼs army at the battlefield of Buxar.
            - Allies forces stood at nearly 40,000 to 60,000 whereas the English forces stood at nearly 7,000 commanded by Major Munro.
            - It was a close contested battle with heavy casualties on both sides and ended in the victory of the superior military power, the British.
            - This was one of the most decisive battles in Indian History.
            - It firmly established the British as masters of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa.
            - Awadh was placed at their mercy.
        - Consequences of Battle of Buxar
            - Buxar sealed the fate of Bengal Nawabs and transferred it into the hands of the English Company.
            - Mir Jafar was Brought back to the throne of Bengal, this time on much harsher terms.
                - Hand over the districts of Midnapore, Burdawan and Chittagong to the English
                - Permitted them duty free trade in Bengal (with exception of 2% duty on salt).
                - He and his successors had to pay Rs 5 Lakh per month to the English Company, allow Companyʼs intervention in matters of appointments and reduce military establishments.
            - Mir Jafar died shortly thereafer and his minor son, Nizam-ud-Daulh was appointed as Nawab.
            - Clive was appointed as Governor and Commander-in-Chief of Bengal (Clive second Governorship).
                - Primarily he took up the tasks of defining relations with the defeated powers and showed considerable practical wisdom in his settlements.
        - Settlement with Nawab of Awadh
            - Clive went to Awadh, met Shuja-ud-Daula at Allahabad and concluded with him the first Treaty of Allahabad.
            - As per the treaty,
                - Nawab of Awadh was to pay a war indemnity of Rs 50 Lakh to company.
                - Nawab was made to surrender Allahabad and Kora to emperor Shah Alam.
                - The Nawab was made to enter an offensive and defensive treaty with the Company.
            - In this way, Nawab of Awadh became dependent on the Company.
        - Settlement with Mughal Emperor, Shah Alam II
            - Clive concluded with the Second Treaty of Allahabad with the Mughal Emperor Shah Alam II, as per the treaty -
                - The Emperor was taken into Companyʼs protection and given the districts of Kora and Allahabad ceded by Awadh (The Emperor resided in the fort of Allahabad for 6 years as a virtual prisoner of the English).
                - In return, the Emperor issued a Farman dated 12 August 1765 granted to the Company the Diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa in return for the Company making an annual payment of Rs 26 Lakhs to him and providing for the expenses of the Nizamat of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa which was fixed at Rs 53 Lakhs. (In this way, the Farman legalised the Companyʼs gain in Bengal, it established a Dual system in Bengal in theory and converted the Emperor into useful ʻrubber stamp of the companyʼ)
            - Afer the death of Mir Zafar, Nizam-ud-Daulah was allowed to succeed his father on the following conditions -
                - The Nawab would surrender Defense and Foreign Affairs (both were Nizamat functions) into the hands of the Company and civil administration into the hands of a Naib-Subahdar nominated by the Company.
                - Going by this strategy, Company gained Diwani from the Emperor and Niamat functions form the Nawab of Bengal.
                - In this way, the infamous dual system was ʻdualʼ only in theory, while in practice the Company acquired all real power in Bengal.
                - The Nawab lost all independent military or financial power and become a mere figurehead.

    - Dual System of Administration in Bengal (Dyarchy or Double Governance System)
        - Structure and Functioning
            - Dual System meant the system of Governance where the Company exercised Diwani functions while the Nawab of Bengal exercised the Nizamat functions (defence, Law and order and administration of justice).
            - However, Nizam-ud-Daulah was allowed to succeed as Nawab of Bengal only on the condition that he surrendered the Nizamat functions.
            - Hence in reality, Dual System was just a sham as it was company that exercised all political power and merely used the Nawab and his administrate as an instrument for its purposes.
            - Thus, in practice, dual system referred to the system of Government whereby the Company acquired all real power while the ʻresponsibilityʼ for administration rested on the shoulders of the Nawab.
            - A fatal divorce of power form responsibility was inherent in this dual system.
        - Impacts of the Dual System
            - The advantage of this system was that British had power without responsibility.
            - This had adverse consequences for the people of Bengal, as neither the company nor the Nawab cared for their welfare.
            - In the words of Clive himself - ʻSuch a state of confusion, bribery, corruption and extortion was never seen or head of in any country but Bengalʼ
            - Thus began the Drain of Wealth from Bengal.
                - The Company stopped sending money from England to buy Indian goods.
                - These goods were now purchased form the revenue of Bengal and sold abroad.
            - This Drain of Wealth greatly improvised Bengal and was a major factor responsible for the terrible Bengal famine of 1770.
            - Though the famine was caused by failure of rains, the devastating effects were accentuated by policies of British.
            - Due to the Dual System of Government, The English Company acquired a real power without any responsibility while the position of Nawab was virtually reduced to a cipher.
            - Other Adverse impacts include -
                - Disruption of trade and Commerce - Company merchants exercised strict monopoly and would undersell the Indian merchants in Bengal, pushing many of them in destitution and beggary.
                - It ruined Indigenous Industry and skill - Company used its political power to compel the cotton weavers of Bengal to buy raw material from and sell woven cloths to the Company exclusively at arbitrary prices.
                - The Company also encouraged the industries selectively.
        - Significance of Battle of Buxar
            - Afer this battle, the English emerged as the supreme power.
            - Now the Nawab of Bengal was their puppet, the Nawab of Awadh a pliant ally and the Mughal Emperor their pensioner.
            - Lands from Bengal upto Allahabad lay at their feet and the roads to Delhi were open.
            - While the Plassey made them, a powerful factor in Bengal politics, Buxar made them a powerful factor in the politics of north India.
            - Battle of Plassey decided the fate of Bengal while Battle of Buxar decided the Fate of India.
                - In fact, the British now emerged as strong contenders for the Indian Empire.


- Rise of British Supremacy (3): Anglo Mysore Wars
    - Kingdom of Mysore
        - 
        - Political History of Mysore
            - Mysore was located south of Hyderabad at the junction of eastern and western Ghats.
            - Originally a viceroyalty under the Vijayanagar Empire in the 16th century.
                - With the decline of the Vijayanagara Empire, they declared themselves as Independent and were only a nominal part of the Mughal Empire.
            - In the early 18th century, Wadiyar/Wodeyar King Chikka Krishnaraja ruled Mysore.
            - Two of his ministers, Nanjaraja (Prime Minister) and Devaraja (Commander in Chief) seized power and reduced the king to a mere puppet.
            - The mid 18th century saw the emergence of Mysore as the second most significant power in south India, afer Hyderabad.
                - The foundation of Mysore power were laid by Hyder Ali, which was later consolidated by his able son, Tipu Sultan

    - Hyder Ali
        - 
        - Rise to Power
            - He began his career as a petty officer in the Mysore Army.
            - Though Uneducated, he was a man of great energy and intellect.
            - He gradually rose in the Mysore Army to be a brilliant commander.
            - In 1761, Hyder overthrew Nanjaraja and established authority over Mysore.
            - He established his control over rebellious poligars (Zamindars) and conquered Bindur, Malabar etc.
        - Military and Expansionist Ambitions
            - He was an expansionist at heart and naturally clashed with other regional powers - Maratha, Nizam and the English.
            - Faced Maratha raids in 1764, 1766, and again in 1771 as the Marathas recovered fast under Peshwa Madhav Rao.
            - He also inflicted a heavy defeat on the English near Madras in 1769.
            - His most remarkable achievement was his vision to modernise his army along the European lines and this way he had the best standing army among the regional powers of India.
                - He inducted French Experts and established a modern Arsenal at Dindigul.
                - Formed the first Indian controlled corps of sepoys armed with firelocks, bayonets, and backed by artillery served by Europeans
            - Hyder died during the course of the second Anglo Mysore War (1782) and was succeeded by his son, Tipu Sultan.

    - Tipu Sultan
        - 
        - Assertion of Autonomy
            - Afer assuming the throne, he strongly asserted his autonomy.
                - In 1787, he assumed the title of Padshah, issued coins in his own name with any reference to the Mughal Emperor.
                - He even issued a new Calendar.
                - He also sought a Sanad (Royal order) from the Ottoman Khalif to legitimize his rule.
            - Being a realist, he recognised Mughal authority when it suited him and defied it when it did not.
        - Administration and Welfare
            - For the General Public - he took great care for the welfare and happiness of his people.
            - More than any other 18th century Indian ruler, Tipu thoroughly recognised the threat that the English posed to South India and thus raised an efficient military force, employed French officers to train his troops and even raised a French corps, Made efforts to build modern Navy in 1796.
            - Economically - He was very sound and understood the importance of modern trade and Industry.
            - Hence promoted both Inland and Foreign Trade but imposed tight government regulations and control over it.
                - Set up a number of Factories in Mysore Territory such as those of sugar, silk fabrics etc.
                - Sent Emissaries to France, Turkey, Iran, Myanmar etc.
                - Tried to set up trading companies on the European Lines - which did not fructify,
                    - Pioneered iron cased rocket technology,
                - Worked for modernisation of Agriculture and introduced sericulture in Mysore.
            - Under his Administration, He acted like a despotic one - Himself being the civil. Political and military authority of the State.
            - There was no office of Wazir in his office but there were separate departments each under a different officer such as Military department, Finance department etc.
        - Personal Interests and Legacy
            - He was an avid learner and had a library with books on different subjects.
            - Tipu showed keen interest in the French Revolution and is believed to have planted the ʻTree of Libertyʼ at Seringapatam, became a member of the Jacobian Club and allowed himself to be addressed as Citizen Tipu.
                - His famous Line - “Better to live a day as a lion than a lifetime as a Sheep”.
            - He ruled Mysore till his death at the hands of the British in 1799 - where he died defending his capital during the course of Fourth Anglo Mysore War.

    - British Expansion in South India
        - Threats to British Interests
            - Hyder and Tipuʼs dominance over the rich trade of Malabar Coast was seen as the threat to the English Companyʼs Trade in pepper and cardamom.
                - In 1785, Tipu declared an embargo on export of pepper and cardamom through the ports within his kingdom.
                - In 1788, he clearly forbade dealings with English traders.
            - Thus Mysore was also seen as a threat to English control over Madras.
            - Moreover, The French Alliance with Mysore was seen as a threat to English dominance.
            - The British home government was in need of finances for growing expenditure in Napoleonic wars, and so it encouraged an aggressive expansionist policy of the Companyʼs merchants.

    - Anglo Mysore Wars (1767 – 1799)
        - 
        - Overview
            - The rise of Mysore under Hyder Ali was viewed as a threat by the neighbouring states - the Marathas - The Nawabs of Carnatic and The Nizam of Hyderabad.
            - The Marathas and the Nizam formed an alliance against Mysore and also co-operated with the English Company to curb Mysore.
            - But the Nizam was equally apprehensive of the Marathas and therefore cooperated with the English company against the Marathas also.
        - First Anglo Mysore War (1767 – 1769)
            - Hyder Aliʼs friendship with French alongside the Mysore border dispute with Nawab of Carnatic is considered as one of the main reasons for this war.
            - The immediate context of war was provided by the rivalry over control of Northern Circars.
            - This part was under the Nizam of Hyderabad and The English Company was seeking an overland connection between its territories in Madras and Bengal, and the Northern Circars was a series of coastal territories that lay in between.
            - The Nizam of Hyderabad initially rejected the British demand, however later signed a treaty with the Company in view of his poor financial condition.
            - As per this treaty, the Company got the Northern Circars and in return promised to give military support to the Nizam in his endeavours against Hyder Ali.
            - At the same time, Nizam had formed an alliance with the Marathas as both felt threatened by the expansionist attitude of Hyder Ali.
            - Hyder himself was annoyed with the English as they had set up a fortified outpost at Vellore.
            - In this way, The Marathas, The Nizam and the English entered into a tripartite treaty against Hyder Ali and attacked Mysore in 1766.
            - Hyder successfully persuaded the Nizam and the Marathas to join hands with him against the English.
            - He then launched an offensive attack against the Company and reached upto the gates of Madras, He forced the Madras Council to sign peace on his terms in 1769, known as the Treaty of Madras.
            - As per the treaty, both the powers agreed to help each other in case of an attack by a third party.
        - Second Anglo Mysore War (1780 – 1784)
            - The British had no intention of abiding by the Madras treaty.
            - When the Marathas invaded Hyderʼs territory in 1771, the English Company did not come to his help.
            - So this feeling of being betrayed laid the foundation of the Second Mysore War, On the other hand the French proved more helpful to Hyder with his military demands.
            - Further, The American War of Independence had broken out in 1775 and the French help to American colonists made Governor General Warren Hastings extremely anxious of Hyderʼs reaction with the French.
            - Under these circumstances, The Company attempted to capture Mahe (A French settlement on the Malabar Coast within Hyderʼs Jurisdiction), which was a direct challenge to Hyder Ali.
                - The English had also offended Hyder Ali when an English force trespassed on Hyderʼs territory in order to take over Guntur in Northern Circars.
            - The English capture of Mahe provided the immediate context for the second Mysore War, The English won over the Marathas and the Nizam (Bribery).
            - In July 1781, The English Army under Sir Eyre Coote defeated Hyder Ali at Porto-Novo.
            - Skirmishes continued for the time being.
            - But later Hyder Ali died of cancer in December 1782 during the course of the second Mysore War.
            - Tipu, son and successor continued the war.
            - However in 1783, The American War of Independence ended, and the French sailed back to Europe leaving Tipu to fight his own battles.
            - The Madras Government also desired peace due to lack of resources and uncertainty of the Maratha attitude.
            - Tipu also needed time to strengthen his own administration.
            - Thus the war ended with the Treaty of Mangalore (March 1784) signed on the basis of mutual restitution of each otherʼs territories.
            - This was the last British treaty with an Indian ruler on an equal footing.
        - Third Anglo Mysore War (1790 – 1792)
            - 
            - The above treaty was only a temporary respite before a final showdown.
            - Lord Cornwallis had become the Governor General in 1786 and through his diplomacy he brought the Marathas and The Nizam to the English side.
            - Convinced of the inevitability of the war, Tipu also sought help from the Turks and the French by sending embassies to Constantinople and France during 1784-1787.
            - However, Tipuʼs attack on Travancore became the immediate cause of the Third-Mysore War as the English were already itching for a war and decided to side with the ruler of Travancore.
            - The War started in 1790, supported by the Marathas and the Nizamʼs troops, Cornwallis himself headed a large army and approached Seringapatanam.
            - Tipu suffered a serious setback and showed initiative for peace.
            - Thus, the Treaty of Srirangapatnam was signed in 1792 under which -
                - Tipu had to surrender half of his territory to the Company and his allies.
            - While the Company acquired Baramahal, Dindigul and Malabar
            - The Marathas gained territory on the Tungabhadra side.
            - Nizam acquired territories form the Krishna to beyond the Pennar.
                - Tipu also had to pay war indemnity with Rs. 3 crore, but as he was unable to pay this sum indemnity, his two sons were kept as prisoners with Cornwallis.
            - Afer Cornwallis, Sir John Shore became the Governor General but he adopted the Policy of non-intervention towards Mysore.
            - For this reason, John Shore is known as the Father of policy of non-intervention.
        - Fourth Anglo Mysore War (1799)
            - Lord Wellesly sailed for India in 1797, this was a period when English were struggling against the French all over the world.
            - Napoleon Bonaparte had captured Egypt and Syria and he now turned towards India.
                - In 1801, he made an alliance with Czar Paul of Russia and drew up a serious plan to invade India via Herat and Kandahar.
            - The English realised that the consequences of a defeat at the hands of Napoleon would mean an end to its commerce that had brought immense financial and political gains.
            - In Napoleonʼs plan, Tipu Sultan of Mysore saw the right opportunity to oust the English from India.
            - He entered into negotiations with the French and started making elaborate preparations for war with the Company.
            - He planted a ʻFlag of Libertyʼ at Srirangapatna, styled himself ʻCitizen Tipuʼ.
            - He employed the French Commandant Monsieur Raymond and raised a corps of 14000 men with his help.
            - Wellesly alarmed by this, Tried to convert as many as Indian states into the subsidiary allies of the Company, either by persuasion or by force.
            - War began in February 1799 and was over by May, The English Company won a decisive victory at the Battle of Serinagapatnam.
            - Tipu was killed while defending the city before French help could reach him.
            - He died a heroes death defending the capital.
            - The Company, The Nizam and The Marathas annexed much of the remaining Mysorean territory.
            - The remaining core around Mysore and Serinagapatnam, was restored to the minor Indian prince, two year old Krishnaraj belonging to the Wadiyar Dynasty, whose forefathers had been the actual rulers before Hyder Ali became the defacto ruler.
            - A special Treaty of Subsidiary Alliance was imposed on the Wadiyar King and Mysore virtually became a dependance of the British.
            - Tipuʼs family was imprisoned in Vellore and his two sons pensioned.
            - Afer the success , Wellesley arrogantly pronounced “Now the Kingdom of the East is under our feet”


- Rise of British Supremacy (4) - Anglo Maratha Wars (1777 - 1818)
    - Overview
        - From the ashes of the Mughal Empire had emerged the Marathas and the English East India Company.
        - Both had emerged victorious in their own spheres - The Marathas among other Indian states, The English among other European contenders in India.
        - But now, Towards the last quarter of the 18th century, the two came face to face and indulged in direct clash and conflict.
        - The 1st, 2nd and 3rd Anglo Maratha Wars were fought between the army of the English East India Company, which afer the 1757 was de facto ruler of Bengal, and the Maratha Empire, in the Deccan.
        - Maratha Empire comprised of five major chiefs
            - The Peshwas of Poona
            - Bhonsle of Nagpur
            - Scindia at Gwalior
            - Holkar at Indore
            - Gaekwad at Baroda.
        - Peshwa was the nominal head of this Maratha confederacy and there existed bitter mutual rivalry among all of them, Particularly Scindia and Holkar.
        - Their strength had already been weakened by their defeat in the Third battle of Panipat (1761), in which the Marathas were defeated by the Ahmed Shah Abdali.
        - The wars started in 1777 and ended with British victory in 1818.
        - This lef the British in control, directly or indirectly via treaties of Princely states, of a vast proportion of India, making India the jewel in the crown of the British Empire.
    - First Anglo-Maratha War (1775 - 1782)
        - Background
            - It was a result of the excessive ambition of the English, accentuated by the internal strife of the Marathas.
            - The Bombay Government under the British had its longing eyes upon the Maratha territories, particularly the ports of Salsette and Bassein and hoped to gain political influence at the Poona Court.
            - The dispute over succession to the Peshwaship afer the death of fourth Peshwa Madhav Rao gave the English the much needed opportunity to intervene in the Maratha affairs.
            - Story - Afer Madhav Rao's death in 1772, his brother Narayan Rao succeeded him as the fifh Peshwa.
            - However, Narayan Raoʼs uncle Raghunath Rao got him assassinated and named himself as the next Peshwa, although he was not a legal heir.
                - Narayan Raoʼs widow Gangabai then gave birth to a son afer her Husbands death (Swaii) and he was legally the next Peshwa.
                - Then happened the Barbhai conspiracy under Nana Phadnavis which removed Raghunath Rao from the seat.
        - Treaty of Surat
            - Thus out of frustration, Raghunath Rao went over to the British and tried to capture power with their help.
            - This was the immediate background of the Anglo-Maratha War.
            - The war began with the Treaty of Surat and ended with the Treaty of Salbai.
            - In Raghunath Rao, the Bombay government saw a plaint tool and hoped to set up in Maharashtra the type of Dual Government as Clive had done in Bengal.
            - This treaty was signed between Raghunath Rao and the British Government at Bombay on 7 March 1775.
            - As per the treaty it was decided that the British would help Raghunath Rao in becoming the Peshwa by sending 2500 soldiers.
            - In return, Raghoba would cede territories of Salsette and Bassein and revenues from Surat and Bharauch to the British.
        - Treaty of Surat and Battle of Adas
            - In accordance with the Treaty of Surat, A British Army reached Surat led by Colonel Keating.
            - On 18 May 1775, at Adas (Battle of Adas) there took place a terrible battle between the English and the Maratha Armies.
            - In this battle, though the Marathas were defeated, however they retained control over Poona.
        - The Reversal with Treaty of Purandar
            - When a copy of Treaty of Surat reached the superior British Government in Bengal, it condemned the treaty as unjust and unauthorised.
            - Further, Warren Hastings sent Colonel Upton to Poona Darbar and signed the Treaty of Purandar.
            - Thus the Treaty of Purandar was signed between the British Government at Calcutta and Poona Court and it stated the following.
                - The Treaty of Surat was annulled.
                - The British lef the side of Raghunath and it was decided that Raghunath will be given a monthly pension of Rs 25,000 by Peshwa Government and he will reside in Gujarat.
        - Plot Twist - Treaty of Purandar Not Implemented
            - However, The Treaty of Purandar was not implemented due to a sudden turn of events.
                - The American War of Independence had erupted.
                - In 1775 and 1778, France joined on the American side against England.
            - At the same time, A French adventurer, Chevailer de St. Lubin, reached Poona.
            - This greatly alarmed Warren Hastings, who immediately scrapped the Treaty of Purandar, sent a large force to reinforce Bombay Government and sanctioned operations against the Marathas.
        - Movie Begins - First Anglo-Maratha War
            - Thus the Bombay Government resumed the war and sent an army towards Poona under Colonel Egerton (Later replaced by Colonel Cockburn).
            - The English and Maratha forces met on the outskirts of Poona.
            - The Maratha Army was led by the brilliant General Mahadji Schinde.
            - He lured the English Army into valleys of the Western Ghats near Talegaon (Battle of Talegaon) and trapped them from all side.
            - The Marathas also used the ʻScorched Earth Policyʼ, burning farmlands and poisoning wells.
        - Treaty of Wadgaon
            - The English were forced to retreat to villages named Wadgaon.
            - Finally by mid January 1779, The English surrendered and the Bombay Government was forced to sign a humiliating treaty with Poona Darbar called as Treaty of Wadgaon.
            - A per this treaty, it was decided that Bombay Government would return all territory conquered by it afer 1773 and the Scindias would get a part of revenue from Bharuch.
        - Humiliation Too Much for Hastings
            - Hastings refused to accept this humiliating treaty of Wadgaon.
                - He quickly sent an army from Bengal which soon captured Ahmedabad and Vasai.
                - But the British Army got defeated by Marathas in poona.
                - But meanwhile Gwalior was captured by British and eventually Scndia was seated in the Battle of Sipri.
            - Finally the Treaty of Salbai was signed between the British and the Poona Government.
        - Treaty of Salbai
            - This treaty between the British and The Poona Government was signed with the mediation by the Mahdji Scindia.
            - The treaty was signed on the basis of mutual restitution of each otherʼs territories or ʻstatus quo ante bellumʼ.
            - The main provision of this territory were -
                - Madhav Narayan Rao will be accepted as Peshwa.
                - British will give up the cause of Raghunath Rao and he be given an yearly pension of Rs 3.5 Lakhs.
                - The Company gave up Bassein and other territories captured since the Treaty of Purandar , but retained Salsette and The Elephanta Island.
                - Scinida got back the entire territory on the west of Yamuna.
        - Treaty of Salbai - First War Ends in Draw
            - In this way, the British attempt to gain supremacy in the Marahta state proved premature.
            - The First Anglo Maratha war was drawn.
            - Both sides had a taste of each otherʼs strength which ensured mutual respect and peace for the next 20 years.
            - It also gave British the required time to concentrate on other fronts especially Mysore.
    - Second Anglo-Maratha War (1803 - 1805)
        - Prelude to War
            - Lord Wellesly came to India in 1798 and aggressively followed his policy his subsidiary alliance.
            - The Marathas earlier refused all offers to accept subsidiary alliance, however their internal differences ultimately drove them into Wellesleyʼs trap.
            - Story -
                - Due to internal ongoing politics among the Marathasm Peshwa Baji Rao II murdered the brother of Jaswant Rao Holkar.
                - It led to Holkar forces defeating Sindhia and the Peshwa and installing an adopted brother on the throne of Pune.
                - The Peshwa of Poona sought assistance from Wellesley, resulting in Wellesley singing a treaty on December 31, 1802 known as the Treaty of Bassein.
                - This treaty, essentially a subsidiary alliance with the Peshwa, would ultimately mark the downfall of the Maratha Empire.l)
            - Imp Point - Subsidiary Alliance - Hyderabad (1798), Mysore (1799), Tanjore (1799), Awadh (1801), Peshwa (1802), Scindia (1803), Gaekwad (1803)
        - Treaty of Bassein (Subsidiary Alliance, 31 December 1802)
            - This treaty of ʻperpetual and general allianceʼ was signed between Peshwa Bajirao II and the English Company.
            - It was a treaty of the Subsidiary Alliance.
            - The main provisions of this treaty were -
                - Peshwa accepted the Companyʼs protection and had to pay a large annual subsidy.
                - Peshwa accepted that an English army and Resident will be stationed at Poona and no European enemy of the English will be kept in the Maratha Army.

        - Background
            - The third and final phase of the Anglo-Maratha war started with the arrival of British Governor Lord Hastings.

                - November 1817 - Treaty of Gwalior with Scinida

                - Peshwa was forced to breakup the Maratha confederacy

        - Why Marathas Lost?
            - Inept Leadership
                - (Maratha leaders like Baji Rao II lacked the strategic vision and unity of earlier figures like Shivaji, often prioritizing personal gain over collective resistance, weakening their response to British aggression.)
            - Defective Nature of Maratha State
                - (The Maratha Confederacy’s decentralized structure, with autonomous chiefs like Scindia and Holkar, fostered rivalry rather than cooperation, undermining a unified front against the British.)
            - Loose Political Setup
                - (The lack of a strong central authority after the Third Battle of Panipat left the Marathas politically fragmented, making it easier for the British to exploit divisions through diplomacy and treaties.)
            - Inferior Military System
                - (Despite early adoption of European tactics, the Marathas failed to modernize consistently, relying on outdated cavalry against the British’s disciplined infantry and artillery.)
            - Unstable Economic Policy
                - (Frequent internal conflicts and reliance on plunder rather than a stable revenue system drained Maratha resources, leaving them financially unable to sustain prolonged wars against the well-funded EIC.)
            - Superior English Diplomacy and Espionage
                - (The British, under leaders like Wellesley and Hastings, masterfully used treaties like Bassein and espionage to turn Maratha factions against each other, securing allies like the Nizam and isolating opponents.)
            - Progressive English Outlook
                - (The British leveraged industrial advancements and organizational efficiency, contrasting with the Marathas’ traditional feudal approach, giving them an edge in logistics, communication, and sustained warfare.)

            - In 1820, the treaty was renewed further to exclude Americans also from Sindh.

- Revolt of 1857
    - Political Causes
        - Discontent among Ruling Chiefs and Royal Families
            - Broken pledges and oaths resulted in loss of political prestige for some.
            - Policies such as ‘Subsidiary Alliance’ and 'Doctrine of Lapse’ caused alarm and suspicion in the minds of others.
        - Collapse of Rulers (Erstwhile Aristocracy)
            - Adversely affected sections of Indian society dependent on cultural and religious pursuits for sustenance.
        - Alien Rule of the British
            - Practice of ‘Absentee Sovereignty’ embittered Indians against the British.
            - Anti-British feelings particularly strong in regions like Burma, Assam, Coorg, Sind, and Punjab, unjustly annexed to the British Empire.
        - Abolition of Regal Titles
            - Mughal Emperor Bahadur Shah II had grown old and weak.
            - Lord Dalhousie recognized succession of Mughal Prince Faqir-Ud-Din but imposed strict conditions, opposing an imperium in imperio.
                - In 1856, Faqir-Ud-Din died, and Lord Canning declared the next prince in succession would renounce the regal title and ancestral Mughal palaces.
            - British abolished regal titles of Nawabs of Carnatic and Tanjore.

    - Economic Causes
        - Exploitative Land Revenue Settlements Introduced by British
            - Huge drain of wealth, destruction of industry, and increasing land revenue (Permanent, Ryotwari, and Mahalwari settlements) were common in the latter half of the eighteenth century.
            - Summary Settlement of Awadh
                - Proceeded to remove taluqdars wherever possible.
                - Pre-British times: Taluqdars held 67% of villages in Awadh; post-Summary Settlement, reduced to 38%.
                - Southern Awadh taluqdars hardest hit, some losing over half their villages.
                - British land revenue officers aimed to settle land with actual owners, reducing peasant exploitation while increasing state revenue.
            - East India Company used political power to fund British trade and commerce at the cost of Indians.
            - New Plantation System (1833)
                - Resulted in incalculable misery for Indian peasants.
                - Permitted Englishmen to acquire land plantations in India.
                - Hardest hit: Peasants on indigo plantations in Bengal and Bihar (Teen Kathiya System).
                - Zamindars (traditional landed aristocracy) often lost land rights, leading to loss of status in villages.
                - Dispossessed taluqdars seized opportunities during the sepoy revolt, especially in Awadh.
        - Destruction of Indian Trade, Handicraft, and Industry
            - High tariffs in Britain against Indian goods damaged Indian trade and manufacture.
            - Encouraged import of British goods to India.
                - Ruin of millions of artisans and craftsmen not accompanied by new industrial growth.
                - Annexation of Indian states cut off major sources of patronage.
        - Moneylenders and Traders as New Landlords
            - Peasants, compelled by new revenue settlements, resorted to loans from moneylenders/traders at usurious rates.
            - Moneylenders often evicted peasants for nonpayment of debts.
            - Ruination of Indian industry increased pressure on agriculture and land.

    - Social Causes
        - Arrogant Attitude of the British and Racial Discrimination
            - Racial overtones and superiority complex characterized British behavior.
            - Indiscrimination and assaults on Indians by Englishmen became common.
        - British Interference in Religion and Culture
            - Activities of Christian missionaries raised alarm among Hindus and Muslims (Charter Act of 1813).
                - Missionary-established educational institutions inculcated Western education and culture, replacing oriental learning.
            - Legislations
                - Prohibition of female infanticide, sati, and slavery.
                - Widow marriage legislation.
                - Religious Disabilities Act (1850) modified Hindu customs (e.g., a change of religion did not debar inheritance from a heathen father).

    - Military Causes
        - Low Salary and Poor Prospects of Promotion
            - Conditions of service in the Company’s Army conflicted with sepoys’ religious beliefs and prejudices.
                - Restrictions on wearing caste/sectarian marks and rumors of chaplains’ proselytizing (maintained at Company expense) seen as interference in religious affairs.
            - Crossing seas meant loss of caste for religious Hindus.
            - General Service Enlistment Act (1856) required Bengal Army recruits to serve anywhere needed, decreed by Lord Canning’s Government.
            - Vellore Mutiny (1806) was a precursor to 1857, reflecting such tensions.
            - Indian sepoys unhappy with emoluments compared to British counterparts.
            - No foreign service allowance (batta) when serving in Sindh or Punjab.
            - Racial discrimination in promotion and privileges; sepoys felt subordinate.
            - Sepoys as ‘peasants in uniform,’ consciousness tied to rural population.
            - Post Office Act (1854) withdrew sepoys’ free postage privilege.
            - Reports of bone dust in flour rations close to 1857.
        - Invincibility of British Rule Shattered
            - British losses in Afghan Wars (1838–1842), Punjab Wars (1845–1849), and Crimea War (1854–1856) emboldened sepoys.
            - Sepoys awaited a trigger (greased cartridge event) to express anger.
                - New rifle cartridges greased with beef and pig fat; administration did nothing to allay religious fears.
            - Enhanced growing disaffection with the government.
            - Long history of revolts: Bengal (1764), Vellore (1806), Barrackpore (1825), Afghan Wars (1838–1842).

    - Events Leading up to the Revolt
        - 19th Native Infantry at Berhampur
            - Refused Enfield rifle, mutinied in February 1857, disbanded in March 1857.
        - 34th Native Infantry
            - Mangal Pande fired at sergeant major at Barrackpore, overpowered, executed April 6; regiment disbanded in May.
        - 7th Awadh Regiment
            - Defied officers on May 3, met similar fate.
        - 3rd Native Cavalry
            - April 24: Ninety men refused greased cartridges.
            - May 9: Eighty-five dismissed, sentenced to 10 years’ imprisonment, fettered.
            - May 10: General mutiny at Meerut; released comrades, killed officers, unfurled revolt banner, set off for Delhi.

    - The Great Revolt
        - Began at Meerut (58 km from Delhi) on May 10, 1857; spread to Punjab (north), Narmada (south), Bihar (east), Rajputana (west).
        - Delhi
            - Local infantry joined, killed European officers (e.g., Simon Fraser), seized city.
            - Bahadur Shah Zafar proclaimed emperor; traditional symbol of India’s unity.
            - Indian chiefs proclaimed loyalty to Mughal emperor.
            - May 12: Rebelling sepoys overcame Lt. Willoughby, seized Delhi—a blow to British prestige.
            - Bahadur Shah urged chiefs/rulers to form a confederacy against British.
        - Spread of Revolt
            - Bengal Army rose; Awadh, Rohilkhand, Doab, Bundelkhand, central India, Bihar, East Punjab shook off British authority.
            - Civil population rebelled, especially in north-western provinces and Awadh.
            - Widespread participation: Peasantry, artisans, shopkeepers, laborers, zamindars, mendicants, priests, civil servants gave it popular character.
            - Peasants/petty zamindars attacked moneylenders/zamindars, destroyed debt records, targeted British law courts, revenue offices, police stations.
            - Limited spread: North of Narmada largely undisturbed.
            - Notable Hindu-Muslim unity during revolt.

    - Storm Centres and Leaders of the Revolt
        - 
        - Delhi: Bahadur Shah (nominal/symbolic), real command with General Bakht Khan (led Bareilly troops).
        - Kanpur: Nana Saheb (adopted son of Baji Rao II), expelled English, proclaimed Peshwa, acknowledged Bahadur Shah (Tantya Tope).
        - Lucknow: Begum Hazrat Mahal, revolt on June 4, 1857, popular support for deposed Nawab.
        - Jhansi: Doctrine of Lapse rejected claim of Damodar Rao (adopted son of Rani and Gangadhar Rao).
        - Bareilly: Khan Bahadur Khan.
        - Bihar: Kunwar Singh (zamindar of Jagdishpur), joined sepoys at Arrah, grudge against British for estate loss.
        - Faizabad: Maulvi Ahmadullah, fought stiff battle, acknowledged leader in Awadh (May 1857).
        - Singhbhum (Chotanagpur): Gonoo (tribal cultivator), led Kol tribals.
        - Chaurasee Des: Shah Mal mobilized cultivators, acted as Raja, set up justice hall and intelligence network; killed July 1857.

    - Suppression of Revolt
        - Delhi captured September 20, 1857 after bitter fighting.
            - Bahadur Shah taken prisoner; royal princes butchered publicly; exiled to Rangoon, died 1862.
            - Fall of Delhi extinguished Mughal house and revolt’s focal point.
        - Nana Saheb defeated at Kanpur, escaped to Nepal (1859).
            - Tantya Tope escaped, captured April 1859 after betrayal, executed.
        - Rani of Jhansi died on battlefield, June 1858.
        - By 1859: Kunwar Singh, Bakht Khan, Khan Bahadur Khan, Rao Sahib, Maulvi Ahmadullah dead; Begum of Awadh hid in Nepal.
        - British authority fully reestablished by end of 1859.
        - British Officers
            - Delhi and Lucknow: Sir Colin Campbell.
            - Jhansi: Sir Hugh Rose.
            - Banaras: Colonel Neil.

    - Reasons for Failure
        - Limited Territorial Spread
            - No all-India veneer; eastern, southern, western India largely unaffected.
            - Certain classes/groups (e.g., Scindia of Gwalior) worked against revolt.
        - Opposition by Big Zamindars
            - Acted as "breakwaters to storm"; Awadh tehsildars backed off with land restitution promises.
            - Moneylenders/merchants saw interests protected under British.
        - Support by Educated Indians
            - Viewed revolt as backward-looking, hoped British would modernize India.
            - Most Indian rulers refused to join, often aided British.
        - European Modern War Machinery
            - Indian soldiers poorly equipped (swords, spears, few guns) vs. British with Enfield rifles.
            - Electric telegraph kept British commanders informed.
        - Poor Organization
            - No coordination or central leadership.
            - Leaders (Nana Saheb, Tantya Tope, Kunwar Singh, Laxmibai) outmatched by British generals.
            - Rebels lacked clear understanding of colonial rule, forward-looking program, ideology, or societal alternative.
            - Diverse elements with differing grievances and political concepts.

    - Consequences
        - British Crown assumed direct administration; Company rule abolished.
            - Announced by Lord Canning at Allahabad durbar via Queen’s Proclamation, November 1, 1858.
        - Sowed seeds of Indian nationalism.

    - Post 1857 Scenario
        - Aftermath of Revolt of 1857
            - The Act for Better Government of India, 1858
                - Passed August 2, 1858, before revolt fully suppressed.
                - Exposed Company’s administrative limitations.
                - Provisions
                    - India governed by Crown via Secretary of State and 15-member council (advisory).
                    - Initiative/final decision with Secretary of State; dual system of Pitt’s India Act ended.
                    - Governor-General became Viceroy (increased prestige, not authority).
                    - Formal assumption of power by Crown; end of Company administration.
            - Queen’s Proclamation, 1858
                - Announced by Lord Canning at Allahabad durbar, November 1, 1858.
                - Governor-General titled ‘Viceroy’.
                - Provisions
                    - End of annexations/expansion; respect for native princes’ dignity/rights.
                    - Indian states to recognize British Crown’s paramountcy.
                    - Freedom of religion without British interference.
                    - Equal/impartial protection under law, equal opportunities in government services, respect for old Indian rights/customs in law-making.
            - Changes in the Army
                - Thorough reorganization; “division and counterpoise” policy.
                - Reduced Indian soldiers, increased European soldiers.
                - Recruits from ‘martial’ races (Punjab, Nepal, NW frontier) loyal during revolt.
                - Army separated from civilians.
                - Indian artillery units (except mountain batteries) defunct; higher posts reserved for Europeans.
                - Military Policy Changes
                    - European soldier ratio: Bengal (1:2), Bombay/Madras (1:3 or 2:5).
                    - Big positions/artillery reserved for Europeans.
                    - Awadh, Bihar, Central India soldiers deemed non-martial; Punjabis, Gorkhas, Pathans (loyalists) recruited heavily (half of army by 1875).
            - Changes in Approach
                - Reformist zeal faded; conservatives in Britain viewed Indians as beyond reform, ending reform era.
                - Empire became more autocratic, denying educated Indians’ power-sharing aspirations.
                - Long-term frustration for Empire.
                - Divide-and-rule policy intensified, using one class/community against another.
                - Systematic economic exploitation began without fear.
                - Government structure remodeled on ‘master race’ notion, widening ruler-ruled gulf.
            - Other Changes
                - Policy towards Upper Class (Princes and Landlords)
                    - Annexation policy abandoned.
                    - Princes/landlords rewarded and befriended.
                    - Britain as paramount power; Queen Victoria titled ‘Empress of India’ (1876).
                - Hostility towards Higher Education
                    - British became hostile, curtailed education post-Indian National Congress (1885).
                - Indian Civil Services Act of 1861
                    - Annual competitive exam in London for equality/transparency.
                    - Rules framed to keep civil services a British preserve.
                - Change in Attitude towards Educated Indians
                    - Indian Councils Act of 1861
                        - Sir Bartle Frere’s 1860 Minute pushed for Indian representation in Legislative Council due to ruler-ruled miscommunication.
                        - Marked start of active Indian representation.
                    - Indian Civil Services Act of 1861 (repeated for emphasis)
                        - Exam in London; rules preserved British dominance.
            - The Azamgarh Proclamation of 1857
                - Authored mostly by Firoz Shah (grandson of Bahadur Shah II).
                - Highlights
                    - Detailed complaints of social groups against unfair colonial authority.
                    - Proclaimed Mughals’ capacity to establish suzerainty over India.
                    - Advised rulers/chieftains to accept Delhi throne, defend people.
                    - Claimed both Hindus and Muslims ruined under English tyranny.
                    - Assured brighter future post-English defeat; urged acceptance of Badshahi Government.
            - British Portrayal of the Revolt
                - The Clemency of Canning
                    - Governor-General Canning’s leniency to win sepoy loyalty mocked in British press.
                    - Punch cartoon: Canning as father figure over bloodied sepoy, titled ‘The Clemency of Canning’.

            - Dominated by wealthy and aristocratic elements.
            - Local or regional in character.
            - Demands through long petitions to British Parliament:
                - Administrative reforms.
                - Association of Indians with the administration.
                - Spread of education.

            - Increasingly dominated by educated middle class (lawyers, journalists, doctors, teachers, etc.).

    - Political Associations in Bengal
        - Raja Ram Mohan Roy
            - Morning Star of Reform Movement; first great leader of modern India.
            - Pioneer of political movement in India.
            - Well-read, influenced by Western ideas; first to focus Englishmen’s attention on Indian grievances.
        - Bangabhasha Prakasika Sabha (1836)
            - Formed by associates of Raja Rammohan Roy.
            - Aim: Discuss government policy, seek redressal through petitions and memorandums.
        - Landholders Society/Zamindari Association (1838)
            - Founded by Dwarka Nath Tagore, RadhaKanta Deb, and others.
            - Purpose: Safeguard interests of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa.
            - Credited with launching organized political activity in India using constitutional methods for grievance redressal.
            - Marked beginning of organized political activity and constitutional agitation.
        - Bengal British India Society (1843)
            - Objectives:
                - Collection and dissemination of information on the actual condition of British India’s people.
                - Employ peaceful, lawful means to secure welfare, extend just rights, and advance interests of all classes.
        - British Indian Association (1851)
            - Merger of Landholders’ Society and Bengal British India Society.

                - Establishment of a separate, popular legislature.

                - Reduction in salaries of higher officers.

        - Bombay Presidency Association (1885)

    - Political Associations in England

- Consolidation of British Empire in India
    - Evolution of the East India Company (EIC)
        - Transition from Trading to Political Power
            - Initially established as a trading enterprise.
            - Gradually expanded control over India through conquests.
            - Evolved into a formidable political force.
        - Shift from Commercial to Territorial Entity
            - Transformed from primarily commercial to predominantly territorial in nature.
        - Issues with Company Administration
            - Misuse of Power
                - Widespread abuse of authority by company officials.
                - Led to growing concerns in Britain about governance in Indian territories.
            - Financial Crisis
                - Continuous wars and administrative mismanagement caused severe financial distress.
            - Revenue Maximisation
                - British Government sought to maximize revenue from EIC territories.
        - British Parliament's Intervention
            - Concerns about Company’s administration pressured Parliament.
            - Introduced constitutional laws to regulate and control Company operations.

    - Let’s Understand Company Structure - Important Learning from the Past
        - 
        - Dual Government of Bengal
            - 
            - Failed dual government in Bengal, coupled with abuse of power and corruption, prompted British Parliament to regulate EIC activities.
            - Abolished by Warren Hastings in 1772.
        - Additional Fact
            - Bengal Famine of 1770 (Chhiattorer Monnontor)
                - Estimated 10 million deaths.
                - Primary cause: Company’s mismanagement and harsh cash-based land tax collection.

    - Regulating Act of 1773
        - Background
            - Company had acquired political power and revenue-yielding territories, becoming an empire.
            - British Parliament realized Company could no longer remain outside state control.
            - Subjected Company to constitutional laws and Acts.
        - Regulating Act of 1773 (Factors Leading to the Act)
            - Dual Government of Bengal
                - Led to rampant corruption and a fatal divorce of power from law and order.
                - Abuses of private trade peaked (e.g., Clive returned to England at 34 with wealth yielding £40,000/year).
                - Wealth attracted British Parliament’s interest, suggesting Crown takeover.
                - 1767 intervention demanded £400,000 annually from Bengal’s plunder.
            - Jealousy in Britain
                - Company officials dubbed ‘Nabobs’ (corrupt Nawab analogy), symbolizing exploitation.
            - Military Setbacks
                - Defeat against Hyder Ali of Mysore in 1769.
            - Financial Crisis in EIC
                - Continuous wars and expansion pushed Company to bankruptcy by 1770.
                - Committee formed; Lord North presented bill on May 18, 1773, becoming Regulating Act.
            - Loan
                - Separate Act granted Company £1,40,000 at 4% interest.
        - Regulating Act of 1773 (Main Provisions)
            - Main Aim
                - Regulate Company activities in India and England.
            - Control by British Government
                - Allowed Company to retain territories but regulated its activities.
            - Restructuring of Company
                - 
                - Direct Parliamentary control over Court of Directors (CoD) for the first time.
                    - Raised qualifications for Court of Proprietors (CoP) and CoD (no re-election, no private trade).
                    - CoD required to report all civil, military, and revenue matters of Bengal to British Government.
                - Provided liberal salaries, prohibited private trade, bribes, and money lending.
            - In India
                - Established empowered central authority to address lack of supreme authority.
                - New Bengal Presidency to check corruption.
                    - Governor of Bengal designated ‘Governor General of Bengal’ with 4-member Executive Council.
                        - Decisions by majority, binding on GG; GG had ordinary and casting vote, no veto.
                        - Warren Hastings: First Governor General.
                - Established Supreme Court of Justice at Calcutta.
                    - Composed of Chief Justice (Sir Elijah Impey) and three puisne judges.
        - Regulating Act of 1773 (Limitations)
            - Intentionally vague in provisions.
            - Neither gave state definite control over Company nor Company definite control over servants.
            - Broke down under its own defects when implemented.

    - Amending Act (Act of Settlement 1781)
        - Objective
            - Resolve conflicts over Supreme Court jurisdiction left vague in 1773 Act.
        - Key Provisions
            - Jurisdiction Exemptions
                - Governor-General (GG) and Council, plus Company servants, exempted from Supreme Court jurisdiction for official actions.
            - Exclusion from Revenue Matters
                - Supreme Court excluded from revenue matters or collection issues.
            - Calcutta Jurisdiction
                - Supreme Court authority over all Calcutta residents.
                - Hindus and Muslims tried under respective personal laws.
            - Regulatory Authority
                - GG and Council empowered to establish rules for Provincial Courts and Councils.
                - First effort to delineate executive and judiciary functions in India.

    - Pitt’s India Act of 1784
        - Background
            - Introduced by William Pitt to address Regulating Act weaknesses.
            - Aimed to bring Company administration under British Parliament’s supreme control.
            - Fox-North Coalition’s similar bill defeated, causing ministry resignation (first British ministry wrecked on Indian issue).
        - Pitt’s India Act of 1784 (Main Provisions)
            - Increased Control of British Government
                - Areas controlled by Company officially termed “British Possessions in India.”
            - Governance Structure
                - Court of Directors managed commercial affairs.
                - Board of Control (6 commissioners) appointed for political affairs.
                - Introduced Double Government: Company ruled, Board controlled.
                - Civil and military government subordinated to British Government until 1858.
            - Anti-Corruption Measures
                - Mandated EIC officials to declare assets; introduced stringent corruption penalties (framework lasted until 1858).
        - Changes with Respect to Company’s Governance in India
            - Governor General and Council
                - Council reduced to 3 members (including Commander-in-Chief).
                - Madras and Bombay presidencies subordinated to Bengal; Calcutta became capital.
                - GG and Council subordinated to British Government; war declaration required Directors’ sanction.
            - Non-Intervention Principle
                - Introduced to end further wars and conquests in Indian princely states.
        - Significance
            - Increased British Control
                - Board of Control shifted power from EIC to British Government.
            - Ambiguity in Governance
                - Confusion in power division between Board of Control and Court of Directors.
            - Allegation of Favouritism and Anti-Corruption Measures
                - Addressed through asset declarations and penalties.

    - Act of 1786
        - Background
            - Pitt introduced bill to convince Cornwallis to accept Governor-Generalship.
            - Cornwallis sought combined GG and Commander-in-Chief powers.
        - Provisions
            - Merged offices of GG and Commander-in-Chief in one person.
            - Empowered GG to override Council majority on war and peace matters.

    - Charter Act of 1793
        - Background
            - Concerns about:
                - Power concentration in GG’s hands.
                - Need to regulate Company’s financial and commercial activities.
                - Codify laws for fairness and transparency in governance.
        - Provisions
            - Extension of Company’s Charter
                - Extended trade monopoly and political control for 20 years.
            - Increased Power of Governor General
                - GG could bypass Council decisions.
            - Revenue Collection and Economic Reforms
                - Company empowered to issue trade licenses to individuals and employees.
                - Separated revenue collection and legal duties, ending Maal Adalats (revenue courts).
            - Codification of Laws
                - Laid foundation for governance by written laws (started in Bengal).
                - Laws to be printed and translated into Indian languages for public familiarity with rights.

    - Charter Act of 1813
        - Background
            - Britain’s Industrial Revolution sought markets and raw materials.
            - Public and parliamentary opposition to EIC trade monopoly demanded free trade and laissez-faire policies.
            - Company’s expanded territories made dual commercial-political role nearly impossible.
            - Napoleonic Wars shifted British trade focus to India.
            - Christian missionaries pushed for access against Company restrictions.
        - Charter Act of 1813 (Main Provisions)
            - Retention of Territories and Revenue
                - Renewed Company’s Charter for 20 years; allowed territory possession and revenue collection.
                - Declared Crown sovereignty over Company territories.
            - Ending Company’s Monopoly
                - Trade monopoly broken, except for tea, opium, and China trade.
            - Expanded Jurisdiction of Indian Courts
                - Granted greater jurisdiction over British and European subjects.
            - Christian Missionaries Allowed
                - Permitted missionaries to settle in India under license (facilitated Bishop appointment in Calcutta).
            - Funding for Education
                - Allocated Rs. 1 Lakh yearly for literature and sciences among Indian subjects.
            - Civil Services Reforms
                - Proposed open competition for civil service; Indians eligible but opposed and unimplemented.
        - Charter Act of 1813 (Assessment of the Act)
            - Economic Liberalisation
                - Encouraged competition and growth by allowing Indian and European trade.
            - Social and Religious Changes
                - Facilitated Christianity spread and social reforms.
            - Educational Advancement
                - Funded Indian education, recognizing its importance.

    - Charter Act of 1833
        - Background
            - Also known as Saint Helena Act (transferred Saint Helena island to Crown).
            - EIC trade monopoly seen as harmful to British administration and Indians.
            - Industrial Revolution drove demand for export markets.
            - Whigs (Liberals) in power (1830); Macaulay and James Mill influenced Act.
        - Charter Act of 1833 (Main Provisions)
            - Legal British Colony
                - India made a British colony; Government of India (GOI) established over all British territories.
                - Removed restrictions on Europeans/British acquiring property in India.
            - Office of GG - Executive Powers
                - GG of Bengal became GG of India (William Bentinck, first GG); Council called Indian Council.
                - Controlled civil, military, and revenue matters.
                - Financial centralization under GG’s Council.
                - For the first time, Government officially termed Government of India.
            - Office of GG - Legislative Powers
                - GG in Council gained exclusive legislative powers:
                    - Repeal, alter, or amend laws for all persons in British India territory.
                    - Deprived Bombay/Madras Governors of legislative powers (restored in 1861).
                    - From now on, the rules made by company are called ‘Acts’ (earlier called regulations).
                - Act provided for appointment of 4th Council member (no voting rights).
                    - His addition to the council was to help in making laws, thus his work was fully legislative. Macauly was the first one to be appointed.
                - Court of Directors could veto GG’s laws.
                - GG’s decision prevailed in Council disputes.
            - Abolition of Commercial Privileges
                - Ended Company’s trade privileges; ordered to wind up commercial business.
                - Role shifted from that of purely commercial body to purely administrative.
                - Charter extended 20 years for non-trade functions (e.g., raising armies, ruling territories).
            - Minister for Indian Affairs
                - Replaced President of Board of Control.
            - Formation of Law Commission
                - Appointed in 1834 under Lord Macaulay to codify laws.
                - Resulted in Indian Penal Code and Civil/Criminal Procedure Codes.
            - Civil Service Reform
                - First Act to allow Indian natives in administration.
                - Court of Directors to nominate via competitive exams; ‘fitness’ as eligibility criterion.
                - No discrimination based on religion, birth, descent, or color.
                - System not effectively implemented soon after.
            - Slavery Abolition
                - GG directed to work towards abolition (achieved via Indian Slavery Act of 1843).
            - New Presidency
                - Proposed Agra and Fort William presidency from Bengal division (never implemented).
        - Charter Act of 1833 (Significance)
            - Culmination of centralization process begun with Regulating Act.
            - Paved way for British India unification and strong central government.
            - Transfer of powers from Madras/Bombay to GG restrained government’s ability to resolve public matters.

    - Charter Act of 1853
        - Background, Significant Challenges
            - High costs and delays from Board of Control and Court of Directors raised EIC efficiency concerns.
            - Politically informed Indians (e.g., Bombay Association, Madras Native Association) demanded decentralization and greater governance participation.
        - Charter Act of 1853 (Main Provisions)
            - Indian Territories
                - Held ‘in trust for the Crown’ until Parliament directed otherwise.
                - Company ceased as commercial agency, remained political agent for Crown.
            - Legislative Provisions
                - Separated GG Council’s legislative and executive functions.
                - Added 6 legislative councillors, forming Indian Legislative Council (ICS):
                    - Chief Justice and puisne judge of Calcutta Supreme Court.
                    - Four representatives (Bengal, Madras, Bombay, NW Provinces; civil servants with 10+ years).
                - Powers of Legislative Councillors:
                    - Ask questions, discuss Executive Council policy, highlight government lapses.
                    - Bills referred to Select Committees; business conducted publicly.
                    - GG assent required for laws; GG could veto bills.
            - Executive Provisions
                - Law member became full member of GG Executive Council (strength back to 4).
            - Directors and Their Powers
                - Reduced from 24 to 18 (6 Crown-appointed).
                - Divested of patronage; appointments opened to competitive exams (Macaulay Committee).
                - Empowered to:
                    - Constitute new Presidency.
                    - Alter state boundaries.
                    - Incorporate newly acquired states.
                - Created Lt. Governorship of Punjab (1859) due to administrative difficulties.
            - Governor of Bengal
                - Separate post created.
            - Open Competition System
                - Established for civil servant selection; opened covenant civil service to Indians.
        - Assessment of the Act
            - Marked beginning of Parliamentary system in India:
                - Verbal discussions in legislative capacity (previously written).
                - Bills passed through stages, referred to select committees.
                - Legislative business conducted publicly; proceedings officially published.
            

- Indian National Movement (1885 - 1907)
    - Events Leading to Formation of INC
        - By 1885, need for a pan-Indian political organization evident, recognized by nationalists since 1870s through various initiatives:
            - Laws Causing Discontent
                - Vernacular Press Act (1878) and Arms Act (1878) met with widespread opposition.
            - Ilbert Bill Controversy
                - Sparked political awareness and agitation.
            - Early Political Groupings
                - Indian National Association established by Surendranath Banerjee in Kolkata.
            - Theosophical Society Meeting (1884)
                - Annual meet in Madras; delegates from across India recognized need for a mediator to unite regional leaders.
                - Chose A.O. Hume as mediator due to his wide acceptance and liberal views.
            - Formation of Indian National Union (May 1885)
                - Hume secured Viceroy’s approval to create “Indian National Union.”
                - Affiliated with government; platform to voice Indian public opinion.
                - Led to inception of Indian National Congress (INC).

    - The Indian National Congress
        - First Session
            - Held at Gokuldas Tejpal Sanskrit College, Bombay, December 25–31, 1885.
            - Governor-General: Lord Dufferin.
            - Hume: General Secretary (1885–1906).
            - Originally named ‘Indian National Union’; planned for Poona but shifted to Bombay due to cholera outbreak.
        - Views of Hume and Lord Ripon
            - Educated middle class in India was permanent; its legitimate aspirations should be met, grievances addressed.
        - Controversies Surrounding Inception (Mains Oriented)
            - Safety Valve Theory
            - Lightning Conductor Theory
        - First Session Details
            - Attended by 72 delegates; presided by Womesh Chandra Bonnerjee.
                - Bonnerjee: Barrister, first Indian to contest British House of Commons election (lost).
            - 9 Resolutions Passed (General Idea, No Need to Memorize):
                - Abolish Indian Council of Secretary of State for India.
                - Expand and reform Imperial and Local Legislative Councils (Indian Councils Act of 1861).
                - Establish Legislative Councils for Punjab, etc.
                - Implement simultaneous Public Service Exams in England and India; raise candidate minimum age.
        - Early Composition of INC
            - Educated middle class had largest representation.
            - Legal profession most heavily represented.
            - Brahmins prominent among castes.
            - Bombay, Calcutta, and Madras presidencies led participation.
            - Masses and landed class largely absent.

    - Early Nationalists (1885 - 1905) - The Moderates
        - Moderate Phase (1885–1905)
            - Known as period of Tea-Party Politics or Political Mendicancy.
            - Grew from 72 delegates (Bombay, 1885) to 434 (Calcutta, 1886).
                - Delegates elected from local bodies nationwide; decided to meet annually in different regions.
            - Called Moderates for trusting British justice, protesting within legal bounds.
        - Method of Work of the Moderates
            - Peaceful, constitutional agitations within law.
            - Relied on prayers, petitions, meetings, speeches, resolutions (via press and Congress sessions).
            - Sent deputations to Britain:
                - Dadabhai Naoroji became British MP, worked for Indian interests.
                - 1889: British Committee of INC established.
                - 1890: Weekly journal ‘India’ started to educate British public on Indian issues.
                - 1892: Planned INC session in London postponed due to British objections, never resumed.
        - Political Demands
            - Achievements via Indian Councils Act of 1892:
                - Expanded Legislative Councils with more powers and Indian representation.
                - Introduced budget discussions.
            - Other Demands:
                - Form Provincial Councils.
                - Abolish Indian Council.
                - Increase Indian opportunities in Civil Service; simultaneous ICS exams in London and India.
                - End aggressive foreign policy against Indian neighbors.
        - Economic Demands
            - End economic drain.
            - Enquire into economic and industrial conditions.
            - Reduce land revenue.
            - Abolish salt tax.
            - Encourage modern industry via direct government aid.
        - Agriculture-Related Demands
            - Develop agricultural banks to curb moneylender atrocities.
            - Extend irrigation facilities to prevent famines.
            - Introduce permanent settlement nationwide.
        - Military Demands
            - Repeal Arms Act.
            - Appoint Indians to commissioned army ranks.
            - Reduce military expenditure.
        - Social Demands
            - Increase welfare expenditure.
            - Secure civil rights: freedom of speech, thought, association, press.
            - Improve plantation laborers’ conditions.
        - British Reaction to Congress
            - Initially flexible; post-1887, attitude stiffened.
            - Branded nationalists as ‘Disloyal Babus,’ ‘Seditious Brahmins,’ ‘Violent Villains.’
            - Lord Dufferin: Congress represented a ‘Microscopic Minority.’
            - Encouraged pro-British elements (e.g., Sir Sayed Ahmed Khan, Raja Shiv Prasad of Banaras).
            - Formed Aligarh Movement and United India Patriotic Association to counter Congress.
            - Advanced ‘Divide and Rule’ policy to widen Hindu-Muslim divide.
            - 1890s: Government employees forbidden from attending Congress meetings.
        - Achievements of the Moderates
            - Political Achievements
                - Indian Councils Act of 1892.
                - ICS Examination in India (1893): House of Commons resolution for simultaneous exams.
                - Demand for Swaraj:
                    - 1905 Benaras Session: First demand for self-rule within British Empire (GK Gokhale).
                    - 1906 Calcutta Session: Explicit demand by Dadabhai Naoroji.
                - Birth of democratic ideas.
                - Trained Indians in political work; laid foundation for Extremists.
            - Economic Achievements
                - Economic Critique of British Rule:
                    - Dadabhai Naoroji, RC Dutt, DE Wacha advanced ‘Drain of Wealth’ theory.
                    - Naoroji’s ‘Poverty and Un-British Rule in India’: Per capita income Rs 20 (1867-68).
                    - RC Dutt’s ‘Economic History in India’ detailed exploitation.
                - Welby Commission (1895): Enquired into Indian expenditure.
            - Constitutional Agitation Within Law
                - Believed British wanted justice but were unaware of real conditions.
                - Two-pronged methodology:
                    - Create public opinion, arouse consciousness, unite people on political questions.
                    - Persuade British Government and public for reforms.
                - Used ‘prayer and petition’; resorted to constitutional agitation if unsuccessful.

    - Extremists
        - Rise of the Extremists (Phase of Militant Nationalism or Radical Politics)
            - Note: ‘Militant’ and ‘Terrorists’ had different meanings then; described extremist approaches vs. Moderates. Revolutionaries were a militant extremist faction.
        - Growth of Militant Nationalism
            - Radical trend emerged in 1890s; took shape by 1905 (Bengal Partition).
        - Recognition of the True Nature of British Rule
            - Economic Miseries (1890s):
                - Severe famines killed 90 lakh (1896–1900).
                - Bubonic plague hit Deccan; large-scale riots followed.
            - Government curtailed rights:
                - Tilak’s imprisonment (sedition), Official Secrets Act, Indian Universities Act.
        - Growth of Confidence and Self-Respect
            - Faith in self-effort grew; Tilak, Aurobindo, Bipin Chandra Pal urged reliance on Indian character and capacities.
            - Belief that masses could make sacrifices to win freedom.
        - International Influences
            - Defeat of European powers:
                - Italians by Ethiopians (1896).
                - British reverses in Boer Wars (1899–1902).
                - Japan’s victory over Russia (1905).
            - Inspiration from nationalist movements in Ireland, Russia, Egypt, Turkey, Persia, China.
        - Growth of Education
            - Spread of education increased awareness.
            - Unemployment/underemployment among educated highlighted poverty and underdevelopment.
        - Reaction to Increasing Westernisation
            - Intellectuals (Swami Vivekananda, Bankim Chandra Chatterjee, Dayananda Saraswati) inspired nationalists.
            - Countered Western superiority myth with India’s rich past; Dayananda’s ‘India for Indians.’
        - Dissatisfaction with Achievements of Moderates
            - Young Congress members dissatisfied with Moderates’ 15–20-year record.
            - Criticized ‘Three P’s’ (prayer, petition, protest) as ‘political mendicancy.’
            - Indian Councils Act of 1892 disappointing.
            - Repressive Measures:
                - 1897: Natu brothers deported without trial (linked to ‘Poona Vaibhav’ editorials).
                - 1897: Tilak and editors imprisoned for anti-government rhetoric.
                - 1898: ‘Feeling of disaffection’ made an offence.
                - 1899: Reduced Indian members in Calcutta Corporation.
                - 1904: Indian Official Secrets Act restricted press freedom.
            - Tilak’s Views:
                - Called Congress ‘a Congress of flatterers,’ sessions ‘holiday recreation.’
                - Founded New English School (1880, later Fergusson College).
                - Opposed armed struggle; advocated Swaraj via self-help, determination, sacrifice.
                - Slogan: ‘Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it.’
                - First Congress leader with multiple imprisonments for outspokenness.
        - Reactionary Policies of Curzon
            - Curzon’s seven-year rule (missions, commissions, omissions) provoked reaction.
            - Refused to recognize India as a nation.
            - Measures: Official Secrets Act, Indian Universities Act, Calcutta Corporation Act, Bengal Partition.
        - Existence of a Militant School of Thought
            - Early 20th-century nationalist thinkers advocated militancy:
                - Bengal: Raj Narain Bose, Ashwini Kumar Datta, Aurobindo Ghosh, Bipin Chandra Pal.
                - Maharashtra: Vishnu Shastri Chiplunkar, Bal Gangadhar Tilak.
                - Punjab: Lala Lajpat Rai.
            - Tilak as leading representative.
            - Tenets:
                - Hatred for foreign rule.
                - Swaraj as national goal.
                - Direct political action.
                - Belief in masses’ capacity to challenge authority.
                - Readiness for personal sacrifices.
        - Emergence of a Trained Leadership
            - New leadership channeled masses’ political struggle potential.
            - Energy released during Swadeshi agitation against Bengal Partition.
            - Believed Swaraj required direct action, self-reliance, sacrifice, strong will.
            - Faith in masses; aimed to make Congress and national movement mass-based.
            - Inspired by India’s past but focused on building the future.
        - Mode of Action - The Extremists’ Ideology
            - Inspired by Swami Vivekananda, Dayananda Saraswati, Bankim Chandra Chatterjee.
            - Programmes:
                - Boycott and Swadeshi.
                - National Education:
                    - National Scheme of Education for students boycotting government institutions.
                    - Bengal Council of National Education set up by Gurudas Banerjee.
                - Non-Cooperation or Passive Resistance.
                - Co-operative Organizations.
        - Work of Extremists
            - Tilak’s Actions:
                - 1891: Opposed Age of Consent Bill (raised age from 10 to 12); argued against British interference in private life.
                - Organized Ganpati and Shivaji Festivals.
                - Published newspapers: Mahratta (English), Kesari (Marathi).
                - 1895: Ousted Gokhale and Ranade from Poona Sarvajanik Sabha, giving Extremists control.
                    - Gokhale formed Deccan Sabha, completing Moderate-Extremist split in Maharashtra.
            - Aurobindo’s Contributions:
                - 1893–94: ‘New Lamps for the Old’ articles in Indu Prakash; criticized Congress as out of touch with ‘proletariat,’ ‘dying of consumption.’
            - Extremist Groups:
                - Maharashtra: Led by Bal Gangadhar Tilak.
                - Bengal: Led by Bipin Chandra Pal, Aurobindo Ghosh.
                - Punjab: Led by Lala Lajpat Rai (Lal-Bal-Pal trio).
        - Difference Between Moderates and Extremists
            - Understanding of Swaraj.
            - Attitude towards British benevolence.
            - Methodology: 3 P’s (Moderates) vs. 3 S’s (Extremists).

    - Some Important Moderates
        - Surendranath Banerjee
            - Known as the Indian Burke.
            - Opposed Bengal Partition.
            - Founded Indian Association (1876) for political reforms.
            - Convened Indian National Conference (1883), merged with INC in 1886.
        - G. Subramanya Aiyar
            - Preached nationalism via Madras Mahajana Sabha.
            - Founded The Hindu and Swadesamitran.
        - Dadabhai Naoroji
            - Grand Old Man of India; India’s unofficial ambassador in England.
            - First Indian British MP.
            - Wrote ‘Poverty and Un-British Rule in India,’ detailing Drain Theory:
                - Wealth drained via salaries, savings, pensions, British troop payments, company profits.
        - Dayananda Saraswati
            - First to call for Swaraj (1876: ‘India for Indians’); some call him ‘Grandfather of Indian Nation.’
        - Bankim Chandra Chatterjee (1838–1894)
            - Wrote ‘Vande Mataram’ in Anandmath (1882, Sanyasi Rebellion backdrop) to counter British influence.
            - First sung by Rabindranath Tagore at 1896 Congress Session.
            - First 2 verses adopted as National Song in October 1937 by Congress.
            

- Indian National Struggle (1900 - 1911)
    - Key Events
        - Partition of Bengal
        - Swadeshi Movement

    - Lord Curzon
        - Curzonian Attack - Governor-General and Viceroy of India (1899–1905)
            - Believed to know more about India and its problems than any prior Viceroy.
            - Arrived amid 1896–97 famine and plague; aimed to reform every department.
            - Method: Appoint expert commissions, then enact legislation.
        - Reforms and Policies
            - Police Reforms
                - 1902 Police Commission under Sir Andrew Frazer.
                - Found police corrupt, oppressive, lacking public trust.
                - Recommendations: Increase salaries, establish training schools, provincial police service, Central Department of Criminal Intelligence.
            - Education Reforms
                - 1902 Universities Commission to address discipline and declining standards.
                - Curzon viewed universities as factories of political revolutionaries.
                - Indian Universities Act of 1904: Increased control over universities, strict affiliation rules for private colleges.
            - Army Reforms
                - Led by Lord Kitchener, Commander-in-Chief.
                - Indian Army split into Northern and Southern Commands.
                - Battalions subjected to rigorous “Kitchener Test.”
            - Foreign Policy
                - Mission to Tibet (Colonel Younghusband) to counter Russian influence.
                - Mission to Persian Gulf (Sir Henry McMahon).
            - Other Works
                - Famine Commission under Sir Anthony MacDonnell.
                - Irrigation Commission under Sir Colin Scott Moncrieff.
                - Co-Operative Credit Societies Act, 1904: Cheap loans for cultivators.
                - Ancient Monuments Act, 1904: Protected historical monuments.
                - Partition of Bengal announced (1905).
                - PUSA Agricultural Institute established (1903).

    - Partition of Bengal
        - 
        - Background
            - British claimed Bengal (78 million people, a quarter of British India’s population) was too large for efficient administration.
            - Originally included Bengal, Bihar, Orissa, Assam.
                - 1874: Assam separated as Chief Commissioner’s Province.
            - Decision publicized in December 1903.
            - Plan: Divide into two provinces:
                - Bengal (Western Bengal, Bihar, Orissa).
                - Eastern Bengal and Assam.
            - Motives:
                - Linguistic division to reduce Bengalis to a minority in Bengal.
                - Religious division: Western half Hindu-majority, Eastern half Muslim-majority.
            - Note: Nationalists supported separating linguistically divergent Orissa and Bihar for administrative efficiency.
        - Implementation
            - Scheme began with Curzon’s Minute on Terrestrial Redistribution, June 1, 1903.
            - July 20, 1905: Curzon ordered division:
                - Eastern Bengal and Assam: 31 million.
                - Rest of Bengal: 54 million (18 million Bengalis, 36 million Biharis/Oriyas).
        - Anti-Partition Campaign
            - Leadership: Surendranath Banerjee, K.K. Mitra, Prithwishchandra Ray.
            - Methods: Petitions, public meetings, memoranda, propaganda via newspapers (Hitabadi, Sanjibani, Bengalee).
            - Objective: Pressure government through educated public opinion in India and England to prevent partition.
            - July 1905: Government ignored opposition, announced partition.
            - August 7, 1905: Boycott Resolution passed at Calcutta Town Hall meeting; launched Swadeshi Movement targeting Manchester cloth and Liverpool salt.
            - October 16, 1905: Partition effective; observed as mourning day in Bengal.
                - Vande Mataram became movement’s anthem.
                - Rabindranath Tagore composed ‘Amar Sonar Bangla’ (later Bangladesh’s anthem, 1971).
                - Tagore called for Raksha Bandhan (Rakhi) as unity symbol.
                - Ramendra Sunder Trivedi urged Aradhana (unlit hearths) as mourning sign.
            - Leaders Surendranath Banerjee and Ananda Mohan Bose addressed large gatherings.
            - Movement spread beyond Bengal:
                - Poona/Bombay: Tilak.
                - Punjab: Lala Lajpat Rai, Ajit Singh.
                - Delhi: Syed Haider Raza.
                - Madras: Chidambaram Pillai.
            - INC 1905 Session (Gokhale’s presidency):
                - Condemned partition and Curzon’s reactionary policies.
                - Supported anti-partition and Swadeshi Movement.
            - Militant Nationalists (Tilak, Lajpat Rai, Bipin Chandra Pal, Aurobindo Ghosh):
                - Wanted movement to extend nationwide, evolve into mass struggle for Swaraj.
            - Moderates: Limited scope to Bengal, opposed broader escalation.

    - Swadeshi Movement
        - INC Calcutta Session (1906, Dadabhai Naoroji’s presidency)
            - Declared goal: ‘Self-government or Swaraj like the UK or colonies.’
            - Four resolutions: Swaraj, Swadeshi, Boycott, National Education.
        - Moderate-Extremist Dispute
            - Deadlock at Surat Session (1907, Rash Behari Ghosh): Congress split.
        - Extremist Expression
            - Periodicals:
                - New India (Bipin Chandra Pal).
                - Vande Mataram (Aurobindo Ghosh).
                - Sandhya (Brahmabandhav Upadhyaya).
                - Yugantar (Bhupendranath Dutt).
        - New Forms of Struggle
            - Boycott of Foreign Goods
                - Public burning of foreign cloth, boycotts of foreign salt/sugar.
                - Priests refused to solemnize marriages with foreign goods; washermen refused to wash foreign clothes.
                - First suggested by Krishna Kumar Mitra in Sanjibani.
            - Public Meetings and Processions
                - Key methods for mass mobilization and popular expression.
            - Corps of Volunteers (Samitis)
                - Swadesh Bandhab Samiti (Ashwini Kumar Dutta, Barisal) highly effective.
            - Imaginative Use of Festivals and Melas
                - Spread political messages via traditional events.
                - Tilak’s Ganapati and Shivaji festivals in western India and Bengal.
                - Bengal’s folk theatre used for propaganda.
            - Emphasis on Self-Reliance (Atma Shakti)
                - Swadeshi Enterprises:
                    - Textile mills, soap/match factories, tanneries, banks, insurance companies, shops.
                    - Reasserted national dignity, honor, confidence; regenerated villages economically/socially.
                    - Acharya P.C. Ray founded Bengal Chemicals Factory.
            - Programme of National Education
                - Bengal National College (inspired by Tagore’s Shantiniketan) with Aurobindo Ghosh as principal.
                - National schools/colleges emerged nationwide.
                - National Council of Education (August 15, 1906): Organized literary, scientific, technical education on national lines, using vernaculars.
                - Bengal Institute of Technology for technical education; funds raised to send students to Japan.
                - Charly’s Circular: Bengal Chief Secretary threatened student activists with disciplinary action.
        - Workers
            - October 1905: Printer’s Union formed after strikes in government presses; first real labor union.
        - Peasants
            - Minimal peasant mobilization; participation negligible.
            - 1906: Southern Andhra/Tamil Nadu held Vandemataram Movement gatherings supporting Bengal.
        - Muslims
            - Failed to gain Muslim support; a key weakness.
            - 1906: All India Muslim League formed under Nawab Salimullah of Dacca with government patronage to advance Muslim interests.
            - Eastern Bengal saw communal riots at movement’s peak.

    - The Surat Split
        - Benaras Session (December 1905, Gokhale’s presidency)
            - Moderate-Extremist differences emerged.
            - Extremists: Extend Boycott/Swadeshi beyond Bengal, include all associations (government service, courts, councils) for a nationwide mass movement.
            - Moderates: Limit movement to Bengal, opposed boycotting councils; favored constitutional methods.
        - Calcutta Session (December 1906)
            - Moderate enthusiasm waned due to Extremist/revolutionary popularity.
            - Dadabhai Naoroji elected president; Swaraj defined as self-government like UK/colonies.
            - Extremists called for widespread passive resistance, boycotts of schools, colleges, councils, courts, municipalities.
        - Moderates’ Shift
            - Encouraged by rumored council reforms, toned down Calcutta programme.
            - Misjudged reforms as rewards rather than a tactic to isolate Extremists.
        - Surat Session (1907)
            - Extremists: Wanted session in Nagpur with Tilak or Lajpat Rai as president, reaffirming Swadeshi, boycott, education resolutions.
            - Moderates: Chose Surat to exclude Tilak; elected Rashbehari Ghosh, sought to drop resolutions.
        - Government Strategy
            - Three-pronged approach: Repression, Conciliation, Suppression.
                - Stage 1: Mild repression of Extremists to intimidate Moderates.
                - Stage 2: Placate Moderates with concessions, hint at further reforms if Extremists distanced, isolating Extremists.
        - Post-Swadeshi
            - Stage 3: With Moderates aligned, government suppressed Extremists fully, then ignored Moderates.
            - 1907–1911 Laws to Check Anti-Government Activity:
                - Seditious Meetings Act, 1907.
                - Indian Newspapers (Incitement to Offences) Act, 1908.
                - Criminal Law Amendment Act, 1908.
                - Indian Press Act, 1910.
            - Extremist Leaders’ Fate:
                - Tilak: Jailed in Mandalay (Burma) for 6 years.
                - Aurobindo and B.C. Pal: Retired from active politics.
                - Lajpat Rai: Left for abroad.
            

- First World War and Nationalist Response in India
    - Home Rule League Movement
        - Overview of the Home Rule League Movement
            - The Home Rule Movement emerged as India’s response to the First World War, reflecting a growing demand for self-governance.
            - It was inspired by the Irish Home Rule Leagues and marked the rise of a more aggressive approach to Indian politics.
        - First World War (1914–1918)
            - When the First World War began, the Moderates chose to support British war efforts, seeing it as both a duty and a strategic move to negotiate concessions later.
            - The Extremists disagreed with this approach, but Bal Gangadhar Tilak took a unique stance. He pledged loyalty to the British Crown and encouraged Indians to assist Britain during its time of need, hoping this support would yield political gains.
        - Opinion of Industrialists
            - Wealthy Indian industrialists viewed the war as a chance to profit. The conflict disrupted British industrial production, creating shortages that increased demand for goods from Indian industries.
            - This economic boost fueled the growth of Indian industries, and industrialists wanted to maintain this momentum after the war. They were eager to organize and back nationalist movements to protect their interests.
        - Reasons for the Home Rule League Movement (HRLM - Why?)
            - Some nationalists believed that only popular pressure could force the British government to grant political concessions.
            - The Moderates grew disillusioned with the limited reforms of the Morley-Minto Reforms (1909), which failed to meet their expectations.
            - Ordinary people were suffering from wartime hardships, including high taxes and rising prices, making them ready to join aggressive protests.
            - The war exposed the hypocrisy of imperialist powers. As major nations fought each other and spread propaganda, the myth of white superiority weakened, inspiring Indians to challenge British rule.
            - Tilak, released from prison in June 1914, was prepared to lead. He reassured the government of his loyalty and signaled to the Moderates that he sought administrative reform, not revolution, similar to the Irish Home Rule model.
            - Annie Besant, an Irish theosophist living in India since 1896 and president of the Theosophical Society, decided to expand her work by launching a movement for Home Rule, modeled on the Irish example. She later became INC president in 1917.
            - Background on Theosophical Society: Founded in 1875 by H.P. Blavatsky and Colonel Olcott in New York, it established its first headquarters in Adyar, near Madras, in 1882.
        - Objectives of the Movement (What?)
            - The Home Rule League campaign aimed to educate the common person about home rule, presenting it as self-government within the British Empire.
        - Methods of the Movement (How?)
            - The movement sought to achieve its goals through widespread political education and engagement. This included holding public meetings, setting up libraries and reading rooms with books on national politics, organizing conferences, conducting classes for students on political issues, and spreading propaganda through newspapers, pamphlets, posters, illustrated postcards, plays, and religious songs. 
            - It also involved collecting funds, organizing social work, and participating in local government activities.
        - Composition of the Leagues
            - Both Tilak and Besant understood that success required the support of the Moderate-dominated Indian National Congress (INC) and full cooperation from the Extremists.
            - After failing to bridge the Moderate-Extremist divide at the 1914 INC session, Tilak and Besant chose to launch their own political initiatives.
            - By early 1915, Annie Besant began campaigning for post-war self-government, inspired by white colonies like Canada and Australia. She used her newspapers, *New India* and *Commonweal*, and organized public meetings and conferences.
            - At the 1915 INC session, Tilak and Besant’s efforts gained traction. The Congress agreed to admit Extremists, committed to educative propaganda, and revived local-level Congress committees.
        - Tilak’s League
            - Established in April 1916 during the Bombay Provincial Conference in Belgaum, with G.S. Khaparde as a founding member.
            - Operated in Maharashtra (excluding Bombay city), Karnataka, Central Provinces, and Berar, with six branches.
            - Demands included Swarajya (self-rule), formation of linguistic states, and education in vernacular languages.
            - Tilak famously declared, “Swaraj is my birthright and I shall have it,” during this agitation.
            - In his speeches, Tilak avoided religious appeals, opposed untouchability, and urged Brahmins to tolerate non-Brahmins. He stated, “If God were to tolerate untouchability, I would not recognize him as God at all.”
            - The British government issued Tilak a show-cause notice, but the High Court cleared him, giving the Home Rule Movement legal legitimacy. By 1917, Tilak’s league had 14,000 registered members.
        - Besant’s League
            - Launched in September 1916 in Madras, covering the rest of India, including Bombay city.
            - Had 200 branches, loosely organized compared to Tilak’s League. George Arundale served as organizing secretary, and Sir S. Subramaniam Aiyer (Grand Old Man of South India) was honorary president.
            - Earlier in 1916, impatient followers like Jamnadas Dwarkadas, Shankerlal Banker, and Indulala Yagnik set up Home Rule groups, started the Bombay newspaper *Young India*, and created an All India Propaganda Fund for pamphlets.
            - Key figures like Motilal Nehru, Jawaharlal Nehru, Bhulabhai Desai, Chittaranjan Das, Madan Mohan Malaviya, Mohammad Ali Jinnah, Tej Bahadur Sapru, and Lala Lajpat Rai later joined, some leading local branches. Joseph “Kaka” Baptista was another prominent Bombay activist.
        - Government Response
            - The government responded with harsh repression, especially in Madras, where students were banned from political meetings.
            - In June 1917, Annie Besant, B.P. Wadia, and George Arundale were arrested. In protest, Sir S. Subramaniya Aiyar renounced his knighthood, and Tilak called for passive resistance.
            - Annie Besant was released in September 1917.
        - Shortcomings and Fade-Out
            - The movement lacked effective organization.
            - Communal riots in 1917–18 weakened its momentum.
            - Moderates were pacified by Montagu’s 1917 statement promising reforms and Besant’s release.
            - Extremists’ talk of passive resistance from September 1918 distanced Moderates.
            - The Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms, announced in July 1918, further divided nationalists.
            - Leadership weakened: Tilak went abroad in September 1918 for a legal case, while Besant wavered on reforms and passive resistance strategies.
        - Contributions
            - By amplifying the Moderates’ political education program, the movement gained support from many Moderates. Members of Gokhale’s Servants of India Society, though barred from joining, actively promoted Home Rule nationwide.
            - It shifted focus from the educated elite to the masses, altering the nationalist movement’s trajectory away from the Moderate path.
            - Established organizational links between urban and rural areas.
            - Inspired a generation of passionate nationalists.
            - Prepared the masses for Gandhian-style politics.
            - Tilak and Besant’s efforts led to a Moderate-Extremist reunion at the 1916 Lucknow Session, revitalizing the Congress as a tool of Indian nationalism.
            - Post-1916 Congress session, a joint meeting of both leagues addressed over 1,000 delegates under one canopy.

    - Lucknow Session of the Indian National Congress, 1916
        - Readmission of the Extremists to Congress
            - Presided over by Moderate Ambika Charan Majumdar, the session welcomed back Extremists led by Tilak.
            - Facilitated by the deaths of staunch Moderate opponents Gopal Krishna Gokhale and Pherozshah Mehta in 1915.
            - Tilak’s League organized a “Congress Special” or “Home Rule Special” train to transport Western India delegates to Lucknow, starting a new Congress tradition.
            - Tilak’s proposal for a Congress Working Committee (to handle daily operations and implement resolutions) was rejected.
        - Lucknow Pact Between Congress and Muslim League
            - Opposed by Madan Mohan Malaviya.
            - The Muslim League agreed to joint constitutional demands with Congress, accepting separate electorates until any community requested joint ones.
            - Muslims received a fixed proportion of seats in all-India and provincial legislatures.
        - Joint Demands
            - Expand representative assemblies at central and provincial levels with elected majorities and greater powers.
            - Set a 5-year term for the Legislative Council.
            - Ensure half the members of the Viceroy’s and provincial governors’ executive councils were Indian.
        - Montagu’s Statement (August 1917)
            - Government policy aimed at “increasing participation of Indians in every branch of administration and gradual development of institutions with a view to the progressive realization of responsible government.”

    - Nationalist Resurgence After the War
        - Post-War Economic Hardships
            - Industry
                - Rising prices followed by a recession, coupled with increased foreign investment, pushed many Indian industries toward closure. They demanded protection from imports and government aid.
            - Workers and Artisans
                - Faced unemployment and struggled with high prices.
            - Peasantry
                - Burdened by high taxes and poverty, peasants awaited leadership to protest.
            - Soldiers
                - Returning from foreign battlefields, they shared experiences with rural communities, shocked by India’s poverty and reduced liberties.
            - Educated Urban Classes
                - Suffered unemployment and were acutely aware of British racism.
        - Expectation of Political Gains for War Cooperation
            - India’s contribution to the British war effort was immense, with nationalists offering support and countless troops sacrificing their lives.
        - Nationalist Disillusionment with Imperialism Worldwide
            - Allied powers promised democracy and self-determination to colonial allies during the war, but post-war actions betrayed these pledges.
            - Wartime propaganda exposed colonial misdeeds, yet the Paris Peace Conference and treaties showed imperialist powers intent on retaining and redistributing colonies, not freeing them.
            

- Gandhiji Before 1915
    - Introductory Note
        - Message by Kawal Sir
            - From an exam perspective, detailed knowledge of Gandhi’s life before 1915 isn’t critical. However, understanding his South African experiences is key to grasping his philosophy and methods, which shaped his role in India’s independence struggle. Learning history this way turns it into a compelling story.
            - Feel free to skip this section if time is short!

    - Gandhi Before India
        - Early Life
            - Born on October 2, 1869, in Porbandar, Kathiawar, Gujarat, into a well-to-do Vaishya family deeply influenced by Jainism, where ahimsa (non-violence) is a core principle.
            - In 1881, at age 12, he went to England to study law. He returned after 10 years as a young barrister and began practicing at the Bombay High Court.
            - Struggling to build a successful practice in Bombay, he moved to Rajkot, earning a living by drafting petitions.
        - Move to South Africa
            - In 1893, at age 24, Gandhi traveled to Durban in Natal Province, South Africa, for a one-year contract related to a legal case for his client, Dada Abdullah. He was the first Indian barrister to arrive there.
            - He faced racism and humiliation, notably when, despite holding a first-class ticket, he was thrown off a train to Pretoria after a white passenger objected to sharing the compartment with an Indian.
            - Intended as a brief stay, he remained in South Africa for 20 years, dedicating himself to organizing Indian workers to fight for their rights.

    - Conditions of Indians in South Africa
        - Indians faced severe racial discrimination, denied citizenship rights, freedom to trade, and property ownership.
        - They were barred from voting, restricted to specific residential areas, and prohibited from using footpaths or leaving home at night without permits.
        - First- and second-class railway compartments were off-limits to them, and all Indians were derogatorily labeled “coolies,” meaning laborers.

    - First Phase: Moderate Phase of Struggle (1894–1906)
        - Struggle Against Disenfranchisement Bill
            - Gandhi opposed the Disenfranchisement Bill, relying on petitions and memorials sent to South African and British authorities. He believed that informing them of the Indians’ plight would prompt action, as Indians were British subjects.
            - He founded the Natal Indian Congress to unite diverse Indian groups and launched the newspaper *Indian Opinion* to amplify their voice.
            - This organization led non-violent protests against the oppressive treatment of Indians and native Africans by white authorities.
        - Early Actions
            - In 1896, Gandhi briefly visited India, recruiting 800 Indians to join him in South Africa. On their arrival, an angry mob attacked them, injuring Gandhi. He exercised self-restraint, marking the start of his philosophy of winning over opponents with peaceful resistance. This approach paid off, as British pressure forced the abandonment of the disenfranchisement effort.
            - During the Boer War (1899), Gandhi organized the Natal Indian Ambulance Corps with 1,100 Indians to aid the British, despite ongoing discrimination against Indians.
        - Connection with Indian National Congress (INC)
            - In 1901, Gandhi attended the Calcutta INC session (presided by W.E. Wacha), his first contact with the organization. He successfully passed a resolution highlighting the plight of Indians in South Africa.
        - Leadership and Lifestyle
            - By 1903, Gandhi was the recognized leader of South Africa’s Indian community, living a disciplined life of self-restraint.
            - Inspired by John Ruskin’s *Unto This Last*, which praised rustic living, Gandhi established Phoenix Farm near Durban. Here, he trained cadres in non-violent Satyagraha—peaceful defiance of unjust laws, mass arrests, occasional hartals (economic shutdowns), and bold marches—fostering a fearless spirit against repression. He also published *Indian Opinion* from the farm.

    - Second Phase: Phase of Passive Resistance (1906–1914)
        - Introduction to Satyagraha
            - Starting in 1906, this phase marked Gandhi’s use of passive resistance or civil disobedience, which he named Satyagraha—devotion to truth through non-violent resistance.
        - Satyagraha Against Registration Certificates (1906)
            - The Asiatic Registration Bill required Indians to carry registration certificates with fingerprints, a discriminatory measure. Gandhi formed the Passive Resistance Association to defy this law, encouraging Indians to face penalties rather than comply.
            - He and others were jailed for refusing registration. When authorities tricked some into registering, Gandhi led a public burning of the certificates. A compromise eventually ended the standoff.
            - During this campaign, Gandhi abandoned his legal practice to focus fully on the struggle.
        - Campaign Against Restriction on Indian Migration
            - The movement expanded to oppose new laws restricting Indian migration between provinces. Indians defied these by crossing borders and refusing to show licenses, leading to many arrests.
        - Campaign Against Poll Tax
            - A £3 poll tax burdened ex-indentured Indians earning less than 10 shillings monthly. Gandhi’s campaign to abolish it broadened the movement’s support base.
        - Campaign Against Invalidation of Indian Marriages
            - A Supreme Court ruling invalidated non-Christian marriages, affecting Hindu, Muslim, and Parsi unions and deeming their children illegitimate. This insulted Indian women, drawing many into the movement.
        - Tolstoy Farm (1910)
            - As Satyagrahis courted arrests, funds to support their families dwindled. Gandhi established Tolstoy Farm, funded by Hermann Kallenbach (a German architect friend), Jamshedji Tata, the Nizam of Hyderabad, the INC, and the Muslim League, to house these families.

    - Gandhi Returns to India - Indian Relief Act of 1914
        - Through negotiations with Lord Hardinge, C.F. Andrews, and General Smuts, an agreement was reached. The South African government conceded key demands: abolishing the poll tax, easing registration certificate rules, recognizing Indian marriages, and promising sympathetic treatment of immigration issues. Gandhi returned to India in January 1915.

    - Gandhi in India (January 9, 1915)
        - Feeling his South African mission was complete, Gandhi planned to return to India. Gokhale, his political mentor, urged him to stop in London first. Arriving during the First World War, Gandhi organized an Ambulance Corps to aid the British, earning the Kaiser-e-Hind Gold Medal in 1915.
        - He arrived in India on January 9, 1915, at age 46. Gokhale wanted him to join the Servants of India Society, but opposition from some members prevented this.
        - Gandhi spent his first year traveling India, studying its issues, and establishing the Satyagraha Ashram in Ahmedabad (later moved to the Sabarmati River banks, becoming Sabarmati Ashram).
        - Annie Besant invited him to join her Home Rule League Movement, but he declined, not wanting to trouble the British during the war.
        - At the 1916 Lucknow INC session, Gandhi remained neutral between Moderates and Extremists, successfully passing a resolution to end indentured labor recruitment.

    - Early Experiments in India
        - Champaran Satyagraha (1917) - First Civil Disobedience Movement
            - Issue
                - European planters forced Champaran (Bihar) peasants to grow indigo on 3/20 of their land (tinkathia system). When German synthetic dyes replaced indigo, planters demanded high rents and illegal dues to maximize profits, forcing peasants to sell at fixed low prices.
            - Action
                - Invited by local farmer Rajkumar Shukla, Gandhi investigated despite initial ignorance of the issue (noted at the 1916 Lucknow session). Authorities ordered him to leave, but he defied them with passive resistance, a novel tactic then.
                - Permitted to inquire, Gandhi joined the Champaran Agrarian Committee, convincing authorities to abolish the tinkathia system and compensate peasants (25% of illegal dues, per the Champaran Agrarian Act of 1917).
            - Support
                - Leaders like Rajendra Prasad, Mazharul-Haq, Mahadeo Desai, Narhari Parekh, J.B. Kripalani, Brajkishore Prasad, Anugrah Narayan Sinha, Ramnavmi Prasad, and Shambhusharan Varma assisted.
        - Kheda Satyagraha (1918) - First Non-Cooperation Movement
            - Issue
                - A 1918 drought in Kheda, Gujarat, caused crop failure. The Revenue Code entitled farmers to tax remission if yields fell below one-fourth normal, but the government refused despite Gujarat Sabha petitions (Gandhi as president).
            - Action
                - Gandhi urged farmers to withhold taxes. Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, Narahari Parikh, Mohanlal Pandya, and Ravi Shankar Vyas organized villagers, maintaining discipline across communities.
                - When property was seized, buyers were ostracized. Over 2,000 peasants pledged non-payment, but a good Rabi crop and government concessions (tax only from those able to pay) led Gandhi to end the agitation, despite limited participation (70 of 559 villages).
            - Impact
                - Though a token victory, it expanded Gandhi’s rural Gujarat base.
        - Ahmedabad Mill Strike (1918) - First Hunger Strike
            - Issue
                - A dispute arose between Ahmedabad cotton mill owners and workers over ending a plague bonus. Wartime inflation doubled prices, prompting workers to demand a 50% wage hike; owners offered 25%. Workers struck, facing arbitrary dismissals.
            - Action
                - Anusuya Sarabhai, sister of mill owner Ambalal Sarabhai, sought Gandhi’s mediation. He advised a 35% demand and non-violent strike. When talks stalled, Gandhi fasted unto death—his first—bolstering workers and pressuring owners. A tribunal awarded a 35% hike, ending the strike.
        - Rowlatt Satyagraha (1919)
            - Background
                - In 1917, the Rowlatt Committee (chaired by Justice Sydney Rowlatt) investigated “revolutionary crime,” recommending repressive laws alongside the Montagu-Chelmsford report. The Rowlatt Act (March 21, 1919) continued wartime restrictions, allowed trials by special courts (no appeals, in-camera evidence), warrantless arrests, and detention up to two years.
            - Gandhi’s Response
                - On February 24, 1919, Gandhi formed the Satyagraha Sabha in Bombay to resist the Act, with members pledging defiance. He said, “We shall obtain salvation only through suffering, not by reforms from the English—they use brute force, we soul force.”
                - He called for a nationwide hartal on April 6, 1919 (initially March 30), involving work stoppage, fasting, and prayer, and sold banned books like *Hind Swaraj* as defiance.
                - Arrested on April 8 en route to Punjab, his detention sparked violence, including the Jallianwala Bagh Massacre (April 13, 1919) in Amritsar under General Dyer. Gandhi halted the Satyagraha on April 18, calling it a “Himalayan Blunder” due to unprepared participants.
            - Opposition
                - Liberals (Surendranath Banerjee, D.E. Wacha, T.B. Sapru, Srinivas Shastri), Annie Besant (no civil resistance warranted), and Tilak’s followers (N.C. Kelkar, G.S. Khaparde) opposed it, fearing it would derail constitutional reforms.
            - Support
                - Young Home Rule League members and Pan-Islamic leaders (Abdul Bari, Dr. M.A. Ansari) backed Gandhi, spurred by the Khilafat issue post-World War I.
            - Jallianwala Bagh
                - Punjab Governor Sir Michael O’Dwyer and General Dyer enforced martial law after arresting local leaders Saifuddin Kitchlew and Dr. Satyapal (April 9). Dyer’s troops fired on an unarmed crowd, killing hundreds. Rabindranath Tagore renounced his knighthood in protest on May 30, 1919.

    - Gandhian Ideology
        - Influences
            - In *My Experiments with Truth*, Gandhi credits his parents, family’s socio-religious milieu, the *Bhagavad Gita*, Jainism, and later, Tolstoy, Thoreau, and Ruskin.
        - Fundamental Principles
            - Satya (Truth), Ahimsa (Non-Violence), Asteya (Non-Stealing), Aparigraha (Non-Possession), Brahmacharya (Self-Control).
        - Tools
            - Swaraj (self-rule), Swadeshi (self-reliance), Sarvodaya (upliftment of all).
        - Swaraj
            - In *Hind Swaraj*, Gandhi saw modern Western civilization, not British rule, as the true enemy, criticizing railways for aiding plagues and famines via food exports. Though called obscurantist, this highlighted modernization’s rural toll.
            - Initially, Swaraj meant a traditional, uncorrupted Indian lifestyle. Later, it evolved to include village reconstruction, Khadi promotion, and Harijan welfare, emphasizing socio-economic renewal.
        - Swadeshi
            - Gandhi opposed Western influence but supported Indian industries, maintaining ties with industrialists like Ambalal Sarabhai and G.D. Birla. His Trusteeship theory viewed capitalists as workers’ stewards, rejecting class-based militancy.
        - Sarvodaya
            - Meaning “upliftment of all,” Sarvodaya aimed for the greatest good through selfless effort. Gandhi’s Ram Rajya envisioned a cooperative, stateless society sustained by voluntary effort, not violence.
            - Agreeing with Thoreau (“the best government governs least”), he saw the state’s role as regulating behavior, ideally replaced by self-sufficient, non-violent villages. Recognizing human flaws, he settled for a predominantly non-violent society where individuals, not the state, were paramount.
        - Religion
            - Gandhi believed religion should permeate all actions, not remain private. His universalist outlook rooted Indians in their culture while embracing others’ virtues, saying, “I want all cultures to blow about my house freely, but I refuse to be blown off my feet.”
        - Economic Decentralization
            - He advocated cottage industries where workers owned production means, enabling pursuit of higher values.
        - Varna System and Non-Possession
            - Gandhi saw varna as equal professions serving society as a duty, not for wealth accumulation.
        - Education
            - The 1937 Wardha Scheme (published in *Harijan*) promoted learning through activity for basic education.
        - Women
            - He opposed all gender discrimination, advocating perfect equality.
        - Untouchability
            - Gandhi called it a “crime against humanity,” fiercely denouncing it.
                        

- National Struggle (1918-1923)
    - Non-Cooperation and Khilafat Movement
        - Background on the Khilafat Issue
            - Muslims in India and worldwide regarded the Sultan of Turkey as their spiritual leader, the Khalifa. During World War I, Turkey aligned with the Axis powers, and Indian Muslims naturally sympathized with Turkey.
            - After the war, Britain took a harsh stance: Turkey was dismembered, and the Khalifa was removed from power, incensing Muslims globally.
        - Muslim Demands from the British
            - Retention of the Khalifa’s control over Muslim sacred places.
            - Ensuring the Khalifa retained sufficient territories post-war territorial arrangements.
        - Formation of the Khilafat Committee
            - In early 1919, the Ali brothers (Shaukat Ali and Muhammad Ali), Maulana Azad, Ajmal Khan, and Hasrat Mohani formed the Khilafat Committee to pressure Britain into changing its policy toward Turkey.
            - Initially, the committee relied on moderate tactics like meetings and petitions to support the Khalifa. Later, a militant faction emerged, pushing for active agitation, including non-cooperation with the British.
        - All India Khilafat Conference (November 22-23, 1919)
            - Held in Delhi, this conference saw militant Muslim leaders call for non-cooperation with the British government in India.
            - In April 1920, Shaukat Ali threatened a joint Hindu-Muslim non-cooperation movement led by Mahatma Gandhi if Muslim demands were unmet. Gandhi was made president of the All-India Khilafat Committee.
            - Gandhi favored launching Satyagraha and non-cooperation over the Khilafat issue, but the Indian National Congress (INC) was divided on this approach.
            - Some opposed Gandhi’s broader non-cooperation program, especially the council boycott, though he later gained Congress approval.
        - Muslim League and Congress Alliance
            - The Muslim League pledged full support to the Congress and its political agitations.
            - Tilak opposed a Hindu-Muslim alliance, advocating a secular basis instead. Much of the INC’s old guard also resisted this partnership.
        - Call for Complete Independence
            - At the 1921 Ahmedabad Congress session, Hasrat Mohani became the first to demand complete independence (Poorna Swaraj) for India, despite Gandhi’s opposition. He also coined the slogan "Inqalab Zindabad" (Long Live Revolution).

    - The Non-Cooperation Khilafat Movement (August 1920)
        - Initial Steps
            - Early 1920 saw a joint Hindu-Muslim deputation meet the Viceroy to address Khilafat grievances, with little success.
            - In February 1920, Gandhi declared the Khilafat issue had overshadowed Punjab wrongs and constitutional reforms, threatening non-cooperation if peace treaty terms dissatisfied Muslims.
        - June 1920
            - An all-party conference in Allahabad approved boycotts of schools, colleges, and law courts, appointing Gandhi to lead the effort.
        - August 31, 1920
            - Tilak passed away on August 1, 1920, a significant loss to the movement.
            - The Khilafat Committee formally launched its non-cooperation campaign in September 1920.
        - Calcutta Special Session (September 1920)
            - Presided over by Lala Lajpat Rai, this INC session approved non-cooperation until Punjab and Khilafat wrongs were addressed and Swaraj achieved. Despite support, opposition came from C.R. Das, Madan Mohan Malaviya, and Annie Besant.
        - Program of Non-Cooperation
            - Destructive Steps:
                - Boycott of government schools and colleges.
                - Boycott of law courts, with justice dispensed via panchayats.
                - Boycott of foreign cloth, promoting khadi and hand-spinning.
                - Renunciation of government honors and titles.
                - Boycott of legislative councils, which saw massive participation—Congress candidates withdrew, and voters largely abstained despite some internal dissent.
            - Constructive Steps:
                - Building national institutions and promoting unity.
        - Nagpur Session (December 1920)
            - Presided by C. Vijayaraghavachariar, this session endorsed non-cooperation for Khilafat, Punjab redress, and Swaraj.
            - The Congress shifted its goal from self-government via constitutional means to Swaraj through peaceful, legitimate, extra-constitutional mass struggle.
            - Organizational Changes (New Constitution by Gandhi):
                - Congress Committees formed from village to provincial levels.
                - An All-India Committee of 350 members, with a 15-member executive, was created to enable year-round work (a demand Tilak raised in 1916).
                - Provincial committees organized on linguistic lines.
                - Hindi promoted for official and non-official Congress work.
                - Membership opened to all over 21 for a four-anna annual fee.
            - Gandhi clarified Swaraj as self-government within the empire if possible, or outside if necessary, predicting its achievement within a year if fully implemented.
            - Dissenters like Jinnah, Besant, Khaparde, and Pal left the INC, favoring constitutional methods, while Surendranath Banerjee formed the Indian National Liberal Federation.

    - Phases of Struggle
        - First Phase (January to March 1921)
            - Gandhi and the Ali brothers toured nationwide, inspiring thousands of students to leave government institutions for around 800 national schools and colleges led by Acharya Narendra Dev, C.R. Das, Lala Lajpat Rai, Zakir Hussain, and Subhash Bose (principal of National College, Calcutta), including Jamia Millia, Kashi Vidyapeeth, Gujarat Vidyapeeth, and Bihar Vidyapeeth.
            - Prominent lawyers—Motilal Nehru, Jawaharlal Nehru, C.R. Das, C. Rajagopalachari, Saifuddin Kitchlew, Vallabhbhai Patel, Asaf Ali, T. Prakasam, and Rajendra Prasad—abandoned their practices.
            - Foreign cloth imports halved as heaps were publicly burned, and picketing targeted foreign liquor and toddy shops.
        - Second Phase (April to June 1921)
            - Focused on raising one crore rupees for the Tilak Swaraj Fund by August 1921, enrolling one crore Congress members, and installing 20 lakh charkhas by June 1930. The Tilak Swaraj Fund exceeded its goal, collecting one crore rupees.
        - Third Phase (July to November 1921)
            - Emphasized boycotting foreign cloth, opposing the Prince of Wales’ November visit, and promoting charkhas and khadi. Gandhi, dubbed a “half-naked fakir,” led this phase. Congress volunteer corps acted as a parallel police force.
            - Local Congress bodies were urged to start civil disobedience where people were ready. No-tax movements emerged in Midnapore (Bengal) and Guntur (Andhra) against union board taxes.
            - In Assam, strikes hit tea plantations, steamer services, and the Assam-Bengal Railways. The Prince of Wales’ visit in November 1921 triggered strikes and demonstrations, with Gandhi lighting a massive bonfire of foreign cloth at Elphinstone Mill, owned by nationalist Umar Shobhani.
            - Local movements like the Awadh Kisan Movement (UP), Eka Movement (UP), Mappila Revolt (Malabar), and Sikh agitation against mahants in Punjab grew in this defiant spirit.

    - Government Response
        - In May 1921, the government attempted to divide Khilafat leaders and Gandhi via the Gandhi-Reading Talks, where Viceroy Reading urged Gandhi to curb the Ali brothers’ violent rhetoric, but this failed.
        - By late 1921, repression intensified: Congress and Khilafat volunteer corps were declared illegal, public meetings and processions were banned, newspapers faced censorship, Congress and Khilafat offices were raided at midnight, police used lathis and gunfire on Satyagrahis, and by year-end, most prominent leaders except Gandhi were jailed.

    - INC vs. Government
        - Gandhi faced mounting pressure from Congress grassroots to launch civil disobedience. The 1921 Ahmedabad session, presided by C.R. Das (in jail, with Hakim Ajmal Khan acting), made Gandhi the sole authority on this decision.
        - On February 1, 1922, Gandhi threatened civil disobedience from Bardoli, Gujarat, unless political prisoners were freed and press controls lifted, setting February 5 as the start date. The Chauri Chaura incident on February 5 disrupted these plans.

    - Chauri Chaura Incident
        - In Chauri Chaura, police beat a volunteer leader campaigning against liquor sales and high food prices, then fired on a protesting crowd outside the police station. The enraged crowd set the station ablaze, trapping policemen inside. Those escaping were hacked to death and thrown back into the fire, killing 22 officers.
        - The Congress Working Committee met in Bardoli in February 1922, halting law-breaking activities, shifting focus to constructive work: promoting khadi, national schools, temperance, Hindu-Muslim unity, and anti-untouchability efforts.
        - Leaders like C.R. Das, Motilal Nehru, Subhash Bose, and Jawaharlal Nehru opposed Gandhi’s decision. Gandhi was arrested in March 1922, sentenced to six years, and held in Yerwada Jail, Poona, until his early release on February 5, 1924, due to ill health.

    - Participation of Various Sections
        - Middle Class
            - In elite centers like Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras, the response to Gandhi’s call was lukewarm, with little enthusiasm for resigning from government service or surrendering titles. Newcomers like Rajendra Prasad (Bihar) and Vallabhbhai Patel (Gujarat) found a voice in the movement.
        - Business Class
            - Indian businesses supported the economic boycott, benefiting from Swadeshi emphasis, though some big industrialists hesitated, fearing labor unrest in their factories.
        - Peasants
            - Peasants turned against landlords and traders, using the movement to vent frustrations against both British rule and local oppressors.
        - Students
            - Thousands abandoned government schools and colleges, becoming active movement volunteers.
        - Women
            - Women participated in large numbers, bolstering the Non-Cooperation Movement.
        - Hindu-Muslim Unity
            - The movement achieved significant Hindu-Muslim unity, a major success despite incidents like the Mappila Uprisings.

    - Swaraj Party & No-Changers
        - Aftermath of Non-Cooperation Movement
            - Why Withdrawal? (Gandhi’s Thoughts)
                - Gandhi believed people hadn’t fully grasped non-violence, and violence gave the colonial regime an excuse to crush the movement with force.
            - Other Causes for Failure
                - The movement showed signs of exhaustion. The Khilafat issue lost relevance when Mustafa Kemal Pasha’s uprising in Turkey in November 1922 stripped the Sultan of power, abolished the Caliphate in 1924, and modernized Turkey as a secular state.
            - Reactions by Others
                - Gandhi’s withdrawal shocked many Congress members, who protested the decision. Senior leaders like Motilal Nehru, C.R. Das, and Subhash Bose questioned why the nation suffered for a few villagers’ actions, with Bose calling it a “national calamity” in "The Indian Struggle".
            - End of the Movement
                - The Civil Disobedience Enquiry Committee found the country unready for mass civil disobedience, and constructive programs garnered limited response. On February 12, 1922, the Congress Working Committee’s Bardoli resolution condemned Chauri Chaura, officially ended the movement, and urged peasants and tenants to resume tax and rent payments.
            

- Post Non-Cooperation Era - Swarajists & No Changers
    - Swaraj Party & No Changers
        - Formation of Swaraj Party (January 1, 1923)
            - Connecting the Dots from the Last Lecture
                - Gandhi’s suspension of the Non-Cooperation Movement in February 1922 caused disappointment and a rift within Congress leadership. Some wanted to continue non-cooperation, constructive work, and council boycotts, while others sought to end the boycott and contest elections.
                - No Changers: Advocated continuing the original program, including leaders like Rajendra Prasad, Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, and C. Rajagopalachari.
                - Pro-Changers (Swarajists): Wanted to enter legislative councils to obstruct the British government from within, led by C.R. Das, Motilal Nehru, and Srinivasa Iyengar. This group later split into Responsivists and Non-Responsivists.
            - Key Context
                - In 1922, the All India Congress Committee (AICC) formed the Civil Disobedience Inquiry Committee, which advised Congress President C.R. Das that India wasn’t ready to relaunch Non-Cooperation. Recommendations included building a mass movement rooted in non-violence, educating farmers and laborers about their rights and self-rule, and starting initiatives like khadi, village enterprises, and Hindu-Muslim unity.
                - Post-1922, after the Non-Cooperation Movement ended, communal tensions flared in places like Multan (September 1922), Amritsar (April 1923), and Calcutta (May 1923).
            - Formation of Congress-Khilafat Swaraj Party
                - At the 1922 Gaya Congress Session (Bihar), presided by C.R. Das, he proposed council entry, but it was defeated. C.R. Das resigned as president, and Motilal Nehru stepped down as General Secretary, though both remained Congress members.
                - On January 1, 1923, C.R. Das, with Motilal Nehru as a secretary, formed the Congress-Khilafat Swaraj Party. Other leaders included N.C. Kelkar, Huseyn Shaheed Suhrawardy, Subhash Chandra Bose, and Vithalbhai Patel.
                - This created a deadlock, as both factions were equally dominant, leading to a compromise.

    - A Group Within the Congress
        - The tussle between No Changers and Pro Changers risked repeating the 1907 Surat Split. Following Gandhi’s advice, they chose to stay within Congress but operate independently.
        - Congress Special Session (Delhi, September 1923), presided by Maulana Azad, allowed Congressmen to contest upcoming elections.
        - At the 1923 Kakinada annual session, council entry was permitted, with the stance that non-cooperation could be practiced inside councils, thus avoiding a split.

    - Swarajists in Action
        - The Swarajists’ first gathering was in Allahabad in 1923. They declared ‘Dominion Status’ as a preliminary objective, leaving their long-term goal undefined.
        - Election Manifesto: Focused on the right of Indians to control their government, disrupting 1919 reforms, halting administrative functions, aligning the government with Indian perspectives, and backing Gandhi’s constructive initiatives outside councils.
        - The party’s constitution and ideology evolved over time, solidifying its position at the 1924 Belgaum Session, presided by Gandhi. It aimed for Swaraj through lawful, non-violent means, though Swaraj’s exact definition remained vague, and the party was skeptical of mass political involvement.

    - Party Makes Its Mark
        - 
        - General elections in November 1923 were held for the Central Legislative Assembly (145 seats, 105 elected) and Provincial Assemblies.
        - Swarajists allied with figures like M.A. Jinnah to form the Nationalist Party in the Central Legislative Assembly, with Motilal Nehru as opposition leader, known as the ‘Parliamentary Wing of the Congress’.
        - In March 1925, they elected Vithalbhai Patel as President of the Central Legislative Assembly. They became the largest party in Bombay, the Central Assembly, and Bengal Councils, forming similar alliances in provinces, consistently challenging the government.
        - 

    - Swarajists Mean Business
        - Focused on obstructing reforms from within, a strategy British authorities found more challenging than outright rebellion. They rejected votable budget parts and expenditures to affirm their rights and vetoed legislation enhancing bureaucratic power.
        - Constructive Aims: Establishing economic strategies to prevent wealth outflow, boosting local economic and industrial sectors, and championing workers’ and laboring class rights.
        - C.R. Das stated: “I want you to enter the Councils and secure a majority and put forward national demands. If they are not accepted, I want you to oppose the Government in every measure, good, bad, and indifferent and make the work of Council impossible.”

    - Gandhi’s Evolving Stance
        - Released from jail in February 1924, Gandhi initially opposed council entry ideologically, leading to disputes within Congress.
        - At the June 1924 AICC meeting in Ahmedabad, he pushed a resolution to sideline Swarajists, mandating dissenters to resign from AICC (Hand Spinning Resolution). However, with the Swarajists’ growing strength, he moderated his stance, reaching a compromise.
        - Eventually, Gandhi supported the Swarajists, endorsing them as Congress’s official negotiators with the government.
        - At the 1924 Belgaum Congress Session, the only session Gandhi presided over, a truce between Pro and No Changers was established. Gandhi gave Swarajists a majority in his Working Committee and reinforced constructive programs as the primary strategy for Swaraj.

    - Achievements of Swarajists
        - Legislative Achievements
            - Advocated for the release of political prisoners, provincial autonomy, and an immediate Round Table Conference for full self-governance.
            - In 1928, defeated the Public Safety Bill, which targeted socialist and communist activities by allowing detention and deportation without trial.
            - Overthrew dyarchy in Bengal and Central Provinces, cut salt tax and train fares, secured tax relief for provinces, scrapped excise duties, and pushed tariffs to support local industries.
            - Key legislation passed included the Workmen’s Compensation Act (1923), Indian Trade Union Act (1926), Indian Bar Councils Act (1926), and Child Marriage Prevention Act (1926).
            - Repealed repressive laws like the State Prisoners Act (1850) and Prevention of Seditious Meetings Act (1921).
            - Vithalbhai Patel became Speaker of the Central Legislative Assembly in 1925.
        - Muddiman Commission
            - Due to Swarajist persistence, the government established a committee to investigate defects in the 1919 Act and propose solutions.
        - Filling the Political Vacuum
            - They bridged the gap left by the Non-Cooperation Movement’s withdrawal.
        - Constructive Work Outside Councils
            - Promoted khadi (though resisted its compulsory status for Congress membership), supported the Vaikom Satyagraha against untouchability, and advocated alcohol prohibition.

    - Failure of Swarajists
        - Lack of coordination with mass struggle.
        - Limitation of their obstructionist strategy.
        - Conflicting ideas with coalition partners.
        - Failure to resist perks and privileges of office.
        - Failure to support peasants’ causes.
        - Bipin Chandra wrote: “The Swarajists failed to provide a coherent strategy for the national movement, and their tendency to rely on the councils alone was a strategic error.”

    - Decline of Swaraj Party
        - From 1925, the Swarajists’ council position weakened due to C.R. Das’s sudden death, widespread communal riots, loss of popular base, and a split between Responsivists and Non-Responsivists.
        - These factors led to major losses in the 1926 elections. The Simon Commission’s arrival further shifted the political landscape, diminishing the council entry movement’s impact.
        - The Swaraj Party merged with Congress for a unified push toward complete independence. In 1930, Swarajists left councils following the Lahore Congress resolution on Poorna Swaraj and the start of the Civil Disobedience Movement.

    - Understanding Responsivists and Non-Responsivists
        - Responsivists
            - Advocated cooperation with the government and holding office where possible. Leaders included Pandit Malaviya and Lala Lajpat Rai.
        - Non-Responsivists
            - Opposed office acceptance, favoring obstruction over cooperation. Led by Motilal Nehru.

- British Acts and Regulations (1858–1935)
    - GOI Act, 1858
        - Background
            - Key Context
                - The Revolt of 1857 dealt a fatal blow to the existing governance system, accelerating demands for transferring power from the East India Company to the Crown.
                - Lord Palmerston (British PM) first introduced a bill for this transfer.
                - Later, Edward Henry Stanley (the first Secretary of State for India) introduced “An Act for the Better Government of India,” passed on August 2, 1858.
                - 
        - Main Provisions
            - Power Transfer
                - Ended the Company’s rule in India, transferring power to the Crown.
                - Abolished the Court of Directors and Board of Control, shifting their powers to the Secretary of State for India and a 15-member Indian Council.
                    - The Indian Council assisted and advised the Secretary of State, governing India in the Crown’s name.
                    - The Secretary of State, a British Cabinet member, was accountable to Parliament and required to submit periodic reports on India’s moral and material progress.
                    - The Secretary of State was declared a corporate body, capable of suing and being sued in England and India.
                - The Governor General was titled Viceroy, becoming the Crown’s direct representative.
        - Significance
            - Impact of the Act
                - Marked the end of Company rule, though the power transfer was more formal than substantial.
                    - The name change aimed to erase past grievances and offer a fresh start for the empire.
                - Followed by Queen Victoria’s Proclamation on November 1, 1858, at a grand Durbar in Allahabad, known as the “Magna Carta of the People of India,” outlining future British policy in India.

    - Indian Councils Act, 1861
        - Background
            - Historical Context
                - The Regulating Act initiated centralization, culminating in the 1833 Act, which stripped provinces of legislative powers. The 1861 Act reversed this trend, starting decentralization.
                - Lord Canning (Governor General) and Sir Charles Wood (Secretary of State) facilitated its passage.
        - Main Provisions
            - Changes in Governance
                - Changes in the Viceroy’s Executive Council
                    - Expanded by adding a fifth member, a jurist.
                - Changes in the Central Legislature
                    - Size and Composition
                        - Enlarged by adding 6–12 additional members, nominated by the Governor General for two years, with at least half being non-officials. Legislative functions were strictly limited to law-making.
                    - Functions
                        - Restored legislative powers to Bombay and Madras Councils, though laws required the Governor General’s assent, imposing rigid restrictions.
                - Other Changes Introduced
                    - Allowed establishment of new councils in provinces like North Western Provinces (1886), Burma, and Punjab (1897).
                    - Empowered the Governor General to issue ordinances in emergencies (valid for six months) without Legislative Council concurrence.
                    - Authorized the Governor General to create new provinces, appoint Lieutenant Governors, and adjust territorial boundaries.
                    - Decentralization of Business
                        - The Governor General could make rules for efficient council transactions.
                        - Lord Canning introduced the portfolio system, dividing departments among council members, laying the foundation for India’s cabinet system.
        - Limitations
            - Legislative Constraints
                - No clear demarcation of legislative powers between Central and Local Legislatures; the Governor General retained authority to legislate for all of India.

    - Indian Councils Act, 1892
        - Main Provisions
            - Legislative Adjustments
                - Changes in the Central Legislature
                    - Increased additional members to 10–16, with at least half non-officials.
                    - Provided for 8–12 non-official members in Provincial Legislatures, though official majorities were maintained centrally and provincially.
                - Other Changes
                    - Empowered the Governor General to introduce limited elections.
                        - Regulations for nominating additional members were set by the Governor General-in-Council, though nominees were not representatives but appointees.
                        - Introduced a cautious, indirect election system, avoiding the term “election.”
                    - Expanded Functions
                        - Members could discuss the annual financial statement (unalterable) and ask questions on public interest matters with six days’ notice, though supplementary questions were barred.
            - Criticism
                - The Indian National Congress criticized the Act in its 1892 and 1893 sessions for failing to introduce direct elections.

    - Morley-Minto Reforms, 1909
        - Background
            - Political Climate
                - Introduced amid rising political unrest and dissatisfaction with British rule.
                - The 1906 British Liberal Party victory raised reform prospects, believing Indian legislative participation would strengthen British control.
                - The 1906 Shimla Deputation, led by Aga Khan, proposed separate Muslim electorates to Lord Minto.
        - Main Provisions
            - Structural Reforms
                - Indian Entry in Viceroy’s Executive Council
                    - Satyendra Sinha became the first Indian appointed in 1909.
                - Expansion of Members
                    - Increased elected members in Imperial and Provincial Legislative Councils, with non-official majorities in provinces.
                    - Central Legislative Council: 69 members (37 officials, 5 nominated non-officials, 27 elected non-officials), with seats for Muslims, British capitalists, landlords, and a general electorate.
                    - Emphasized representation by classes and interests over territorial representation.
                - Legislative Powers
                    - Members could discuss financial statements, move resolutions (as recommendations only), and ask supplementary questions, though some topics (e.g., foreign relations) were restricted.
                - Introduction of Elections
                    - Indirect elections for non-officials, based on class and community, with recognized bodies recommending candidates.
                - Provision of Separate Electorates
                    - Introduced for Muslims in the Central Legislative Council, with overrepresentation and low-income voting qualifications serving British strategic interests.

    - Montagu-Chelmsford Reforms, 1919
        - Background
            - Foundational Declaration
                - Based on the Montagu Declaration of August 20, 1917, promising increased Indian administrative participation and gradual self-governance within the British Empire.
        - Provisions
            - Governance Changes
                - Changes in Home Government
                    - Appointed a High Commissioner for India in London for trade, funded by Indian revenue, not the British Exchequer.
                - Changes in Government of India
                    - Viceroy’s Executive Council
                        - Three of eight members were to be Indian, aligning with the policy of Indian association.
                    - Bicameral System
                        - Replaced the single Legislative Council with the Council of States (Upper House) and Legislative Assembly (Lower House), with elected members under limited franchise.
                    - Expansion of Functions
                        - All members could ask supplementary and short-notice questions, enjoying freedom of speech.
                - Changes in Provincial Governments
                    - Provincial Autonomy
                        - Introduced via “Devolution Rules,” defining independent provincial powers in finance, legislation, and administration.
                    - Legislative Devolution
                        - Divided legislative items into Central and Provincial Lists, with residual powers vested in the Governor General-in-Council.
                        - Provincial List split into “Transferred” (managed by Indian ministers accountable to the Legislative Council) and “Reserved” subjects (managed by Governors and their Executive Councils), creating dyarchy.
                    - Financial Devolution
                        - Separated revenue sources: land revenue, excise, irrigation, and stamps became provincial; budgets were split from the center, with provinces authorized to frame their own.
                - Other Provisions
                    - Introduced direct elections with limited franchise.
                    - Extended communal representation to Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians, and Europeans, alongside Muslims.
                    - Established the Central Public Service Commission (1926) for civil servant recruitment.
                    - Provided for a Statutory Commission to review the Act after 10 years.
        - Points to Know
            - Additional Insights
                - Initially applied to eight provinces (Bengal, Madras, Bombay, UP, Punjab, Bihar, Orissa, Central Provinces), extended to Burma (1923) and NWFP later.
                - Women could neither vote nor contest elections, reflecting Britain’s own post-1918 suffrage timeline.
                - The Muddiman Report analyzed dyarchy’s implementation.

    - GOI Act, 1935
        - 
        - Background
            - Policy Foundation
                - Based on the White Paper on the Indian Constitution post-Third Round Table Conference, criticized by all political parties for falling short of complete independence.
        - Main Provisions
            - Structural Reforms
                - Contained four major provisions: All India Federation, Provincial Autonomy, dyarchy at the center, and safeguards vesting special powers with central and provincial executives.
                - Provided legal safeguards against misbehavior by Indian ministers and legislators; notably lacked a preamble.
                - Changes at National Level
                    - Proposal for an All India Federation (never materialized)
                        - Voluntary accession for states, requiring at least half of the 52 allotted Council of States seats and 50% of the states’ population to join.
                    - Abolished the Indian Council (from the 1858 Act) due to anti-India policy agitations.
                - Changes in Government of India
                    - Dyarchy at the Centre
                        - Introduced dyarchy centrally: Reserved Departments under the Governor General with appointed counselors; Transferred subjects advised by ministers answerable to the legislature.
                    - Bicameral Central Legislature
                        - Comprised the Governor General, Council of States, and Federal Assembly, with representation from British India and Princely States (disproportionate weightage for states as nationalist checks).
                - Changes in Provincial Government
                    - Provincial Autonomy
                        - All departments controlled by elected members, provinces recognized as separate legal entities deriving power from the Crown, free from GOI and SOS control except for specific purposes.
                        - Governors could issue ordinances or enact Governor’s Acts.
                    - Abolition of Dyarchy in Provinces
                        - Eliminated the distinction between Reserved and Transferred subjects, establishing fully responsible government with safeguards.
                    - Bicameral Legislature
                        - Introduced in six provinces: Bengal, Madras, Bombay, United Provinces, Bihar, and Assam.
                - Other Provisions
                    - Created new provinces (e.g., Burma separated from India).
                    - Lowered franchise, though tied to property qualifications.
                    - Extended separate electorates to women.
                    - Established Federal Bank, Federal Court (1935, 1937), and Federal Railway Authority (free from ministerial control).

    - Simon Commission (1927)
        - Simon Commission (Indian Statutory Commission)
            - Background and Formation
                - Context and Purpose
                    - The 1919 GOI Act mandated a review after 10 years. The Conservative government, led by Stanley Baldwin, advanced it to November 8, 1927, fearing Labour Party changes.
                    - An all-white, seven-member body chaired by Sir John Simon, excluding Indians (justified as reporting to British Parliament), aimed to assess India’s readiness for further reforms.
                    - Appointed amid Swaraj Party activities and youth movements led by Jawaharlal Nehru and Subhas Chandra Bose.
                - Important Reports Before Simon Commission
                    - Lee Commission (examined racial composition of Imperial Civil Services).
                    - Muddiman Commission (examined dyarchy issues in the 1919 Act).
                    - Linlithgow Commission (explored Indian agriculture and rural economy crises).
            - Congress and Other Political Reactions
                - Opposition and Boycotts
                    - Congress, at the Madras Session (December 1927, presided by M.A. Ansari), resolved to boycott the commission “at every stage and in every form.”
                        - Jawaharlal Nehru pushed for complete independence over Dominion Status.
                        - Congress initiated an All Parties Conference to draft a constitution with other groups.
                    - Hindu Mahasabha, Liberal Federation (Tej Bahadur Sapru), Jinnah’s Muslim League faction, and Indian Industrial and Commercial Congress also boycotted.
                - Supporters
                    - Unionists in Punjab, Justice Party in the South, and All India Achyut Federation supported the commission.
            - Delhi Proposals (December 1927)
                - Unity Efforts
                    - Aimed to unite Congress and Muslim League, with Jinnah presiding. Key Muslim demands:
                        - Joint electorates with reserved Muslim seats.
                        - One-third Muslim representation in the Central Legislative Assembly.
                        - Proportional representation in Punjab and Bengal.
                        - New Muslim-majority provinces: Sindh, Balochistan, NWFP.
                    - Congress accepted most demands, but Muslim League splits and Hindu Mahasabha opposition derailed consensus.
            - All Parties Conference (1928)
                - Constitutional Initiative
                    - Convened by M.A. Ansari with Congress, Muslim League, Hindu Mahasabha, Sikh Central League, and others, agreeing on Dominion Status.
                    - Led to the Nehru Report (May 1928), India’s first constitutional drafting effort, chaired by Motilal Nehru.
            - Challenges and Outcomes
                - British Perspective
                    - Lord Birkenhead defended excluding Indians, citing “vital differences” among political groups, challenging them to draft a unified constitution—spurring the Nehru Report.
            - Key Recommendations of Nehru Report (May 1928)
                - Constitutional Framework
                    - Dominion Status: India as a self-governing Commonwealth.
                    - Fundamental Rights: Universal adult suffrage, equal rights (religion, speech) for all, regardless of religion, caste, or gender.
                    - Minority Safeguards: Joint electorates with reserved minority seats; special provisions for Muslim-majority provinces (e.g., Sindh separated, NWFP provincial status).
                    - Provincial Reorganization: Linguistic basis for provinces.
                    - Federal Structure: Federal government with provincial autonomy, residual powers centralized (more unitary than federal).
                    - Responsible Government: Bicameral legislature and court hierarchy.
                - Additional Recommendations
                    - Baluchistan as a full province; communal representation review after 20 years.
                - Note on Princely States
                    - The Nehru Committee struggled with Princely States’ status. The 1927 States People Conference sought self-governance, while the Butler Committee (under Sir Harcourt Butler) emphasized British paramountcy to preserve them.
            - Calcutta Congress Session (December 1928)
                - Internal Dynamics
                    - Approved the Nehru Report, but younger leaders (Jawaharlal Nehru, Subhas Chandra Bose, S. Satyamurthy) opposed Dominion Status.
                        - The Independence of India League (April 1928) was formed by Nehru, Bose (Secretary), and S. Srinivasa Iyengar (President).
                    - Congress set a one-year deadline for Dominion Status, threatening civil disobedience for complete independence by 1929.
            - Opposition and Amendments
                - Divisions Emerge
                    - Opposed by Muslim League (Jinnah), Hindu Mahasabha, and Sikh League.
                    - Jinnah’s rejected amendments: one-third Muslim representation centrally, proportional representation in Punjab and Bengal, residual powers to provinces.
                    - Result: Jinnah’s 14 Points (March 1929) shaped future Muslim League demands.
            - Nehru Report Compromises and Submission
                - Final Adjustments
                    - Compromises: Joint electorates except in Muslim-majority areas; Sindh separation post-Dominion Status; unitary structure with centralized residual powers.
                    - Approved by the All Parties Conference without Jinnah’s amendments, Congress gave Britain a one-year deadline, threatening non-violent protest in 1930 if unmet.


- March to Civil Disobedience Movement and The Story
    - Gandhi’s Preparations for Action
        - Nationwide Mobilization
            - In 1929, Gandhi traveled extensively across India, rallying the public for direct political action. He urged the youth to prepare for challenges and stressed the importance of constructive work in villages.
        - Boycott of Foreign Goods
            - The Congress Working Committee (CWC) set up a Foreign Cloth Boycott Committee to organize public burnings of foreign cloth and promote the use of Indian-made goods.

    - Lord Irwin’s Declaration (October 1929) - Diwali Declaration
        - Lord Irwin acknowledged Dominion Status as the ultimate goal of India’s constitutional progress, referencing the Montagu Declaration of 1917. He promised a Round Table Conference after the Simon Commission’s report but provided no specific timeline, which left Indian leaders disappointed.

    - Delhi Manifesto (November 2, 1929)
        - Prominent national leaders outlined conditions for attending the Round Table Conference:
            - The conference must aim to formulate a constitution implementing Dominion Status and function as a constituent assembly.
            - Congress should have majority representation.
            - A general amnesty must be granted for political prisoners.
        - Viceroy Irwin rejected these demands, escalating tensions and setting the stage for confrontation.

    - Lahore Congress Session (December 1929)
        - Jawaharlal Nehru was elected Congress President.
        - Key resolutions passed included:
            - Declaring complete independence, or Poorna Swaraj, as Congress’s ultimate goal.
            - Deciding to boycott the Round Table Conference.
            - Empowering the CWC to launch a civil disobedience movement, including non-payment of taxes.
            - Calling upon Congress members in legislatures to resign their seats.

    - First Independence Day (January 26, 1930)
        - January 26, 1930, was observed as the first Independence Day, also called Swarajya Day.
        - On December 31, 1929:
            - Nehru hoisted the tricolor flag on the banks of the Ravi River, symbolizing freedom, amid slogans of “Inquilab Zindabad.”
            - Across India, public meetings were held, the Independence Pledge was read in local languages, and the national flag was raised in towns and villages.

    - Gandhi’s 11-Point Ultimatum
        - Demands Presented to the Government:
            - General Interest Demands:
                - Reduce expenditure on the Army and salaries of highest-grade services by 50%.
                - Enforce prohibition of intoxicants.
                - Reform the Criminal Investigation Department (CID).
                - Release all political prisoners.
                - Accept the Postal Reservation Bill.
                - Amend the Arms Act of 1878 to allow popular control over the issuance of firearm licenses.
            - Demands Catering to Capitalists’ Interests:
                - Reduce the rupee-sterling exchange ratio to 1s 4d.
                - Reserve coastal shipping exclusively for Indians.
                - Provide protection for Indian textiles.
            - Demands Catering to Peasants’ Interests:
                - Reduce the agricultural tax by 50% and place it under legislative control.
                - Eliminate the government’s monopoly on salt and abolish the salt tax.
        - Government’s Response:
            - When the government did not adequately address these demands, the CWC authorized Gandhi to launch the Civil Disobedience Movement (CDM) at his discretion.
            - Gandhi stated: “On bended knees I asked for bread and received a stone instead. The English nation responds only to force and I am not surprised by the viceregal reply.”
        - Additional Note:
            - The Royal Commission on Indian Currency and Finance (1926), also known as the Hilton Young Commission, recommended the establishment of the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), which was founded in 1935.

    - Launch of Civil Disobedience Movement
        - Preparations
            - The CWC empowered Gandhi to decide the timing and location of the CDM. By February 1930, he identified the salt tax as a unifying issue due to its impact on all Indians.
        - Dandi March (March 12 – April 6, 1930)
            - Gandhi informed Viceroy Irwin of his intentions on March 2, 1930. Starting with 78 followers from Sabarmati Ashram, he marched 240 miles to Dandi, addressing crowds along the way.
            - On April 6, 1930, he violated the salt law by picking up a lump of salt, inspiring millions to do the same.
            - Symbolism: The march showcased nonviolent resistance and India’s collective defiance; Subhas Chandra Bose compared it to Napoleon’s march to Paris.

    - Civil Disobedience Movement - Phase 1
        - Spread Across Regions
            - Tamil Nadu: C. Rajagopalachari led a salt march to Vedaranniyam, supported by anti-liquor campaigns.
            - Malabar: K. Kelappan organized salt marches, while P. Krishna Pillai resisted police brutality at Calicut.
            - Andhra Pradesh: Salt marches took place in East and West Godavari, Krishna, and Guntur districts.
            - Bengal: Salt protests occurred in Midnapur and rural areas, combined with tax resistance.
            - Assam: Students protested the Cunningham Circular, which restricted political participation.
            - Peshawar: Local demonstrations were supported by the Garhwal Rifles, who refused to fire on unarmed crowds.
            - Sholapur: Workers went on strike, destroyed symbols of British authority, and established a parallel government.
            - Manipur and Nagaland: Rani Gaidinliu, a 13-year-old Naga spiritual leader, raised a revolt against British rule, following her cousin Haipou Jadonang.
            - Dharasana Raid: After Gandhi’s arrest on May 4, 1930, Sarojini Naidu and others led a nonviolent raid on the Dharasana Salt Works, facing brutal repression.
        - Mobilization Methods
            - Tools and Actions: Prabhat pheris (early morning processions), vanar senas (youth brigades), manjari senas (women’s brigades), secret pamphlets (patrikas), and magic lantern shows spread awareness.
            - Forest Law Violations: Protests occurred in Central Provinces and Karnataka.
            - No-tax Campaigns: Villagers in Gujarat avoided taxes by migrating to neighboring princely states.
        - Government Response
            - Repressive Measures: Issued two ordinances—the Prevention of Intimidation Ordinance and the Unlawful Instigation Ordinance—declared Congress and its affiliates illegal in June 1930, and imposed censorship on press and correspondence.
            - Negotiation Attempts: In July 1930, Lord Irwin proposed a Round Table Conference, emphasizing Dominion Status, and permitted Tej Bahadur Sapru and M.R. Jayakar to explore peace with Congress. In August, Motilal and Jawaharlal Nehru met Gandhi in jail, reiterating demands for independence, control over defense and finance, and an independent tribunal to settle British financial claims.

    - First Round Table Conference (November 12, 1930 – January 19, 1931)
        - Participants: Included Indian minorities, princely states, and British officials; Congress and prominent business leaders boycotted it.
        - Outcomes: Agreement on an All-India Federation including British provinces and princely states, autonomy for British provinces, and Dr. B.R. Ambedkar’s demand for separate electorates for depressed classes.

    - Gandhi-Irwin Pact (March 5, 1931)
        - Negotiation Process
            - Appeal by Liberals: Members of the Indian Liberal Party, including Tej Bahadur Sapru, C.Y. Chintamani, and Srinivasa Sastri, urged Gandhi to negotiate with the Viceroy.
            - Release of Leaders: On January 25, 1931, Gandhi and other CWC members were released unconditionally, and the CWC authorized Gandhi to hold discussions with Irwin.
        - British Concessions
            - Immediate release of all political prisoners not convicted of violence.
            - Remission of all fines not yet collected.
            - Return of all confiscated lands not sold to third parties.
            - Lenient treatment for government servants who resigned during the movement.
            - Right to make salt in coastal villages for personal consumption (not for sale).
            - Right to peaceful and non-aggressive picketing of liquor and foreign goods shops.
            - Withdrawal of emergency ordinances imposed during the CDM.
        - Demands Rejected
            - Gandhi’s call for a public inquiry into police excesses was declined.
            - The demand to commute the death sentences of Bhagat Singh and his comrades to life imprisonment was also rejected.

    - Karachi Special Congress Session (March 26–31, 1931)
        - President: Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel.
        - Key Developments: Approved the Gandhi-Irwin Pact, adopted resolutions on Fundamental Rights and a National Economic Programme (drafted by Jawaharlal Nehru with M.N. Roy’s help), expressed disapproval of political violence while admiring martyrs’ contributions, and reiterated the goal of Poorna Swaraj.
        - Significance: Marked the first time a clear definition of Swaraj was spelled out for the masses.

    - Second Round Table Conference (September 7 – December 1, 1931)
        - Participants: Congress, represented by Gandhi, Madan Mohan Malviya, Sarojini Naidu, G.D. Birla, and others, joined alongside Muslim League, Hindu Mahasabha, and depressed class representatives, including Dr. Ambedkar.
        - Key Issues: The Labour Government was replaced by a National Government, lacking a unified approach; Lord Irwin was succeeded by Lord Willingdon, breaking communication. Communal representation dominated discussions, resulting in a Minorities’ Pact for separate electorates.
        - Outcome: Ended without agreement; MacDonald hinted at a unilateral Communal Award, establishing NWFP and Sindh as Muslim-majority provinces and creating an Indian Consultative Committee plus three expert committees on finance, franchise, and federation.

                - Abolished the Indian Council established under the 1858 Act due to widespread agitation against its anti-India policies.
            - Changes in Government of India:
                - Introduced dyarchy at the center, with Reserved Departments administered by the Governor General through his appointed counselors (responsible only to him) and Transferred subjects managed by the Governor General on the advice of popular ministers answerable to the legislature.
                - Established a bicameral central legislature with the Governor General and two houses—the Council of States and the Federal Assembly—featuring representation from British India and Princely States, with disproportionate weightage for states to check nationalists.
            - Changes in Provincial Government:
                - Granted Provincial Autonomy, with all provincial departments controlled by elected members. Provinces were recognized as separate legal entities deriving power directly from the British Crown, free from GOI and Secretary of State control except for specific purposes. Governors could issue ordinances or enact Governor’s Acts.
                - Abolished dyarchy in provinces, removing the distinction between Reserved and Transferred subjects, establishing a fully responsible government subject to certain safeguards.
                - Introduced bicameral legislatures in six provinces: Bengal, Madras, Bombay, United Provinces, Bihar, and Assam.
            - Other Provisions:
                - Created new provinces, such as separating Burma from India.
                - Lowered the franchise, though it remained tied to property qualifications.
                - Extended separate electorates to include women.
                - Established the Federal Bank, the Federal Court (in 1935 and 1937), and the Federal Railway Authority, which was free from control by ministers and counselors.
            

- Indian National Movement Post CDM (1934–1939)
    - 
    - Understanding Depressed Classes Politics in the 1900s
        - Introduction
            - The early 20th century marked a transformative period for the Depressed Classes in India. British colonialism opened new avenues for social mobility, challenging the rigid caste hierarchy.
            - Awareness of rights grew among the Depressed Classes, intensifying tensions with caste Hindus.
            - Representation and equality emerged as central issues, shaped by key leaders and policies in their fight for political and social justice.
        - Key Developments: The Minorities Pact
            - Context
                - During the Round Table Conference (RTC), Dr. B.R. Ambedkar initially supported joint electorates with reservations but shifted his stance when his demand for universal adult suffrage was rejected.
                - He aligned with Muslim, Anglo-Indian, and Christian representatives to form the Minorities Pact.
            - Provisions
                - Proposed separate electorates for the Depressed Classes, with a referendum planned after 20 years to reassess the arrangement.
            - Reason for Shift
                - Ambedkar’s universal adult suffrage proposal was opposed by the Muslim League, princely states, and the British government, which hesitated to adopt it.
                - Without universal suffrage, Dalit representatives risked being elected mainly by caste Hindus, leaving their political influence stagnant.
        - Key Developments: The Rajah-Moonje Pact (1932)
            - 
            - Overview (No Need to Memorize)
                - In 1932, the Hindu Mahasabha, aiming to preserve Hindu unity, collaborated with M.C. Rajah.
                - Provisions included reserved seats for Dalits within joint electorates to promote unity while consolidating Hindu interests.
                - Impact: Rajah’s shift from supporting separate electorates to joint electorates split Dalit opinion, reflecting the evolving political landscape and lack of consensus among Dalit leaders.
        - The Communal Award (August 16, 1932)
            - Background
                - Announced by British PM Ramsay MacDonald, based on the Lothian Committee (Indian Franchise Committee) recommendations after the Second RTC, to ensure representation for minorities and marginalized groups, including Muslims, Sikhs, Christians, Anglo-Indians, Europeans, and Depressed Classes.
            - Lothian Committee Recommendations (For Understanding)
                - Expansion of electorates for provincial and federal assemblies.
                - Reservation of 2-5% of seats for women.
                - Specific provisions treating Depressed Classes as a distinct minority.
                - Left representation decisions for princely states to the states themselves.
            - Key Provisions
                - Allocated separate electorates for Muslims, Sikhs, Christians, Anglo-Indians, Europeans, and Depressed Classes.
                - Reserved 71 seats for Depressed Classes in provincial legislatures (later raised to 148 under Gandhi) and 18% of total seats in the central legislature.
                - Provided weightage to Muslims in minority provinces.
                - Doubled the number of seats in provincial legislatures for better representation.
                - Reserved 3% of seats for women in most provinces.
                - Allotted seven seats for Marathas in Bombay.
                - Included educational grants to improve facilities for Depressed Classes.
            - Responses
                - Congress: Opposed separate electorates but avoided altering the Award without minority consent, adopting a stance of neither acceptance nor rejection.
                - Gandhi: Saw it as a threat to Indian unity, opposed segregating Depressed Classes politically, and launched an indefinite fast to demand joint electorates with reserved seats.
        - Poona Pact (September 1932)
            - Agreement signed between Mahatma Gandhi and Dr. B.R. Ambedkar at Yerwada Jail, Poona, to resolve the dispute over separate electorates for Depressed Classes.
            - Key Provisions
                - Increased reserved seats for Depressed Classes from 71 to 148 in provincial legislatures and maintained 18% in the central legislature.
                - Established joint electorates with reserved seats for Depressed Classes.
                - Introduced a primary election system, where an electoral college of Depressed Class candidates would finalize nominations.
                - Provided educational grants and ensured no disabilities for Depressed Classes.
                - Made the reservation system subject to future mutual agreement.
            - Outcome
                - Amended the Communal Award and was approved by the British government.

    - Post-CDM and the Transition to Constitutional Politics
        - End of Civil Disobedience Movement (CDM)
            - In April 1934, Gandhi withdrew the CDM, shifting from mass struggles to a phase of non-mass political activity.
            - Three perspectives emerged on the next steps:
                - Gandhian Constructive Work: Focused on Harijan upliftment and rural reconstruction.
                - Constitutional Struggle: Advocated by M.A. Ansari and Asaf Ali for participating in elections and working within legislative frameworks.
                - Leftist Perspective: Nehru and others pushed for continued non-constitutional mass movements, criticizing Gandhi’s “Struggle-Truce-Struggle” approach and favoring “Struggle-Victory.”
        - Understanding These Factions: Neo-Swarajists
            - Council Entry
                - Congress leaders, dubbed Neo-Swarajists, advocated participating in the 1934 Central Legislative Assembly (CLA) elections.
                - In 1933, S. Satyamurthy founded the Madras Swaraj Party to support council entry.
                - C. Rajagopalachari pushed for council entry to broaden Congress’s political base and prepare for future mass struggles.
                - Unlike earlier Swarajists, they aimed for cooperation in legislative councils, including assuming office and forming ministries.
        - Understanding These Factions: Socialists
            - Class Struggle
                - Led by Jawaharlal Nehru, Subhas Chandra Bose, and Jayaprakash Narayan, socialists within Congress focused on:
                    - Mobilizing workers and peasants for a more equitable society.
                    - Nationalizing key industries and resources and undertaking land reforms.
                    - Establishing a planned economy.
                - They opposed council entry, believing it diverted focus from the anti-imperialist struggle, with Nehru strongly against participating in elections.
        - Gandhi Resigns from Congress (1934)
            - By 1934, Gandhi felt out of sync with Congress currents:
                - Many favored parliamentary politics, which he opposed.
                - His emphasis on spinning and Harijan work alienated some workers.
                - Nehru’s socialist group was gaining influence.
            - In October 1934, Gandhi resigned from Congress to serve better through thought, word, and deed.
        - Shift to Constitutional Politics
            - May 1934 (AICC at Patna): Congress established a Parliamentary Board to contest upcoming elections.
            - November 1934 (CLA Elections): Congress won 45 of 75 seats reserved for Indians, becoming the largest party and marking a shift from confrontation to constitutional politics.
        - Congress Nationalist Party
            - Formed by M.M. Malaviya and Madhav Shrihari Aney, who split from Congress to protest the 1932 Communal Award. After the 1934 elections, Congress and the Nationalist Party together held a majority in the CLA.
            - 
        - Lucknow Session (April 1936)
            - President: Jawaharlal Nehru.
            - Key Outcomes:
                - Highlighted socialism as vital for global and Indian issues, with three socialists—Jayaprakash Narayan, Acharya Narendra Dev, and Achyut Patwardhan—in the CWC.
                - Resolved to contest elections based on an election manifesto.
                - Advocated equal rights for princely state residents, matching the rest of India.
            - Left the question of office acceptance pending.
        - Faizpur Congress Session (December 1936) - First Rural Session
            - President: Jawaharlal Nehru.
            - Key Outcomes:
                - Nehru condemned fascism.
                - Called for a Constituent Assembly to draft India’s constitution.
                - Adopted an Agrarian Programme:
                    - Reduced land tax and rent by 50%.
                    - Recognized peasant unions (Kisan Sabhas).
                    - Aimed to abolish taxes paid to landlords completely.
        - 1937 Provincial Elections
            - Held in eleven provinces under the GOI Act 1935; Congress won majorities in seven, forming ministries except in Bengal, Punjab, and Sindh.
            - Performed poorly in Upper House elections due to a limited franchise favoring landlords and moneylenders.
            - Note: CLA elections of 1934 were under the GOI Act 1919, while 1937 provincial elections followed the GOI Act 1935.

        - Question of Office Acceptance (1937)
            - March 1937 (AICC Meeting, Presided by Rajendra Prasad): Conditionally accepted office if governors refrained from using special powers under the GOI Act 1935 to interfere with ministries.
            - June 1937: Viceroy clarified governors would not intervene in government affairs regarding special powers.
            - July 1937 (CWC at Wardha): Authorized office acceptance.
            - Congress formed a Parliamentary Sub-Committee (Sardar Patel, Maulana Abul Kalam Azad, Rajendra Prasad) to prevent British divide-and-rule tactics.
            - Resulted in 28 months of Congress rule focused on public welfare and reforms.
            - Gandhi in Harijan: “These offices have to be held lightly and not tightly. They are crowns of thorns.”
            - Note: Leftists opposed office acceptance as collaboration with imperialism, while right-wing leaders like C. Rajagopalachari supported it for enabling peasant and worker reforms.
        - Key Developments During Congress Rule (1937–1939)
            - Administrative Reforms
                - Repealed emergency laws (e.g., Public Safety Act) and lifted press restrictions.
                - Released political prisoners and removed bans on groups like Hindustan Seva Dal (Communist Party ban remained).
            - Social Reforms
                - Promoted Harijan welfare through temple entry and scholarships.
                - Expanded educational facilities and public health initiatives.
            - Economic Reforms
                - Encouraged khadi production with subsidies.
                - Imposed prohibition in certain areas.
            - Labour Reforms
                - Mediated trade union protests (e.g., Industrial Disputes Bill 1938).
            - Agrarian Focus
                - Adopted a radical agrarian program at the 1936 Faizpur Session.
        - Resignation of Congress Ministries (October 1939)
            - Congress ministries resigned due to India’s involvement in World War II without consultation.
            

- Indian National Movement (1937 - 1947)
    - Congress Leadership and Ideological Divides
        - Subhas Chandra Bose’s Leadership
            - Early Actions
                - In 1921, participated in the Non-Cooperation Movement (NCM) and became a disciple of C.R. Das, became the editor of ‘Forward’, Newspaper of Swaraj party.
                - In 1930, founded the Congress Democratic Party, and assumed the presidency of AITUC.
                - In 1933, exiled after CDM to Europe - Returned in 1936.
            - Haripura Congress (1938)
                - Bose was elected president; believed Congress ministries had immense revolutionary potential.
                - Advocated economic planning and initiated a National Planning Committee.
                - Session also had resolutions like
                    - Establishment of a Socialist State in India
                    - Abolition of Landlordism
                    - Protection of Workers Right and welfare of Peasants and Farmers.
                - At the end of session, Congress also decided to send a medical mission to work with Chinese armed forces, who were facing Japanese aggression.
            - Basic Information
                - Congress leaders were unable to collaborate with a president who publicly questioned their nationalist credentials, Bose criticized prominent leaders in his book - “The Indian Struggle” where he labelled them as ‘Rightist’ and accused them of compromising with the British by accepting the federal scheme outlined in 1935 Act.
            - Presidential Re-Election - Tripuri Congress Session (1939)
                - Despite Gandhi’s opposition, Bose defeated Pattabhi Sitaramayya.
                - Gandhi described Pattabhi defeat as his own defeat.
            - Ideological Differences
                - Bose favored immediate struggle for independence, proposing a six-month ultimatum to Britain.
                - Gandhi opposed this, believing neither the Congress nor the masses were ready.
            - Resignation and Forward Bloc Formation
                - Bose resigned in April 1939 due to lack of support - Rajendra Prasad new president.
                - Formed the Forward Bloc in May 1939 to mobilize radical elements within the Congress, but faced disciplinary action for calling an All India Protest against the AICC resolution.
                    - Thus removed from his post as President of Bengal Provincial Congress Committee.
                    - Banned holding any elective office in Congress for next 3 Years
    - World War II and Congress Response
        - Resignation of Congress Ministries (October 1939)
            - Protesting Britain’s unilateral decision to involve India in the war without consultation.
            - Gandhi welcomed the resignations, calling them a "bitter pill" necessary to cleanse the Congress.
        - Divergent Views Within Congress
            - Support for Britain
                - Gandhi and Rajagopalachari sympathized with Britain’s anti-fascist stance but sought conditional support.
            - Opposition to British Policies
                - Nehru opposed participation until India gained freedom.
                - Socialists and Subhas Bose viewed the war as imperialist and demanded immediate resistance.
            - CWC resolution, 1939
                - Argued India shouldn’t fight a war if it wasn’t free itself.
                - Ask for an end to British Rule in colonies and full democracy in India.
                - Wanted the Govt. to state its goals for the war and how it would bring democracy to India.
                - Offered support for 2 basic conditions
                    - After the War, A constituent Assembly to determine the political structure of free India
                    - Immediately, some form of responsible Govt should be established at the center.
            - Muslim League Response
                - Agreed to support Britain in exchange of protecting Muslim rights in India.
                - Jinnah demanded that Muslim League should be recognised as the only voice for Indian Muslims and wanted the power to reject further changes in the Constitution.

        - Viceroy Linlithgow turned down the Congress Demands
            - Argued giving significant powers to Indians was not suitable during the War.

            - Declined to specify British Goals in War - Says Britain fighting against aggression.

            - October 23, 1939, CWC meeting at Wardha

            - Dominion Status

                - These to remain under British control until the war ends.

                - A commission to safeguard minority interests.

            - Princely States

            - Hindu MahaSabha
                - Criticised the right to secede
            - Depressed Classes
                - Thought Partition would leave them at the mercy of upper caste Hindus.
            - Sikhs
                - Objected the Partition would take away Punjab from them.

            - Rigid - “Take it or leave it” attitude.

    - Indian National Army (1942 - 1945)
        - Indian Independence League (IIL)
            - In March 1942, Rash Behari Bose, a revolutionary leader in Japan since 1915, organized a meeting in Tokyo with other Indian leaders to form the IIL.
            - In June 1942, during a meeting in Bangkok, Rash Behari Bose was appointed as the President of the IIL.
            - A resolution was passed to establish the Indian National Army (INA), also known as the Azad Hind Fauz.
            - It was decided that Subhas Chandra Bose would lead the movement in the future.
        - Formation of the INA
            - INA enlisted volunteers from Indians overseas and ex-British Indian soldiers in Southeast Asia.
            - Captain Mohan Singh was made the Commander-in-Chief.
            - The INA grew to about 40,000 members, preparing to confront British forces in India.
            - Disagreements between Captain Mohan Singh and the Japanese led to the transfer of INA leadership to Subhas Chandra Bose.
        - INA under Subhas Chandra Bose
            - In July 1943, Rash Behari Bose handed over the leadership to Subhas Chandra Bose.
            - Subhas Bose established the Arzi Hukumat-e-Azad Hind (Provisional Government of Free India) in Singapore on October 21, 1943.
            - The Provisional Government declared war on Britain and the USA and was recognized by the Axis powers.
            - In Dec 1943, Japanese handed over Andaman and Nicobar Island to Bose - Renaming Shaheed and Swaraj Dweep.
            - INA HQ’s shifted from Singapore to Rangoon in January 1944 and army prepared to march towards Delhi (Dilli Chalo)
                - At this time regiment of INA were named after Gandhi, Nehru, Azar, Bose, Rani Jhansi (Captain Lakshmi Sehgal)
                - INA Army was arrested by Allied Forces in 1945 meanwhile Subash Bose died in an air crash on August 18, 1945 in Taipei, Taiwan
    - The Story Continues - CR Formula (1944)
        - Following Gandhi’s release from Jail on May 5, 1944 - He tried to revive Congress and also initiated discussions with Muslim League in July 1944.
        - C. R. Formula (1944)
            - Proposed by: C. Rajagopalachari
            - Objective: To resolve the deadlock between the Indian National Congress and the Muslim League over the demand for Pakistan and to facilitate cooperation between the two parties for achieving Indian independence.
            - Main Points
                - The Muslim League was to cooperate with the Congress in forming a Provisional Interim Government for a transitional period and in return League's demand for Pakistan was recognized, but only through a democratic process involving a plebiscite.
                - All parties would be allowed to express their views on partition before the plebiscite.
                - After World War II, a plebiscite would decide whether Muslim-majority areas in the North-West and North-East would:
                    - Form a separate sovereign state (Pakistan), or Remain a part of India.
                - If separation occurred, agreements would be entered into for essential services such as:
                    - Defence
                    - Communication
                    - Commerce.
        - Reactions to C. R. Formula (1944)
            - Muslim League's Stand
                - Supported the concept of a two-nation theory.
                - Muhammad Ali Jinnah proposed that
                    - Only Muslims in the North-West and North-East should vote in the plebiscite.
                    - The rest of the Indian population should not be involved in the decision-making process.
            - Congress's Stand
                - Congress was willing to work with the Muslim League to achieve independence.
                - However, Congress rejected the formula as
                    - It accepted the principle of partition.
                    - The League's primary goal was the creation of a separate nation, which Congress opposed.
            - Other Reactions
                - Hindu Leaders
                    - Led by Vir Savarkar, Hindu leaders opposed the formula, as they disagreed with conceding to the demand for Pakistan.
                - Sikh Leaders
                    - Leaders like Master Tara Singh opposed the plan, fearing division of the Sikh community in Punjab, leaving Sikhs on both sides of the dividing line.
    - Desai Liaquat Pact (1945)
        - Context
            - The failure of the Gandhi-Jinnah Talks (1944) left a deadlock between the INC and the Muslim League.
            - Secret talks were held between Bhulabhai Desai, a senior Congress leader, and Liaquat Ali Khan, a prominent leader of the Muslim League, in January 1945.
        - Objective
            - To negotiate an agreement for a future coalition government representing both Hindus and Muslims equally in the independent government of India.
        - Key Points of the Pact
            - Recognition of the Muslim League: - Desai acknowledged the Muslim League as the representative of Muslims.
            - Formation of an Interim Government: An interim government would be formed with equal representation of the Congress and the League.
            - Liaquat's Concessions: Liaquat Ali Khan gave up the demand for a separate Muslim state (Pakistan).
            - Seats for Minorities: The pact proposed 20% reserved seats for minorities in the government.
        - Outcome
            - The Desai-Liaquat Pact was never formally endorsed by either the Congress or the Muslim League.
    - Special Mention - Sapru Committee Report (1945)
    - Wavell Plan (1945)
        - Background
            - By the end of 1944, with the war turning in favor of the Allies, the British realized that
                - The Indian situation, post-Quit India Movement, could not be ignored and prolonged use of force to subdue India indefinitely was impractical
            - Urgency of Finding a Solution - The upcoming 1945 General Elections in England required the Conservatives to urgently address Indian issues
            - The plan was designed to prevent Congress leaders from exploiting the post-war climate of unemployment and economic challenges.
        - Key Features of the Wavell Plan
            - Restructured Executive Council: - The Executive Council was to be restructured with all members, except the Governor-General and Commander-in-Chief, being Indian representatives.
            - Interim Government: - The interim government would function within the framework of the 1935 Government of India Act and would remain answerable to the Central Assembly.
            - Joint List of Nominees
                - Political parties were to collaborate in creating a joint list of nominees for the Executive Council.
                - If consensus was not achieved, separate lists could be submitted.
            - Equal Representation - Equal representation was to be provided to caste Hindus and Muslims in the Executive Council.
            - Governor-General's Powers -The Governor-General would exercise veto power, but only on the advice of his ministers.
            - Future Constitution - Provisions were made to explore negotiations for a new constitution after the war.
    - Simla Conference (June 1945)
        - To implement the Wavell Plan, Lord Wavell organized a conference in Simla
            - Congress was represented by Maulana Azad, while Jinnah represented the Muslim League.
            - Mahatma Gandhi also advised the Viceroy during the talks.
        - Reactions to the Wavell Plan
            - Muslim League's Response
                - Feared loss of representation if other minority groups (like Sikhs, Christians, and Depressed Classes) aligned with the Congress.
                - Demanded that all Muslim members in the Executive Council be nominated by the League.
            - Congress's Response
                - Perceived the plan as an attempt to limit Congress’s influence, seeing it as a measure to make Congress solely a caste Hindu party.
                - Asserted its right to nominate representatives from all communities.
            

- Indian National Movement (1945 - 1947)
    - Political Negotiations and Developments
        - The Release of Congress Leaders (Post-1944)
            - Post-World War II, the British sought to establish a coalition government in India. (Wavell Plan 1945)
            - Congress leaders were released from prison in May 1944 on grounds of ill health.
            - Public enthusiasm for these leaders signaled renewed resolve to fight for independence.
        - Elections in Britain (1945)
            - The Labour Party, sympathetic to India, won the July 1945 elections.
            - Clement Attlee became Prime Minister, replacing Winston Churchill.
            - The Labour Party manifesto highlighted promoting India's self-government within the Commonwealth.
            - In August 1945, elections for India’s central and provincial legislatures were announced.
            - Viceroy Wavell indicated in September that following the election, Government would intend to
                - Hold Negotiations for establishing a Constitution making body.
                - Form an Executive Council with support from the major political parties.
        - Election in India (1945 & 1946)
            - Muslim League Manifesto
                - Asserted Muslims of India are One Nation.
                - Emphasized Pakistan as the solution to India's constitutional problem.
            - Congress Manifesto
                - Advocated for Indian unity and opposed partition.
                - Raised public issues like Police excesses during QIM and INA Trials.
            - Election Results (1946): The Elections to Central Legislative Assembly were held in December 1945 and the provincial elections were held in January 1946.
                - Congress dominated the elections, winning 91.3% of non-Muslim votes and 57/102 seats in the Central Legislative Assembly.
                - Muslim League secured 86.6% of Muslim votes, winning 30/30 Muslim seats in the Assembly and 402/509 Muslim seats in provinces.
                - Congress formed ministries in 8 provinces (Majority everywhere except Sindh, Punjab and Bengal), while the Muslim League led in Sindh and Bengal.
                    - In Punjab, Congress, Union Party and Akalis formed a coalition Government.
        - Popular Uprising - INA Trials (1945-46)
            - Importance and Impact of INA Trials
                - Widespread Political Support
                    - All major political parties, including Congress, Akalis, Communist Party of India, Hindu Mahasabha, Justice Party, Muslim League, RSS, and Unionist Party.

    - British India - Judicial System

    - British India - Civil Services
        - Overview
            - The Indian Civil Services (ICS) were established to maintain law and order, facilitate governance, and manage trade in British India, evolving over time to meet administrative demands.
        - Evolution of Civil Services
            - Formative Stage (1675–1765)
                - The Covenanted Civil Service emerged, separating commercial agents from military and naval officers. Members managed coastal trade stations under a signed "covenant," earning the title "covenanted civil servants."
            - Transition to Administrative Service (1765–1853)
                - Regulating Act of 1773: Separated civil and commercial functions, banning private trade and bribery among civil servants.
                - Pitt’s India Act of 1784: Set recruitment age limits, empowered the Crown to remove corrupt officials, and under Lord Cornwallis (Father of Civil Services in India) introduced two divisions: Covenanted Service (higher posts for Europeans) and Uncovenanted Service (lower posts open to Indians).
                - Charter Act of 1793: Filled vacancies from existing company civil servants.
                - Reforms Under Lord Wellesley (1800): Founded Fort William College in 1800 to train civil servants in Oriental culture, law, and administration, replaced by Haileybury Institution in England in 1806 (abolished in 1857).
                - Charter Act of 1833: Proposed competitive exams for recruitment, introducing open competition (implemented in 1853).
                - Charter Act of 1853: Allowed Indians into the Covenanted Service via open competition. The Macaulay Committee of 1854 established the Civil Service Commission in London in 1855 to oversee examinations.
            - Imperial Civil Services (1858–1917)
                - Indian Civil Service Act of 1861: Made any Indian or European resident in India for seven years eligible to qualify.
                - Statutory Civil Service (1879): Reserved 20% of covenanted posts for Indians from prominent families, nominated by local governments, following 1870 British Parliament approval of Indian appointments.
                - Major Commissions and Reforms
                    - Aitchison Commission (1886): Proposed abolishing "covenanted civil service," recommending classification into Imperial, Provincial, and Subordinate Services for broader Indian inclusion.
                    - Islington Commission (1912): Suggested reserving 25% of posts for Indians, categorizing services into Class I and Class II under the Government of India.
                    - 1919 Act: Divided services into All India, Central, and Provincial categories, proposing a Public Service Commission (exams began in India from 1922). The Lee Commission (1923) further refined this structure.
                    - 1935 Act: Introduced provincial autonomy, retaining only Indian Civil Service, Indian Police Service, and Indian Medical Service as All India Services, while others became provincial. Mandated Public Service Commissions for provinces and the federation.

        - Law Enforcement Under the Mughals

            - Warren Hastings’ Reforms (1774)

            - 1775

- Economic Impact of British Rule
    - Village Conditions
        - Demographics
            - In the mid-18th century, approximately 90% of India’s population lived in villages.
        - Primary Occupation
            - Agriculture dominated village life, with landowners and tenants growing crops primarily for self-consumption. Other occupations included artisans, village officials, and menial laborers.
        - Social Structure
            - Traditional customs governed rent, wages, and prices within villages. Sir Charles Metcalfe called them “Little Republics,” emphasizing their autonomy and self-sufficiency, as they produced nearly everything their inhabitants needed.

    - Urban Conditions
        - Demographics
            - Urban dwellers made up about 10% of the population.
        - Types of Urban Centers
            - Religious Centers: Towns like Gaya and Banaras thrived as prominent pilgrimage sites.
            - Administrative Capitals: Cities such as Delhi, Agra, and Lucknow served as provincial capitals.
            - Commercial Hubs: Surat and Hubli emerged as key trading and port centers.

    - Agriculture
        - Characteristics
            - Farmers grew crops mainly for sustenance, sharing surplus with village artisans. Agricultural growth was constrained by dependence on monsoons.
        - Crops
            - Food crops included rice, wheat, bajra, jowar, and minor cereals. Commercial crops comprised jute, raw cotton, and groundnut.
        - Land Revenue System
            - Land revenue was a major income source for the state. Zamindars collected taxes and managed local judicial and police duties. The revenue-sharing system split crops between peasants and the state, with the state typically taking one-third.

    - Trade in Pre-British India
        - Nature of Trade
            - India had extensive domestic and international trade, linked to Asia and Europe through land and maritime routes like the Silk Road.
        - Exported Items
            - Cotton textiles, raw silk, indigo, opium, rice, wheat, sugar, pepper, spices, precious stones, and drugs.
        - Imported Items
            - Pearls, wool, dried fruits, and rosewater from Persia and the Gulf; coffee and gold from Arabia; tea and sugar from China; metals and paper from Europe.
        - Key Ports
            - Surat, Cambay, Calicut, and Masulipatnam were vital trade centers.
        - Trade Balance
            - India enjoyed a favorable trade balance, exporting more than it imported.
        - Local Business Communities
            - Hindu, Jain, and Bohra merchants in Gujarat; Khatris and Lohanis in Punjab; Marwari Banias in Rajasthan.

        - Aurang: A permanent market or warehouse for storing and trading goods, used during Mughal and British rule.
        - Banian: Indian intermediaries or brokers who aided European traders in trade and finance.
        - Mirasidar: A hereditary landowner in South India, primarily under the Ryotwari system.
        - Hundi: A financial instrument similar to a bill of exchange, used for money transfers and trade credit.
        - Jotedar: Wealthy landholders in Bengal who controlled land and competed with zamindars in revenue collection.

        - Handicraft Industries
            - Contributed 6% to the 18th-century GDP.

            - A major industry with centers in Dhaka, Surat, Ahmedabad, and Lucknow. Famous products included muslins of Dhaka, brocades of Banaras, and chintzes from the Coromandel Coast.
        - Shipbuilding
            - India was a leading shipbuilder, with centers in Bombay, Surat, and Tuticorin. Indian ships were sturdier and cheaper than European ones, led by the Wadia Group in the 18th century.
        - Metallurgy and Other Industries
            - Wootz steel (high-grade metal) was globally prized. Other products included jute, paper, oils, and perfumes.

    - Significant Agricultural Changes
        - New Land Revenue Policies: Altered land ownership patterns.
        - Land Fragmentation: British policies caused deindustrialization, driving people into agriculture, leading to land subdivision and fragmentation.
        - Commercialization of Agriculture: Shifted focus to cash crops, affecting food security.
        - Entry of Middlemen: Intermediaries exploited farmers, increasing their vulnerability.

    - British Land Revenue Policies
        - Background
            - In 1765, the East India Company gained the Diwani of Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa, granting them revenue collection rights. Robert Clive secured these rights but relied on Nawab’s officials for indirect tax collection, who overtaxed peasants and pocketed profits, causing inefficiency.
            - Warren Hastings attempted reforms like five-year and annual settlements but achieved limited success.
            - Lord Cornwallis introduced the Permanent Settlement in 1793, redefining the landlord-tenant relationship.
        - Permanent Settlement (1793)
            - Introduced by Governor-General Cornwallis in 1790 as a decennial (10-year) settlement, made permanent in 1793, also known as the Zamindari System. Covered 19% of British India, mainly Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa.
            - Features
                - Hereditary Rights: Zamindars gained rights to sell or transfer land hereditarily.
                - Revenue Obligations: Zamindars paid 10/11th of the revenue to the government, keeping 1/11th for themselves.
                - ‘Sunset Law’: Set a fixed payment deadline; failure led to the auctioning of zamindari rights.
                - Landlord-Tenant Formalization: Zamindars were required to issue pattas (contracts) to tenants, specifying land area and rent.
            - Drawbacks
                - Impact on Zamindars: High revenue targets and strict deadlines often resulted in land loss, with many estates auctioned due to non-payment.
                - Impact on Peasants: Peasants faced oppression through property seizures, beatings, and evictions, often borrowing from moneylenders to pay rent, deepening indebtedness.
                - Impact on Agriculture: Absentee landlordism and neglect of land care caused agricultural stagnation, disconnecting revenue demands from productivity.
            - Absentee Landlordism
                - Zamindars delegated management to middlemen and moved to urban centers like Calcutta, reinforcing feudalism and widening the rich-poor gap.
        - Ryotwari System (1820)
            - Introduction
                - Developed by Thomas Munro in 1820, first implemented by Alexander Reed in 1792 in the Madras Presidency. Ryots (cultivators) were recognized as landowners, with families providing the primary labor force, allowing direct tax collection from them. Covered 51% of British India, including Assam, Coorg, Madras, and Bombay.
            - Features
                - Direct Tax Collection: Revenue was collected directly from ryots, bypassing intermediaries.
                - Ownership Rights: Ryots received ownership, occupancy, and transfer rights without landholding limits.
                - Flexible Revenue System: Revenue adjusted based on production levels.
                - Government Control over Barren Land: Such land could be cultivated, with revenue shared between cultivators and the state.
                - Periodic Revenue Revision: Revenue agreements could be revised as circumstances changed.
            - Drawbacks
                - Overestimated Revenue: Unrealistic targets overburdened cultivators.
                - Harsh Collection Methods: Oppressive techniques were frequently used.
                - Rise of Moneylenders: High rents pushed ryots into debt traps.
                - While it eliminated intermediaries like zamindars and aimed to simplify tax collection, it led to landlessness and rural indebtedness.
        - Mahalwari System (1822)
            - Introduction
                - Conceptualized by Holt Mackenzie in 1819 and implemented by R.M. Bird in 1822 in North West territories, revised by Governor-General William Bentinck in 1833. Known as the Modified Zamindari System, it blended features of Zamindari and Ryotwari systems. Land was organized into mahals (villages) overseen by village headmen, acting as revenue units with land distributed among individual peasants who collectively paid the government.
            - Features
                - Village-Based Revenue Collection: Village headmen collected revenue, emphasizing community administration.
                - Mahal as Revenue Units: Villages were grouped into mahals for revenue assessment.
                - Fixed State Share and Regular Revisions: Initially 50%, later raised to 66%.
                - Revenue Based on Net Produce: Calculated after deducting expenses and wages.
            - Drawbacks
                - Inaccurate Records: Collectors manipulated records, skewing assessments.
                - Exorbitant Tax Assessments: High taxes led to indebtedness and land sales.
                - Undermined Authority: Replaced traditional leaders with British-appointed village heads.

    - Deindustrialisation
        - Definition
            - Deindustrialization refers to the decline in manufacturing sector employment due to economic or political changes, evident under British rule as traditional industries weakened.
        - Causes Under British Rule
            - Import of machine-made British goods overwhelmed local production.
            - Loss of traditional Indian craft industries due to competition. Economists like R.C. Dutt and Madan Mohan Malaviya noted the flood of Manchester-made cotton cloth imports.
        - Reasons for Deindustrialization
            - Decline in Royal Patronage: Reduced demand for handmade crafts as artisans competed with machine-made goods.
            - Increase in Trade Imbalance: Raw materials exported to Britain were turned into finished goods sold back in India, replacing indigenous textiles. The Charter Act of 1813 enforced one-way free trade, taxing Indian goods while British imports entered freely.
            - Lack of Modern Industrialization: British policies neglected technological investment in Indian industries, leaving them uncompetitive.
            - Impact of Railways (Post-1853): Facilitated raw material extraction from rural areas and widespread distribution of British goods.
        - Outcomes of Deindustrialization
            - Ruralization: Artisans abandoned their trades and moved to villages, straining agriculture. Between 1881 and 1931, the farming population grew from 70 million to 100 million, while industrial workers dropped from 21 million to 13 million.
            - Collapse of Weavers and Artisans: Pre-1780s, artisans sold freely; post-1780s, gomasthas (East India Company agents) forced them to sell only to the British under the Khatbandi system, legally binding them to the Company.
            - Shift in India’s Trade Dynamics: In the 18th century, cotton cloth was 81% of exports; post-deindustrialization, exports shifted to raw materials like opium and indigo, with cotton cloth falling to 3.7%, while British imports surged, reversing trade trends.
            - Legislative and Policy Impact: 19th-century policies favored British-built ships, further weakening Indian shipping.
            
- Socio-Religious Reform Movements
    - Understanding Socio-Religious Reform Movements
        - Aim
            - To transform society and religion by addressing social evils and promoting enlightenment for overall improvement.
        - Key Issues Addressed
            - Women’s Emancipation: Abolition of practices like sati, infanticide, and child marriage, alongside advocacy for widow remarriage.
            - Caste Reform: Eradication of caste discrimination and untouchability to promote social equality.
            - Promotion of Education: Fostered societal enlightenment through widespread education initiatives.
            - Religious Reforms: Combated idolatry and polytheism, and countered exploitation by priests to purify religious practices.
        - Factors Leading to Socio-Religious Reforms
            - Contact with the West: Exposure to modern ideas of liberty, equality, and democracy influenced India’s emerging middle class.
            - Christian Missionary Efforts: The Charter Act of 1813 permitted missionaries to critique Indian religious practices, sparking reformist responses.
            - Education: Indian and European scholars fostered pride in India’s heritage, empowering reform movements through knowledge and awareness.
        - Reform Strategies and Approaches
            - Reforms from Within: Advocated self-initiated changes through raising societal awareness.
            - Reforms through Legislation: Sought legal solutions to social issues, though colonial support was often limited.
            - Reforms through Symbols of Change: Employed radical actions, such as those by the Derozians, to symbolize transformation.
            - Reforms through Social Work: Combined practical efforts with intellectual endeavors to effect change.

    - Types of Reformers
        - Revivalists: Aimed to restore religion to its original greatness, exemplified by movements like Wahabi and Deobandi.
        - Reformists: Reshaped society with modern ideas, as seen in organizations like Brahmo Samaj and the Aligarh movement.

    - Socio-Religious Reform Movements - Eastern India
        - Raja Ram Mohan Roy and Brahmo Samaj
            - Known as the Father of Modern India for his pioneering reform efforts.
            - Key Contributions
                - Authored “Tuhfat-ul-Muwahhidin” (A Gift to Monotheists) in Persian in 1805, promoting monotheistic ideas.
                - Founded Atmiya Sabha in 1815 to critique idolatry and polytheism, advocating monotheism and rational thought.
                - Established the Calcutta Unitarian Committee in 1823 with Dwarka Nath Tagore, uniting prominent Brahmins and European residents to promote Hindu social reforms.
                - Influenced the abolition of sati in 1829 with Governor-General William Bentinck’s support, drawing on Dayabhaga and Mitakshara legal schools, and championed widow rights and women’s education.
                - Supported the establishment of Hindu College in 1817 to advance education.
                - Founded Vedanta College in 1825 to blend Western and Indian sciences for a holistic education.
                - Translated Vedas and Upanishads into accessible languages and published Sambad Kaumudi (Bengali) and Mirat-ul-Akbar (Persian) to spread reformist ideas.
            - Brahmo Samaj (1828)
                - Focused on monotheism, social equality, and human dignity as core principles.
        - Evolution of Brahmo Samaj After Raja Ram Mohan Roy

            - Debendranath Tagore’s Contributions
                - Founded Tattvabodhini Sabha in 1839 to promote rationalism, focusing on India’s history and indigenous languages, continuing Roy’s legacy.
                - Worked to reform Hinduism internally by eliminating idolatry and irrational customs, while resisting Christian missionaries’ critiques and conversion efforts.
                - Expanded Brahmo Samaj’s influence beyond Bengal to regions like the United Provinces, Punjab, Bombay, and Madras.
            - Keshab Chandra Sen’s Role
                - A prominent reformer who joined Brahmo Samaj in the mid-19th century, bringing new directions.
                - Established the Tabernacle of New Dispensation in 1867 to integrate Hinduism, Islam, and Christianity into a unified spiritual framework.
                - Founded the Indian Reform Association in 1870 to address women’s rights, caste discrimination, and education.
                - His radical ideas, including support for inter-caste marriages and teachings from all religions, led to a split in 1866: Brahmo Samaj of India (led by Keshab Chandra Sen) and Adi Brahmo Samaj (led by Debendranath Tagore).
            - Sadharan Brahmo Samaj (1878)
                - Emerged after Keshab Chandra Sen conducted his underage daughter’s marriage using orthodox rituals, contradicting reformist principles.
                - Disgruntled members formed this group, focusing on original Brahmo ideals: faith in a singular supreme being, emphasis on reason and morality, and opposition to orthodox practices.

        - Henry Vivian Derozio and Young Bengal Movement
            - Derozio: An Anglo-Indian radical thinker and professor at Hindu College, Kolkata, who founded the Academic Association and championed free thought, liberty, equality, and fraternity.
            - Young Bengal Movement: Influenced by Western thinkers, it promoted Raja Ram Mohan Roy’s ideas, emphasizing education, press freedom, and justice.

                - Reformed Sanskrit education by admitting non-Brahmins to Sanskrit College in 1851.

- People’s Resistance Against British Rule (Revolts)
    - Meaning of People’s Resistance
        - Definition
            - The term "people" refers to diverse segments of Indian society adversely affected by foreign domination, including peasants, artisans, tribals, ruling classes (both active and dispossessed), military personnel (serving under the Company or demobilized from ex-rulers’ armies), and religious leaders (both Hindu and Muslim).
        - Purpose of Resistance
            - To safeguard their interests, these groups fought both independently and in collaboration with others against British rule.

    - Factors for People’s Uprisings Against the British
        - Economic and Social Grievances
            - Collapse of indigenous industries due to British economic policies.
            - Exploitation by non-tribal people like moneylenders and traders.
            - Discontent stemming from oppressive land policies.
        - Impact of East India Company’s Rule
            - Resentment from zamindars and artisans over lost privileges and livelihoods.
            - Erosion of traditional rights and ownership systems under Company governance.
        - Cultural Factors
            - Desire for autonomy within the Indian cultural and political framework.
            - Resistance to imposed cultural influences like Sanskritization, which threatened local identities.

    - Civil and Peasant Uprisings During British Rule (Pre-1857)
        - Sanyasi Revolt (1760s–1800)
            - Nature of Revolt
                - An uprising by Hindu Sanyasis and Muslim Fakirs in Eastern India, supported by rural impoverished communities and minor zamindars.
            - Causes
                - Religious Restrictions: British restrictions on pilgrimage and taxes on religious donations angered spiritual leaders.
                - Economic Hardship: The Bengal Famine of 1770 worsened conditions, fueling rebellion.
            - Major Areas
                - Purnea, Malda, Dhaka, Rangpur, Cooch Behar, and Murshidabad in Bengal Province.
            - Notable Leaders and Activities
                - Leaders included Bhawani Pathak, Majnu Shah, Chirag Ali, and Musa Shah, who launched surprise raids on British outposts, treasuries, and factories.
            - Outcome
                - Eventually suppressed after a prolonged struggle by British forces.
            - Significance
                - Inspired literary works like "Devi Chaudhurani" and "Anandamath" by Bankim Chandra Chatterjee, reflecting its cultural legacy.
        - Rangpur Dhing (1783)
            - Area of Revolt
                - Rangpur District of Bengal in 1783.
            - Nature of Revolt
                - A protest by peasants and zamindars against excessive revenue demands under the Ijaradari System (land auctioned to the highest bidder).
            - Leader
                - Kena Sarkar.
            - Outcome
                - Highlighted flaws in the Ijaradari System, contributing to reforms like the Permanent Settlement.
        - Paika Rebellion (1817)
            - Location
                - Odisha.
            - Causes
                - Resistance against British policies, particularly high land revenue demands.
            - Leaders
                - Bakshi Jagabandhu Bidyadhar and Mukunda Deva.
            - Outcome
                - Suppressed by 1818 but led to governance changes, including large remission of arrears, reduced assessments, and a new settlement on fixed tenures.
            - Additional Fact
                - In 2017, Odisha urged recognition as the First War of Independence, though the Indian Council of Historical Research (ICHR) disagreed.
            - Who Were Paikas?
                - Paikas were foot soldiers recruited since the 16th century by Odisha kings for martial services in exchange for hereditary rent-free lands.
        - Mysore Rebellion (1830)
            - Location
                - Mysore (Nagar District).
            - Causes
                - Exploitative taxation after the Wodeyar dynasty’s reinstatement and enforcement of subsidiary alliances, burdening peasants and lower societal sections.
            - Leader
                - Sardar Malla.
            - Outcome
                - Resulted in direct British administration of the region.
        - Kuka Movement (1840–1850)
            - Nature
                - Also known as the Namdhari Movement, it aimed at religious purification and political resistance against British rule.
            - Founder
                - Bhagat JawaharMal.
            - Leader
                - Baba Ram Singh, who emphasized military training and structured resistance; Kukas boycotted British goods, institutions, and laws.
            - Outcome
                - Suppressed by the British; Baba Ram Singh was deported to Rangoon.

    - Tribal Uprisings During British Rule
        - Overview
            - Tribes in the 18th century lived in isolation with distinct agricultural methods, traditions, and societal structures, mainly in forested lands encroached upon by the British, leading to recurrent uprisings.
            - 
        - Understanding the Evolution of Tribal Movements Under British Rule
            - First Phase (1795–1860)
                - Rise of the British Empire; leadership from traditional sections like zamindars whose privileges were undermined.
            - Second Phase (1860–1920)
                - Intensive colonialism and mercantilism impacted tribal economy, land, and forests; leadership emerged from lower tribal ranks.
            - Third Phase (1920–1947)
                - Tribals engaged in nationalist, agrarian, and separatist movements; leadership from educated tribal leaders and outsiders.
            - 
        - Plight of the Tribals Under Colonial Rule - Key Issues
            - Debt Spirals and Outsider Dominance
                - Traders and moneylenders offered loans for non-local goods, leading to dependency, high interest rates, and debt cycles.
            - Eroded Chieftaincy (From Leaders to Enforcers)
                - Tribal leaders reduced to enforcers of British policies, losing respect among their people.
            - The Agrarian Dilemma (Forced Settlements and Fallow Fields)
                - Forced settlement and farming under unsuitable conditions increased hardships.
            - Forests Under Siege (Land Now State Property of British)
                - Forest laws restricted access, impacting livelihoods, forcing relocation, and reducing autonomy.
            - Labour Traps (Plantations and Mines)
                - Recruitment for tea plantations and mining led to loss of mobility and freedom.
        - Indian Forest Act
            - Background
                - A key conflict source due to timber demand in the 1850s for railways (fuel and sleepers) and shipbuilding (Indian teak for British naval dominance).
            - Forest Policy (1864)
                - Established by Lord Dalhousie, declaring forests state property and enabling British control over tree-containing land.
            - Forest Act of 1878
                - Divided forests into Reserved (fully government-controlled for heavy commercial use), Protected (partly government-controlled), and Village Forests (controlled by nearby villages); tribes lost traditional rights like grazing and agriculture, receiving only limited privileges.
            - Forest Act of 1927
                - Did not acknowledge community rights; individuals or communities had to claim rights before a Forest Officer; traditional practices like shifting agriculture and collecting forest produce were made illegal.

    - Revolt by Mainland Indian Tribes
        - Pahariyas' Rebellion (1778)
            - Area
                - Rajmahal Hills (Jharkhand).
            - Cause
                - Protest against British disruption of their lifestyle.
            - Main Tactic
                - Engaged British in guerrilla warfare.
            - Result
                - British declared the Pahariya region “Daman-i-Koh” (forbidden territory), agreeing not to extend administration further.
        - Chuar Uprising (1798–1799)
            - Area
                - Midnapore and Bankura districts of Bengal.
            - Cause
                - Protest against British revenue policies.
            - Activities
                - Multiple waves between 1766 and 1798, led by Durjan Singh, a deprived zamindar; also known as the Revolt of Jungle Mahal; brutally suppressed by British.
        - Kol Revolt (1831)
            - Location
                - Chotanagpur.
            - Cause
                - Against land transfers to non-tribal settlers and oppressive British colonial policies.
            - Leader
                - Budho Bhagat, under whom rebels killed about a thousand perceived oppressors.
            - Outcome
                - Suppressed with large-scale military operations.
        - Ho and Munda Uprisings (1820–1837)
            - Location
                - Singhbhum and broader Chotanagpur region.
            - Cause
                - Resistance to British revenue systems and Bengali settlers.
            - Leadership
                - Raja of Parahat.
            - Activities
                - Ho Revolt persisted until 1827 when Ho tribals surrendered; in 1831, they rebelled again with Mundas, subdued by 1837.
        - Koli Uprising (1822–1829)
            - Location
                - Maharashtra (particularly Akola hills).
            - Cause
                - British interventions in tribal rights and exploitation by moneylenders and zamindars.
            - Leader
                - Ramji Bhangre.
            - Activities
                - Rebels targeted Baniya moneylenders, attacking and looting financial documents; British responded with military force.
        - Ramosi Uprisings (1820s–1830s)
            - Location
                - Western Ghats.
            - Cause
                - Protest against livelihood loss after Maratha territory annexation.
            - Leaders
                - Chittur Singh and Umaji Naik.
            - Activities
                - Armed attacks on British establishments; brutally suppressed by British.
        - Santhal Rebellion (1855–1856)
            - Area
                - Santhal Pargana (Jharkhand and West Bengal).
            - Causes
                - Encroachment by outsiders (Dikus) invading land rights, economic exploitation by zamindars, and loss of traditional lifestyle.
            - Leaders
                - Sidhu and Kanhu Murmu, who rallied Santhals, believing they were divinely chosen to end oppression and restore their way of life.
            - Tactics
                - Armed insurrection with traditional weapons.
            - Outcome
                - Suppressed by British; Santhal Pargana created as an administrative district in 1855 for some autonomy; the 1876 Santhal Parganas Tenancy Act prohibited land transfer to non-tribals.
        - Khond Uprisings (1837–1856)
            - Causes
                - Resentment against British control, cultural disruption, loss of territorial rights, abolition of the Meriah System (human sacrifices for fertility), and economic exploitation via taxes and moneylenders.
            - Regions
                - Hilly tracts of Odisha and Andhra Pradesh.
            - Leader
                - Chakra Bisoi, uniting tribes like Ghumusar and Kalahandi against British policies.
            - Methods
                - Used traditional weapons like tangi (axes), bows, arrows, and swords.
            - Outcome
                - Suppressed due to superior British military strength.
        - Koya Revolts (1803–1886)
            - Causes
                - Oppression by police and moneylenders, exploitation under British forest laws.
            - Notable Leaders
                - Tomma Sora (1879–1880 rebellion) and Raja Anantayyar.
            - Methods
                - Guerrilla tactics exploiting terrain.
            - Outcome
                - Suppressed by British forces.
        - Bhil Revolts
            - Causes
                - Famine, economic hardship, and British misgovernance, with revolts in 1817, 1825, 1831, 1846, and 1913.
            - Regions
                - Western Ghats, southern Rajasthan (Banswara district), and Dhule district (Maharashtra), strategically linking central India to the western coast.
            - Notable Events
                - Sindwa Treasury Loot (1857): Around 500 Bhils, led by Kazi Singh, plundered the treasury.
                - Mangarh Massacre (1913) (Adivasi Jallianwala): Bhils, inspired by Govind Guru, protested peacefully for land and rights; British killed over 1,500, including women and children.
            - Methods
                - Guerrilla warfare and exploitation of mountainous terrain for plundering state treasures.
            - Outcome
                - Revolts suppressed by British.
        - Munda Rebellion (1899–1900) (Ulgulan, meaning Uprising)
            - Region
                - Chotanagpur region (Jharkhand).
            - Causes
                - Loss of tribal lands under the Khuntkatti System (collective ownership), seized by non-tribal settlers (Dikus) becoming jagirdars and zamindars, forcing Mundas into landless labor; protected forests deprived forest rights; exploitation via beth begari (forced labor) and missionary interference.
            - Leader
                - Birsa Munda, who declared himself a messenger of God, started the Birsait faith, blending Hindu-Christian elements into Munda ideology.
            - Methods
                - Transformed into the “Sardari Fight,” a political and religious campaign to establish Munda Raj.
            - Outcome
                - Birsa’s arrest and death in 1900 ended the movement; the Chotanagpur Tenancy Act (1908) recognized Khuntkatti rights, banned begari, and restricted land transfer to non-tribals.

    - Tribal Uprisings of North East
        - Ahom Revolt (1828)
            - Causes
                - British refusal to leave Assam after the 1826 Treaty of Yandaboo, contrary to promises post-Anglo-Burmese War.
            - Regions
                - Sibsagar district, Assam.
            - Leaders
                - Gomadhar Konwar and Dhanjay Borgohain.
            - Outcome
                - Suppressed by British; leaders executed.
        - Anglo-Khasi War (1829–1833)
            - Causes
                - Khasi resistance to British colonization.
            - Leader
                - Tirath Singh, who organized an armed revolt.
            - Outcome
                - Tirath Singh captured and exiled to Dhaka.
        - Singhpo Rebellions (1830, 1843)
            - Regions
                - Arunachal Pradesh.
            - Leaders
                - Bom Singhpo (1830): Attacked British armory but was captured.
                - Beesa Gaum (1843): Led armed resistance but sentenced to life imprisonment.
        - Tai Khamti Resistance (1839–1844)
            - Location
                - Arunachal Pradesh.
            - Causes
                - Fear of British-imposed taxes.
            - Leader
                - Chow Rang Pha Gohain.
            - Outcome
                - British defeated the Khamtis and asserted control over Arunachal Pradesh.

    - Some Important Mutinies During British Rule
        - Vellore Mutiny (1806)
            - Date & Location
                - 10 July 1806, Vellore Fort, Tamil Nadu.
            - Nature
                - Early armed revolt by Indian sepoys against British rule.
            - Causes
                - Cultural Interference: British banned religious symbols (e.g., caste marks) and forced sepoys to wear European hats.
                - Resentment: Anger over racial discrimination, poor treatment, and sympathy for Tipu Sultan’s family imprisoned in Vellore Fort.
            - The Revolt
                - Sepoys killed 14 British officers, unfurled Tipu Sultan’s flag; around 1,500 soldiers participated.
            - Outcome
                - Suppressed by British reinforcements under Colonel Gillespie; 800 sepoys killed, many executed or punished; Tipu Sultan’s family relocated to Calcutta.
            - Significance
                - A precursor to the Indian Rebellion of 1857, highlighting early resistance to British cultural and political dominance.

                
- People’s Resistance Against British Rule (Movements)
    - Peasant Movement Post-1857
        - Overview
            - Post-1857 peasant movements showed greater organization, coordination, and political consciousness, forming unions to fight for rights and participating in political movements for legislative representation.
        - Indigo Revolt (1859–60)
            - Cause
                - Exploitation by British planters under Nij (planters forced workers on owned/rented lands) and Ryoti (peasants coerced into growing indigo, degrading soil and trapping them in loan cycles) systems.
            - Key Events
                - 1859: Digambar Biswas and Bishnu Biswas led Bengal peasants to refuse indigo cultivation.
                - 1860: "Nil Darpan" play by Dinabandhu Mitra and "Hindoo Patriot" newspaper highlighted peasant struggles.
            - Outcome
                - Formation of the Indigo Commission (1860) and passage of the Indigo Act (1862) restricted planter exploitation; cultivation shifted to Bihar and later declined due to German synthetic dyes.
        - Pabna Agrarian Leagues (1870–1880)
            - Overview
                - Location: East Bengal region.
            - Cause
                - Zamindars raised rents beyond legal limits and blocked tenant ownership rights, violating the 1859 Rent Act.
            - Key Leaders
                - Ishan Chandra Roy, Shambhu Nath Pal, and Khodi Mulla formed agrarian leagues to resist oppression.
            - Outcome
                - Government suppressed the movement but initiated an inquiry; resulted in the Bengal Tenancy Act of 1885, defining landlord-tenant rights.
        - Deccan Peasant Uprisings (1860s–1870s)
            - Causes
                - Harsh taxation under the Ryotwari System worsened by consecutive bad harvests, a crash in cotton prices post-American Civil War, a 50% rise in land revenue demand (1867), and moneylender exploitation.
            - Tactics
                - Social boycotts against moneylenders, escalating to agrarian riots targeting their properties and destroying debt receipts and documents.
            - Outcome
                - Passage of the Deccan Agriculturists Relief Act (1879), protecting peasants from excessive debts and exploitation.
        - Bijolia Peasant Movement (1897–1941)
            - Location
                - Bijolia, Mewar (feudal estate).
            - Causes
                - Heavy tax burden by jagirdars, including Talwar Bandhi Tax (successor tax) and Chouthan Tax (tax on human trade).
            - Key Leaders
                - Vijay Singh Pathik, Manakya Lal Verma, and Sadhu Sitaram Das.
            - Outcome
                - 1922 agreement reduced taxes, but unrest persisted until independence.
        - Kisan Sabha Movement (1919–1922)
            - Location
                - United Provinces.
            - Key Leaders
                - Gauri Shankar Mishra, Madan Mohan Malaviya, and Baba Ramchandra organized peasants under Kisan Sabhas; founded United Provinces Kisan Sabha in 1918, followed by Awadh Kisan Sabha in 1920.
            - Objectives
                - Abolish forced gifts (Nazrana), unpaid labor (Begar), and unjust evictions.
            - Outcome
                - Peasant demands incorporated into the Awadh Rent (Amendment) Act (1921).
        - Eka Movement (1921–1922)
            - Location
                - Hardoi, Bahraich, and Sitapur districts (UP).
            - Demands
                - Pay only recorded rent, resist land eviction, and refuse forced labor.
        - Moplah Uprising (1921)
            - Region
                - Malabar, Kerala.
            - Cause
                - 19th-century British legislation granted landlords (mostly upper-caste Hindus) exclusive land ownership, stripping Moplahs (Malayali Muslims) of traditional rights.
            - Activities
                - Sparked by the 1920 Non-Cooperation Movement; driven by Muslim clerics’ speeches and anti-British sentiment, Moplahs launched a violent uprising against British and Hindu landlords.
            - Outcome
                - Violent communal uprising suppressed by the Malabar Special Force.
        - Bardoli Satyagraha (1926–1932)
            - Leader
                - Vallabhbhai Patel, earning the title Sardar.
            - Cause
                - 30% land revenue hike in Surat, Gujarat.
            - Tactics
                - Peasants withheld taxes, formed worker camps, published Bardoli Patrika, and imposed social boycotts on opponents.
            - Outcome
                - Hike withdrawn, strengthening the non-cooperation movement.
        - All India Kisan Congress/Sabha (1936)
            - Formation
                - Established in Lucknow under Swami Sahajanand Saraswati, originating from Bihar Provincial Kisan Sabha; released a “Kisan Manifesto” and a periodical under Indulal Yagnik.
            - Objectives
                - Abolish the zamindari system, reduce land revenue, and transfer land to actual tillers.
            - Leaders
                - N.G. Ranga, R.M. Lohia, and others.
            - Evolution
                - Initially Gandhian, it gravitated toward the Socialist faction within the Indian National Congress.
        - Tebhaga Movement (1946)
            - Region
                - North Bengal.
            - Course of Action
                - In September 1946, Bengal Provincial Kisan Sabha initiated a mass movement to implement the Flood Commission’s Tebhaga recommendations, ensuring bargardars (sharecroppers) received two-thirds of the harvest; supported by communist cadres and urban student militias.
            - Outcome
                - Faded after the Bargardari Bill was introduced.

    - Revolutionary Movement of Indian Independence
        - Phases of the Revolutionary Movement
            - Phase 1: Pre-World War I
                - Focused on individual acts of defiance, inspired by religious and cultural symbols.
            - Phase 2: Post-World War I
                - Marked by coordinated group activities blending nationalism and socialism, influenced by the Russian Revolution.
        - Reasons Behind the Growth of Revolutionary Movement
            - Failure of Existing Political Trends
                - Moderate approaches’ inability to sway colonial authorities drove support for radical methods.
            - Inspiration from Indian Ideologues
                - Aurobindo Ghosh: Advocated militant nationalism via “Bande Mataram,” emphasizing spiritual revival, cultural renewal, and armed resistance.
                - Vinayak Savarkar: His book “Indian War of Independence” celebrated revolutionary martyrdom.
                - Swami Vivekananda: Teachings on spiritual resilience, cultural pride, and social reform indirectly inspired youth revolutionary mindset.
            - Influence of International Movements
                - Irish Nationalist Movement, Ethiopia’s 1896 defeat of Italy, and Japan’s 1905 victory over Russia showed colonial powers could be resisted.
            - Economic and Social Conditions in India
                - Widespread poverty, social disparity, and economic hardship fostered revolutionary sentiments.
            - Attitude of Colonial Rule
                - Exploitative and repressive British policies amplified resentment, fueling the movement.
        - Prominent Revolutionary Activities in India
            - Maharashtra
                - Vasudev Phadke
                    - Known as the “Father of Armed Indian Rebellion,” he organized insurgent activities post-Deccan Famine (1876–77), forming a 40-person band to raid and rob for rebellion funds.
                - Chapekar Brothers (Poona Conspiracy Case)
                    - Outraged by oppressive plague control measures during the 1897 Bubonic Plague in Bombay and Poona, they assassinated plague commissioner Rand and Lt. Ayrest, marking a significant post-1857 aggressive nationalist act.
                - VD Savarkar and Abhinav Bharat
                    - Formation: Founded Mitra Mela Secret Society, evolving into Abhinav Bharat in 1904; set up bomb-making centers in Nasik, Poona, and Bombay.
                    - Activities: Sent pistols from London, used in the Nasik Conspiracy Case to assassinate Nasik magistrate Jackson; his book “1857: First War of Independence” influenced revolutionaries like Bhagat Singh, Lala Hardyal, and Rash Behari Bose.
            - Bengal
                - Anushilan Samiti and Yugantar Group
                    - Origins of militant nationalism in early 20th-century Bengal via Anushilan Samiti, founded by Satishchandra Bose, Jatindranath Banerji, and others.
                    - Influential Publications
                        - Bhawani Mandir (Aurobindo Ghosh): Written during Bengal’s partition, inspired by Anandmath, used as evidence in the Alipore Bomb Case.
                        - Yugantar (Barindrakumar Ghosh): Advocated sedition to build a revolutionary mindset.
                    - Dacca Anushilan Samiti and Yugantar Split (1906)
                        - Split into Dacca Anushilan Samiti in East Bengal (led by Pulin Behari Das) and Yugantar as a Calcutta subgroup (led by Barindrakumar Ghosh and Bhupendranath Dutta).
                    - Notable Activities
                        - Muzaffarpur Murders (1908): Failed attempt to assassinate Judge Kingsford (from Yugantar trials) by Khudiram Bose and Prafulla Chaki; led to the Alipore Conspiracy Case (1908, aka Maniktala Bomb Conspiracy), arresting Aurobindo and Barindra Ghosh.
                        - Post-event, Bengal’s revolutionary movement decentralized but remained active, pressuring the appointment of an Indian law member in the Viceroy’s council.
            - Revolutionary Activities in Other Regions
                - Tirunelveli Conspiracy Case (1911)
                    - Vanchinathan Iyer assassinated Robert Ashe, the British officer who ordered shootings in Tirunelveli.
                - Delhi Conspiracy Case (1912)
                    - Rash Behari Bose and Sachin Sanyal attempted to assassinate Viceroy Hardinge in Delhi.
        - International Revolutionary Activities Before WW1
            - India House (London)
                - Established by Shyamji Krishna Varma in 1905 (a Maharashtra revolutionary who fled to England after the Rand Murder Case) to propagate revolutionary ideas via Indian Sociologist and offer scholarships for radical youth training.
                - Madan Lal Dhingra (1909)
                    - Assassinated Curzon Wyllie in London, becoming the first Indian revolutionary executed on foreign soil.
            - Ghadar Movement (USA)
                - Based in San Francisco, the Ghadar Party, formed in 1913 with Baba Sohan Singh Bhakhna as President and Lala Hardyal as General Secretary, published The Ghadar, advocating rebellion among Indian soldiers; comprised mainly ex-soldiers and peasants from Punjab seeking better opportunities in the USA.
                - Key Events
                    - Komagata Maru Incident and WW1 intensified revolutionary zeal; leaders like GD Kumar and Taraknath Das launched Swadesh Sevak and formed United India House in Seattle.
            - Berlin Committee (Germany)
                - Revolutionaries like Virendranath Chattopadhyay and Lala Hardyal formed the Berlin Committee (Indian Independence Committee), securing German support for uprisings in India.
                - Zimmerman Plan
                    - Junagatar Group of Bengal, with Bagha Jatin and Rashbehari Bose, planned to import German arms during WW1 for an all-India insurrection in Upper India (German Plot); aimed to seize Fort William and incite an armed forces mutiny, but failed due to an infiltrator (Balasore Betrayal).
            - Silk Letter Movement
                - Reshami Rumal Andolan collaborated with Deobandi leaders, the Ottoman Empire, Afghanistan, and Germany to destabilize British rule.
            - Provisional Indian Government (Kabul)
                - Led by Raja Mahendra Pratap Singh, Barkatullah, and others in 1915, aimed to recruit Indian soldiers and rally international support by establishing a provisional government in Kabul.
            - Singapore Mutiny (1915)
                - Muslim soldiers in Singapore rebelled against deployment to fight the Ottoman Empire; suppressed with executions.
        - Revolutionary Activities After World War I
            - Hindustan Republican Association (HRA)
                - Founded by Sachindranath Sanyal to unify revolutionary groups across India.
                - Kakori Conspiracy (1925)
                    - Train robbery to fund HRA activities; post-Kakori, it transformed into Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (HSRA) under Bhagat Singh and Chandrashekhar Azad, adopting socialism.
                - Notable Activities
                    - Saunders’ assassination avenging Lala Lajpat Rai’s death; bomb-throwing in the Central Legislative Assembly protesting civil liberty restrictions (Public Safety Bill & Trade Disputes Bill), using harmless bombs.
                    - Bhagat Singh formed Naujawan Bharat Sabha in 1926 and wrote “Why I am an Atheist” in prison.
            - Revolutionaries from Bengal
                - Chittagong Armoury Raid (1929)
                    - Surya Sen attempted to seize arms and inspire an uprising in Chittagong; Pritilata Wadedar and Kalpana Dutt participated, leading to severe British suppression.
                - Bina Das
                    - Attempted to assassinate Bengal Governor Stanley Jackson at a Calcutta University convocation.
        - Important Writings
            - Bandi Jeevan (A Life of Captivity)
                - Written by Sachindranath Sanyal, called the Bible of revolutionaries.
            - Pathar Dabi
                - By Sharatchandra Chatterjee.
        - Reasons for Decline of Revolutionary Activities
            - Government Repression
                - Stringent laws like the Defence of India Act (1915) and Rowlatt Act (1919) curbed activities.
            - Lack of Coordination and Unified Leadership
                - Fragmented efforts across regions hindered success.
            - Financial Constraints
                - Limited resources impacted sustainability.
            - Shift to Alternative Strategies
                - Rise of mass movements like Gandhi’s Non-Cooperation offered new resistance avenues.

    - Leftist Movement in India National Struggle
        - Origins and Influences
            - Emergence
                - Rooted in modern industrial expansion and inspired by socialist movements in Great Britain and Russia.
            - M.N. Roy's Role
                - Influenced by the Bolshevik Revolution, M.N. Roy founded the Communist Party of India (CPI) in exile in 1920 in Tashkent, Soviet Russia, with Communist International support.
            - Military School
                - Established in Tashkent to train Indian frontier tribes for resistance against British rule.
        - Key Events
            - Peshawar Conspiracy Case (1922)
                - Nature
                    - Trainees from Tashkent, upon returning to India, were arrested and tried for spreading communist ideas.
                - Leaders
                    - Mohammed Akbar and Gauhar Rahman Khan sentenced to rigorous imprisonment.
            - Formation of CPI in India (1925)
                - Kanpur Conference (1924)
                    - Marked a significant step toward forming an all-India communist party.
                - Key Figures
                    - Nalini Gupta, Mujaffar Ahmed, and Singaravelu (elected president).
                - Development
                    - By 1930, CPI affiliated with the Communist International and released its first publication, The Communist.
        - Workers’ and Peasants’ Parties (WPP)
            - Formation and Activities (1925–1929)
                - Nature
                    - Functioned within the Indian National Congress (INC) as CPI front organizations.
                - Prominent Regional Parties
                    - Labour Swaraj Party (Bengal, 1925).
                    - Kirti-Kisan Party (Punjab, 1926).
                    - Bombay Workers’ and Peasants’ Party (Maharashtra, 1927).
                    - WPPs in the United Province (1928).
            - Decline
                - Repression
                    - Severe government actions weakened the WPPs.
                - Isolation
                    - Following Communist International directives, CPI severed ties with INC, labeling it bourgeoisie, leading to WPP disintegration.
        - Reorganization of CPI (1934–1935)
            - Leadership of P.C. Joshi
                - Reorganized the Communist Party, forging alliances with socialists and INC; communists operated underground and within the Congress Socialist Party (CSP).
        - Formation of Congress Socialist Party (CSP)
            - Reason
                - Disillusionment with Gandhi’s methods led young Congress members to embrace Marxism.
            - Establishment
                - Formed in Bombay in October 1934 by Jayaprakash Narayan, Acharya Narendra Dev, and Minoo Masani.
            - Objectives
                - Promote socialism in India, land reforms, workers’ rights, abolition of princely states, women’s emancipation, and national independence.
        - Impact
            - Influence
                - Significantly shaped Indian politics, though repression and isolation reduced momentum, forcing alignment with broader nationalist efforts.

        - Early Initiatives by Individuals and Groups
            - Sasipada Banerjee (Bengal)

            - Influence

                - Limited adult males’ work hours in textile factories to 12 hours/day.

- Communalism in British India
    - Manifestation of Communalism in Significant Events During British Rule
        - Anti-Congress Appeal (1887)
            - Formation of United Indian Patriotic Association
                - Founded by Syed Ahmed Khan in 1887 to oppose the Indian National Congress.
            - Appeal to Muslims
                - Syed Ahmed Khan urged educated Muslims to avoid Congress, though some still joined.
        - Partition of Bengal (1905)
            - Objective
                - British divided Bengal to weaken Bengali nationalism by creating separate Hindu and Muslim regions, intensifying communal identities.
        - Formation of the Muslim League (1906)
            - British Support
                - Established in 1906 under British patronage.
            - Leadership
                - Led by elite figures like Aga Khan.
            - Purpose
                - Aimed to divert Muslim youth from Congress and lay the groundwork for Muslim separatism.
        - Separate Electorates (1909)
            - Introduction by Morley-Minto Reforms
                - The 1909 reforms institutionalized separate electorates in India.
            - Impact
                - Deepened communal divisions and weakened Congress’s role as a unified nationalist body.
        - The Khilafat Movement (1919–1924)
            - Focus on the Ottoman Caliphate
                - Aligned Muslim political interests with preserving the Caliphate, emphasizing religious identity over national unity.
            - Result
                - Heightened communalism in India.
        - Nehru Report (1928)
            - Proposal
                - Suggested a bicameral legislature without separate electorates.
            - Reaction
                - Alienated Jinnah from Congress, leading to his Fourteen Points advancing communal demands.
        - Towards Mass Communalism (Post-1937)
            - Challenges for the League
                - Despite securing the Fourteen Points, the Muslim League struggled with limited Muslim support.
            - Dual Strategy
                - Reduced membership fees and addressed socio-economic issues to broaden support.
                - Accused Congress ministries of favoring Hindus and discriminating against Muslims, deepening the Hindu-Muslim divide.
        - Pakistan Resolution (1940)
            - Demand for Pakistan
                - At its Lahore session, the Muslim League passed the Pakistan Resolution, calling for an independent Muslim state in Muslim-majority regions.
    - British Policies
        - Support for the League
            - Cripps’ Proposals (1942)
                - Legitimized the Pakistan demand by offering provincial autonomy, strengthening the League’s position.
            - Failure of the Cabinet Mission Plan (1946)
                - Rejected Pakistan, leading to heightened communal tensions.
            - Role in Partition
                - British backing of the League’s demands significantly influenced the partition process.
        - Direct Action Day (1946)
            - Trigger
                - Muslim League’s rejection of the Cabinet Mission Plan.
            - Outcome
                - On 16 August 1946, communal violence erupted in Calcutta, spreading across India and causing widespread riots.
    - Culmination of Communalism
        - Outcome
            - The growth of communalism during British rule culminated in the Two-Nation Theory, leading to the Partition of India.


- Development of Education During British India
    - Introduction
        - Background
            - Until the late 18th century, the English East India Company, focused on trade and profit, avoided educational initiatives; efforts were led by evangelists and missionaries.
    - Efforts of Evangelists
        - Missionary Objectives
            - Evangelical missionaries aimed to spread Christianity, attributing Indian social issues to religious practices.

                - Viewed Indian culture as less developed than British culture.
            - Educational Approach
                - Promoted English education for moral values and evangelization, prioritizing conversion over practical learning.

                - The East India Company opposed missionary support until 1813.

        - Emergence of Orientalism
            - Late 18th-century political and economic needs compelled the British to promote education; Orientalists favored pro-native education to avoid rejection of Western systems.
        - Orientalist Contributions
            - Focused on studying Asian cultures, ancient languages, literature, and philosophies.

    - The Charter Act of 1813
        - Significance
            - First instance of the East India Company taking educational responsibility, allocating one lakh rupees annually and permitting missionary educational and proselytizing activities.
        - Delays
            - Funds unutilized until 1823 when the General Committee of Public Instruction was established.
    - Committee of Public Instruction, 1823
        - Led By
            - Lord Macaulay.
        - Actions (1823–1833)
            - Dominated by Orientalists; established Sanskrit College, Calcutta (1824), and colleges in Agra and Delhi.
        - Orientalist vs Anglicist Debate
            - Divided into Orientalists (preserving Indian traditions and classical education) and Anglicists (advocating Western education and English medium).
    - Anglicist-Orientalist Controversy
        - Representation
            - Differed in mode and medium of education, reflecting broader views on India’s future and tradition vs modernity.
        - Proponents
            - Orientalists: Sir William Jones, Warren Hastings, James Prinsep emphasized Indian scriptures and languages like Sanskrit and Persian.
            - Anglicists: Thomas Macaulay, Charles Grant, William Wilberforce prioritized science, mathematics, and English education.
    - Macaulay’s Minute (1835)
        - Declaration
            - Established English education as the government’s objective.
        - Impacts
            - Abolished Persian as the court language, replaced by English.
            - Emphasis on Western education diminished traditional learning.
            - Introduced Downward Filtration Theory, educating higher classes for indirect benefits to lower classes.
    - Wood’s Dispatch (1854)
        - Title
            - Known as the Magna Carta of Indian Education.
        - Key Provisions
            - Advocated vernacular languages in primary schools and English for higher education.
            - Stressed secular education, vocational training, and women’s education.
            - Introduced grants-in-aid to private enterprises.
        - Results
            - Established the Department of Public Instruction (1855) and universities in Calcutta, Madras, and Bombay (1857).
    - Development of Education Under Crown Rule
        - Post-1858 Changes
            - Transition from Company to Crown rule centralized education.
        - 1870 Policy by Lord Mayo
            - Provincial governments controlled education with constraints: university legislation under the Government of India and education funded by assigned revenues, limiting provincial resources for primary and secondary education.
        - Hunter Commission (1882–83)
            - Purpose
                - Assessed Wood’s Dispatch implementation and evaluated state-sponsored primary and secondary education.
            - Recommendations
                - Strengthen primary education in vernacular languages.
                - Transfer primary education control to district and municipal boards.
                - Divide secondary education into literary (university preparation) and vocational (commercial careers) streams.
                - Emphasize female education and private engagement.
            - Outcome
                - Accepted in 1884, delegating primary education to local authorities; promoted private schools and established universities like Punjab (1882) and Allahabad (1887).
        - Raleigh Commission (1902)
            - Purpose
                - Regulated universities amid concerns of nationalist propaganda.
            - Focus
                - Current university state and future reform proposals.
            - Outcome
                - Led to the Indian Universities Act (1904).
        - Indian Universities Act (1904)
            - Reforms
                - Enhanced focus on study and research.
                - Reduced tenure for university governing members.
                - Instituted stricter inspections for affiliated colleges.
                - Introduced government veto power over university regulations.
        - Saddler University Commission (1917–19)
            - Scope
                - Comprehensive review from school to university education.
            - Recommendations
                - 12-year school course with a 3-year university degree system.
                - Establish separate boards for secondary and intermediate education.
                - Encourage female education, vocational training, and university centralization.
        - Education Under Dyarchy (1921–1937)
            - 1921 Reforms
                - Granted provincial governments more educational autonomy.
                - Primary Education Acts passed in Bombay (1923), Assam (1926), U.P. (1926), and Bengal (1930).
            - Secondary Education
                - Rapid expansion doubled school numbers by 1937.
            - Medium of Instruction
                - Promoted modern vernaculars.
        - Wardha Scheme of Basic Education (1937)
            - Proposed By
                - Mahatma Gandhi.
            - Focus
                - Craft-based, self-supporting education using the mother tongue as the medium.
            - Outcome
                - Supported by a national conference but halted due to World War II.
        - Sargent Plan of Education (1944)
            - Aim
                - Post-war educational reforms.
            - Provisions
                - Pre-primary (3–6 years) and compulsory elementary education (6–11 years).
                - High school categorized into academic and vocational streams.
                - 20-year plan to eradicate adult illiteracy.
                - Emphasis on teacher training and specialized education.
            - Impact
                - Became a guideline for future reforms despite political constraints.
