- Important terms -
Term Definition
Abscission In plants, the falling of leaves, flowers, fruits, or stems at the end of a growing season due to the formation of a two-layered zone of specialized cells (abscission zone), often triggered by the hormone ethylene.
Acclimatisation Physiological adjustment to a change in an environmental factor.
Acetylcholine Most common neurotransmitter that functions by binding to receptors and altering the permeability of the post-synaptic membrane to specific ions, either depolarizing or hyperpolarizing the membrane.
Acrosome An organelle at the tip of a sperm cell that helps the sperm penetrate the egg.
Actin A globular multifunctional protein that forms microfilaments.
Active Immunity Immunity resulting from antibodies formed by the human in response to an antigen.
Active Site Specific portion of an enzyme that attaches to the substrate by means of weak chemical bonds.
Active Transport The movement of a substance across the membrane against its concentration gradient with the expenditure of energy and specific transport proteins.
Adaptive Radiation The emergence of numerous species from a common ancestor introduced into an environment, presenting a diversity of new opportunities and problems.
Adolescence Period between childhood and puberty.
ADP Adenosine diphosphate, an important organic compound in metabolism and essential to the flow of energy in living cells.
Adrenaline A hormone produced by the medulla of the adrenal gland that increases glucose concentration in blood, raises blood pressure, and heartbeat.
AMP Adenosine monophosphate, also known as 5'-adenylic acid, a nucleotide used as a monomer in DNA and RNA, produced during ATP synthesis by combining two ADP molecules.
ATP Adenosine triphosphate, a nucleotide triphosphate used in cells as a coenzyme, often called the energy currency of a cell.
Aerobic Refers to the presence of oxygen.
AIDS Acquired Immuno Deficiency Syndrome, a sexual disease caused by HIV, characterized by a reduction of T-cells and appearance of secondary infections.
Aldosterone An adrenal hormone that acts on the distal tubules of the kidney to stimulate reabsorption of sodium (Na⁺) and passive flow of water from the filtrate.
Allele An alternative form of a gene.
Allopatric Speciation A mode of speciation induced when the ancestral population becomes segregated by a geographical barrier.
Allopolyploid A common type of polyploid species resulting from two different species interbreeding and combining their chromosomes.
Alveoli Small air sacs of the lungs through which gaseous exchange takes place between blood and air.
Amniocentesis A technique used for determining genetic abnormalities in a fetus by the presence of certain chemicals or defective fetal cells in the amniotic fluid.
Amphibious Organisms that can live on both land and in water.
Amnion The innermost membrane of extraembryonic membranes, enclosed by a fluid-filled sac in which the embryo is suspended.
Anabolism Set of metabolic pathways that form molecules from smaller units with energy input.
Anaerobic Refers to the absence of oxygen.
Angiosperm A flowering plant that forms seeds inside a protective chamber called an ovary.
Analogous Organs or parts similar in function or appearance but not in origin or development.
Anoxia A condition characterized by an absence of oxygen supply to an organ or tissue.
Antibiotic A substance synthesized by microorganisms that damages or kills other microorganisms, e.g., penicillin, streptomycin, terramycin, neomycin.
Antibody A protein produced by the body’s immune system when it detects foreign substances (antigens) in the blood or tissues.
Antigen A foreign substance (usually protein) that elicits the formation of specific antibodies within an organism.
Aorta The major artery in the blood-circulating system that sends blood to other body tissues.
Apical Dominance Concentration of growth at the tip of a plant shoot where a terminal bud partially inhibits axillary bud growth.
Apoptosis Programmed cell death triggered by signals activating a cascade of suicide proteins in cells destined to die.
Autosomes Chromosomes other than sex chromosomes; humans have 22 pairs, common in both males and females.
Bacteria Simplest, single-celled living organisms found everywhere, constituting a large domain of prokaryotic microorganisms.
Basal Metabolism Amount of energy required by an individual in the resting state for functions like breathing and blood circulation.
Biodiversity Hotspot A relatively small area with an exceptional concentration of species.
Biomass The dry weight of organic matter comprising a group of organisms in a particular habitat.
Biome A large naturally occurring community of flora and fauna occupying a major habitat, e.g., forest or tundra.
Biosphere An artificial structure enclosing a self-contained ecosystem or ecosystems.
Blastocyst A mammalian blastula in which some differentiation of cells has occurred.
Blastula An animal embryo at the early stage of development when it is a hollow ball of cells.
Blood Pressure The hydrostatic force that blood exerts against the wall of a vessel.
Blood Serum Blood plasma without clotting factors.
Blood Sugar The concentration of glucose in the blood, i.e., 100 mg/100 mL.
Blue Baby The appearance produced by some congenital heart defects.
Bundle of His A collection of heart muscle cells specialized for electrical conduction that transmits impulses from the AV node to the apex of the fascicular branches.
Carnivore An animal that eats other animals.
C3 Plant A plant that uses the Calvin cycle for initial steps to incorporate CO₂ into organic material, forming a three-carbon compound as the first stable intermediate.
C4 Plant A plant that prefaces the Calvin cycle with reactions that incorporate CO₂ into four-carbon compounds, supplying CO₂ for the Calvin cycle.
Callus A mass of unorganized parenchyma cells derived from plant tissue (explants) for use in biological research, covering plant wounds.
Calyx Sepals of a flower.
Carcinogen A chemical agent that causes cancer.
Cardiac Output The volume of blood pumped per minute by the left ventricle of the heart.
Casparian Strip A water-impermeable ring of wax around endodermal cells in plants that blocks the passive flow of water and solutes into the stele via cell walls.
Cell Cycle An ordered sequence of events in a dividing eukaryotic cell, involving G₀, G₁, S, G₂, and M phases.
Cell Differentiation The structural and functional divergence of cells as they become specialized during a multicellular organism’s development.
Cellular Respiration An efficient catabolic pathway for ATP production, consuming oxygen as a reactant along with organic fuel.
Cell Sap The solution of inorganic and some organic substances found in the vacuole of a plant cell.
Centriole A small cylindrical cell organelle near the nucleus in animal cells, involved in spindle fiber development during cell division.
Chiasmata The X-shaped, microscopically visible region representing homologous chromatids that have exchanged genetic material through crossing-over during meiosis.
Chromosomes Self-duplicating rod-shaped or thread-like structures of nucleic acid and protein in the nucleus, carrying genetic information in the form of genes.
Chyme Partially digested food after leaving the stomach.
Commensalism An association between two species where one benefits and the other is neither harmed nor benefited.
Conjunctiva The thin mucous membrane that lines the eyelids and the exposed surface of the eyeball.
Consumers Organisms (heterotrophs) incapable of synthesizing their own food, obtaining nourishment by consuming producers or other consumers.
Corpus Callosum A broad band of nerve fibers joining the two hemispheres of the brain.
Corpus Luteum A secreting tissue in the ovary formed from the collapsed follicle after ovulation, producing progesterone.
Cytokinesis The division of the cytoplasm to form two separate daughter cells immediately after mitosis.
Decomposers Organisms that obtain nutrients by feeding on dead organisms.
Deoxyribonucleic Acid (DNA) A double-helix structure consisting of two long chains held together by hydrogen bonds, present in all living organisms.
Desmosome A type of intercellular junction in animal cells that functions as an anchor.
Diabetes Mellitus Inability to metabolize carbohydrates and fats due to insulin deficiency, resulting in glucose excretion through urine.
Dialysis Separation of particles in a liquid based on their ability to pass through a membrane, used for clinical blood purification.
Dicotyledon A flowering plant with an embryo bearing two cotyledons.
Dihybrid A hybrid individual heterozygous for two genes.
Dimorphism Displaying two separate growth forms.
Diffusion Movement of materials from higher to lower concentration.
Ecotype A locally adapted variant of a species, a biological community of interacting organisms and their physical environment.
Embryo The fertilized egg of a vertebrate animal after cleavage.
Endometrium The inner lining of the uterus, richly supplied with blood vessels.
Epistasis The phenomenon where one gene alters the expression of another independently inherited gene.
Evolution The process by which different types of living organisms develop from earlier forms during Earth's history.
Fertilization The process of fusion of a male gamete with a female gamete, resulting in a zygote.
Fibrinogen A soluble protein in blood plasma from which fibrin is produced by thrombin action.
Gastrulation The formulation of a gastrula from a blastula.
Gene The unit of heredity, a segment of DNA in all living organisms or RNA in some RNA viruses.
Glycolysis An anaerobic process breaking down glucose molecules by enzymes, releasing energy and pyruvic acid.
Guttation Secretion of water droplets through hydathodes at the tip and margins of certain leaves.
Haemoglobin Iron-containing, red-colored respiratory pigment that transports oxygen in the blood of vertebrates.
Haploid An organism or structure with a single set of unpaired chromosomes.
Herbivore An organism that feeds on plants.
Heterotrophs Organisms that cannot derive food from complex organic materials.
Heterozygous Organisms with dissimilar alleles at corresponding chromosomal loci, producing dissimilar gametes (e.g., Rt, Tt, Ww).
Homeostasis The tendency to maintain stability in the internal environment without changing the external environment.
Homologous Structures or organs of various animals with similarity in basic structure and development but not necessarily in function.
Homozygous An individual with identical alleles at corresponding chromosomal loci (e.g., RR, rr, tt).
Human Genome Project An international effort to map and sequence the entire human genome.
Host An organism (plant, animal, or microbe) on which another organism (parasite) feeds.
Hydrolysis Breakdown of complex molecules into simpler ones using water.
Hydroponics The process of growing plants in sand, gravel, or aqueous solutions containing essential mineral elements, without soil.
Hymen A fold of mucous membrane that normally partially covers the vaginal opening.
Immunity The tendency of a body to be safe from getting a disease.
Insectivore An animal that eats mainly insects.
Karyogamy Fusion of nuclei of two cells as part of syngamy.
Karyotype A method of organizing chromosomes of a cell by number, size, and type.
Lactation Process of milk secretion by the mammary glands.
Lenticels Minute pores raised through the cork of a woody stem or root for gaseous exchange between the atmosphere and internal tissues.
Leukemia Blood cancer characterized by a rapid, abnormal increase in white blood cells, including many immature cells.
Lichen A symbiotic relationship between an alga and a fungus, often growing on rocks and tree trunks.
Meiocyte A cell that differentiates into a gamete through meiosis.
Meiosis A type of cell division resulting in four daughter cells with half the chromosomes of the parent cell, used in gamete and spore production.
Menarche The first occurrence of menstruation in girls.
Meninges Membranes enclosing the brain and spinal cord.
Mesosomes Special structures formed by infoldings of the bacterial membrane, increasing its surface area.
Metabolism Chemical processes in a living organism to maintain itself and carry out characteristic activities.
Metastasis Transfer of disease from one organ or body part to another not connected to it, significant in cancer.
Metamorphosis A complete change in shape and appearance of an organism during development, e.g., caterpillar to pupa.
Mitosis Type of cell division where chromosomes split into two daughter cells with the same number and kind of chromosomes as the parent nucleus.
Monocarpic A plant that flowers once in its life and then dies, e.g., century plant (Agave).
Monosomic A cell or individual that is basically diploid but has one copy of a particular chromosome (2n-1).

Migration Seasonal movement of animals from one region to another.
Monocotyledon A flowering plant with an embryo bearing a single cotyledon (seed leaf).
Mutualism A relationship between two or more species where both participants benefit.

- Biology: Diversity in Living World - Overview of Living Organisms - Definition: - All living things are identified and categorized based on their body forms and functions. - Some characteristics cause more significant changes in body design than others, influencing classification. - Systematics: - Definition: - The scientific study aimed at recognizing, describing, naming, and arranging diverse organisms according to an organized plan based on unique species and group features. - Components: - Identification: - Determining the correct name and position of an organism within an established classification plan. - Classification: - Arranging organisms into groups based on similarities and differences. - Nomenclature: - A system for naming organisms. - Taxonomy: - Framing rules for classification, with identification, classification, and nomenclature as foundational steps. - Classification - Definition: - Scientific arrangement of organisms in a hierarchical series of groups based on similarities and differences. - Basis of Classification: - Characteristics that distinguish certain groups of organisms from others, such as habitat, blood color, or reproductive methods (oviparous or viviparous). - Aristotle’s Contribution: - Known as the father of biology and zoology, Aristotle classified animals based on: - Habitat (land, water, air). - Blood color (red or not). - Reproduction (oviparous or viviparous). - Objectives of Classification: - Recognize and distribute basic units (species) based on pre-decided characteristics. - Devise criteria for assigning species into an ascending series of groups based on similarities and relationships. - Illustrate evolutionary changes in organisms. - History of Classification - Key Historical Developments: - Early classification into viviparous, oviparous, and minute animals. - Hippocrates and Aristotle grouped animals into birds, insects, fishes, and whales. - Pliny the Elder introduced artificial classification, listing 1000 economic plants in *Historia Naturalis*. - John Ray described over 18,000 plants and animals in *Historia Generalis Plantarum* and introduced the term “species” for morphologically similar organisms. - Carolus Linnaeus: - Developed the binomial system of nomenclature. - Described 5,900 plant species in *Species Plantarum* and 4,326 animal species in *Systema Naturae* and *Philosophia Botanica*. - Classified organisms into two kingdoms: Plantae and Animalia in *Systema Naturae*. - Known as the Father of Taxonomy for creating a uniform system for naming plants and animals. - Evolution of Systematics: - From Aristotle to Linnaeus, classification evolved from classical taxonomy to natural systems, numerical taxonomy (phenetics), and phylogenetic classification (cladistics). - Hierarchy of Classification - - Definition: - A hierarchical system introduced by Linnaeus, later expanded by biologists like Ernst Haeckel (1894), Robert Whittaker (1958), and Carl Woese (1977), to categorize organisms into broad kingdoms. - Major Categories: - Kingdom (Highest) - Phylum (for animals) / Division (for plants) - Class - Order - Family - Genus - Species (Lowest) - Basis: - Organisms are separated from larger, broader groups to smaller, specific groups, with species as the basic unit of classification. - Binomial System of Nomenclature - Definition: - A scientific naming system devised by Carolus Linnaeus, where each organism is given a two-word name: - First word: - Generic name (genus). - Second word: - Specific name (species). - Example: - \( \text{Canis familiaris} \) for dog. - Examples of Common and Scientific Names: -

- Viruses - Definition: - Term coined by Pasteur; discovered by Ivanowski in tobacco plants with mosaic disease. - Recovered in crystalline form by Stanley (Nobel Prize, 1946). - Studied in virology; intracellular parasites lacking enzymes for energy generation. - Characteristics: - Considered non-living outside hosts but act as a link between living and non-living. - Dependent on hosts for survival; exist as crystals outside hosts. - Cause dangerous infections when inside hosts. - Composed of ~94% protein, ~6% nucleic acid (DNA or RNA, single/double-stranded) covered by a protein coat (capsid) made of capsomeres. - Size: - 10–300 mµ; smallest virus: 20 mµ; largest: pox virus (300 mµ). - Shapes: - Isodiametric, spherical, polygonal, anisodiametric, rod-shaped, or thread-like. - Bacteriophages: - Bacteria-infecting viruses discovered by Frederick Twort and Felix d’Herelle. - Kingdom Monera - Definition: - Proposed by Ernst Haeckel (1866); includes small, simple, microscopic prokaryotes (e.g., Archaebacteria, Eubacteria). - Mostly unicellular, lacking a true nucleus and organelles. - General Characteristics: - Unicellular, without membrane-bound nucleus. - Reproduce asexually via binary fission or sporulation. - Nutrition: - Autotrophic (synthesize own food) or heterotrophic (depend on others). - Eubacteria (True Bacteria): - Lack a true nucleus; include bacteria, cyanobacteria (blue-green algae), mycoplasma, actinomycetes, rickettsia. - Bacteria: - Discovered by Antony van Leeuwenhoek (1683); named by Ehrenberg (1829). - Unicellular, ubiquitous, found in water, soil, organisms; vary in shape and size. - Shapes: - Coccus (spherical/oval), bacillus (rod), spirillum (spirally coiled), vibrio (comma-shaped), stalked, budding, mycelial (filamentous). - General Characteristics of Bacteria: - Thick cell wall (chitin) and inner membrane (proteins, phospholipids). - Nutrition: - Autotrophic (photosynthetic/chemosynthetic) or heterotrophic (saprophytic, symbiotic, parasitic). - Reproduction: - Asexual (binary fission); parasexual (conjugation, transformation). - Movement: - Some have flagella for rhythmic movement. - Respiration: - Via mesosomes. - Examples: - Methanogenic bacteria (e.g., Methanococcus produce methane from formic acid); halophilic bacteria (e.g., Halococcus thrive in salt-rich media). - Spore-producing bacteria: - Gram-positive, purple on staining, cell walls composed of murein. - Mycoplasma: - Smallest living cells, lack cell wall, aerobic, non-motile, pleomorphic, prokaryotic, contain RNA and DNA. - Known as PPLO (pleuropneumonia-like organisms). - Economic Importance of Bacteria: -

include methanogens (e.g., Methanobacterium), halophiles (e.g., Halobacterium), thermoacidophiles (e.g., Sulfolobus). - Kingdom Protista - Definition: - Proposed by Ernst Haeckel (1886); includes aquatic, unicellular, eukaryotic organisms, evolved ~1000 million years ago. - Major Groups: - Photosynthetic Protists: - Unicellular algae (e.g., diatoms, euglenoids). - Protozoan Protists: - Holozoic, saprozoic, or parasitic (e.g., Trypanosoma, Plasmodium, Paramecium). - Consumer-Decomposer Protists: - Lack chlorophyll, feed on decaying matter (e.g., slime molds). - General Characteristics of Protozoa: - Size: - 10–52 µm, up to 1 mm. - Movement: - Via flagella (5–10 µm), cilia (20–30 µm), or pseudopodia (2–20 µm thick). - Digestion: - Intracellular in vacuoles. - Nutrition: - Saprophytic, parasitic, symbiotic, or holozoic. - Respiration/Excretion: - Via diffusion through body surface. - Reproduction: - Asexual and sexual. - Examples: - Amoeba, E. coli, Leishmania donovani, Plasmodium, Euglena. - General Characteristics of Protista: - Cell membrane: - Plasmalemma. - Cell wall: - Cellulose, protein strips (pellicle), or silica. - Organelles: - Mitochondria, Golgi body, endoplasmic reticulum, nucleus, chromosomes. - Ribosomes: - 80S. - Chloroplasts: - Present in photosynthetic forms (e.g., dinoflagellates). - Reproduction: - Sexual and asexual (binary fission). - Locomotion: - Pseudopodial (e.g., Amoeba), flagellar, ciliary; flagella/cilia have 9+2 fibrils. - Nutrition: - Autotrophic, heterotrophic, or both. - Notes: - Cellulose-digesting protists found in termites and wood-eating animals. - Gonyaulax causes red tide. - Diatoms (e.g., Navicula) glide toward light. - Pathogenic protists (e.g., Amoeba, Plasmodium, Trypanosoma) cause diarrhea, malaria, African sleeping sickness. - Economic Importance of Protista: -

- Kingdom Fungi - Definition: - Primitive, eukaryotic thallophytes (lacking roots, stems, leaves); unicellular or multicellular; definite cell wall, true nucleus, no chlorophyll. - Types Based on Nutrition: - Saprophytic: - Feed on decayed plants/animals, organic residues (e.g., Rhizopus, Penicillium). - Parasitic: - Feed on living organisms (e.g., Puccinia, Ustilago). - Symbiotic: - Mutualistic associations (e.g., lichens with alga and fungus). - General Characteristics: - Structure: - Body of hyphae (tubular filaments) forming mycelium. - Cell Wall: - Chitin or fungal cellulose. - Food Reserve: - Glycogen and oil. - Reproduction: - Sexual: - Fusion of gametes or nuclei. - Asexual: - Via spores (e.g., Mucor, Rhizopus). - Vegetative: - Fragmentation, budding, fission. - Examples: - Yeast, mushrooms, Aspergillus, Penicillium, Rhizopus, Agaricus. - Economic Importance of Fungi: -

Plant Physiology - Definition: - Study of physio-chemical activities in plants, including transportation, mineral nutrition, photosynthesis, respiration, growth, development, and reproduction. - Plant-Water Relations: - Importance: - Water is essential for all physiological activities in plants. - Functions of Water: - Transports nutrients from soil to plant. - Major component of protoplasm (water with molecules/particles). - Acts as a solvent and coolant. - Varies in amount: e.g., watermelon (92% water), woody plants (less water). - Essential for seed survival and respiration, despite appearing dry. - Limits growth and productivity in agriculture due to high water demand. - Terms Related to Plant-Water Relations: - Water Potential: - Defined by Slayter and Taylor (1966) as the difference in free energy of water molecules in pure water versus other systems. - Pure water has the highest water potential, affected by solute and pressure potentials. - Solute Potential: - Reduction in water potential due to solutes in pure water. - Pressure Potential: - Pressure in an osmotic system due to water entry/exit. - Processes: - Imbibition: - Water absorption by solid particles (imbibant) without forming a solution, causing swelling. - Examples: - Wood swelling in water, seed coat rupture during germination. - Everyday Science: - Wooden doors/windows hard to open/close in rainy season due to imbibition. - Osmosis: - Movement of water from high to low concentration through a semi-permeable membrane (SPM), discovered by Pfeffer. - Affected by solute concentration. - Examples: - Hypotonic Solution: - High external water concentration, cell gains water via endosmosis, becomes turgid (e.g., raisin in water swells). - Isotonic Solution: - Equal water concentration inside/outside, no size change. - Hypertonic Solution: - Low external water concentration, cell loses water via exosmosis, becomes flaccid (e.g., raisin in salt solution shrinks). - Everyday Science: - Excess fertilizer without water causes plasmolysis via exosmosis, killing plants. - Plasmolysis: - Shrinkage of plant cell due to water loss via exosmosis. - Deplasmolysis: Swelling of plasmolyzed cells in hypotonic solution/distilled water. - Means of Transport: - Diffusion: - Movement of ions/molecules (solid, liquid, gas) from high to low concentration without energy. - Active Transport: - Uses ATP to move ions/molecules from low to high concentration. - Transport System in Plants: - Overview: - Flowering plants lack a circulatory system but transport materials via complex mechanisms. - Water absorbed by roots reaches all plant parts, including growing tips, for photosynthesis and growth. - Short-distance transport (e.g., minerals, organic nutrients, growth regulators) via cytoplasmic streaming/diffusion. - Long-distance transport (translocation) of water via xylem, food via phloem. - Transport of Water: - Involves diffusion, osmosis, and potential gradients for short distances (cell-to-cell, across membranes). - Long-distance transport involves root pressure and transpirational pull. - Processes of Water Loss: - Transpiration: - Evaporative loss of 90–95% of absorbed water as vapor, mainly via leaf stomata. - Stomata: - Tiny pores on leaf surfaces for water vapor diffusion and gas exchange (O₂, CO₂). - Guard cells regulate stomatal opening/closing via exosmosis/endosmosis. - Guttation: - Loss of liquid water drops via hydathodes (pores) on leaf margins/vein ends when root pressure is high and transpiration is low. - Occurs in early morning in herbaceous plants (e.g., rose, strawberry, tomato). - Water contains organic/inorganic substances. - Transport of Minerals: - Absorbed by roots, transported upward via xylem with water. - Multidirectional, targeting growing regions (apical/lateral meristems, young leaves, flowers, fruits, seeds, storage organs). - Remobilization from old to young parts. - Translocation of Organic Solutes: - Organic compounds (synthesized in leaves via photosynthesis) transported as starch solution via phloem. - Multidirectional to all plant parts. - Photosynthesis: - Definition: - Enzyme-regulated anabolic process synthesizing organic compounds from CO₂ and H₂O using sunlight, storing energy as sugars (carbohydrates). - Oxygen is a waste product. - Equation: - \( 6CO_2 + 12H_2O \xrightarrow[\text{enzymes, radiant energy (686 kcal)}]{\text{chlorophyll}} C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2 \uparrow + 6H_2O \) - Primarily occurs in leaves. - Process: - Oxidation-reduction process: - O₂ evolved from H₂O dissociation, sugars/starch synthesized from CO₂. - Initiated by light absorption in chlorophyll-containing reaction centers. - Two Phases: - Photochemical Phase (Light/Hill Reaction): - Occurs in thylakoids (grana region), light-dependent. - Involves photolysis of water, producing assimilatory power (NADPH, ATP). - Electrons from water photolysis picked up by P680 (photosystem-II reaction center), follow non-cyclic or cyclic photophosphorylation. - Biosynthetic Phase (Dark/Blackman’s Reaction): - Occurs in chloroplast stroma, does not require light but uses ATP and NADPH from light reaction. - Pathways: - Calvin cycle (C3), Hatch-Slack cycle (C4), Crassulacean Acid Metabolism (CAM). - Factors Affecting Photosynthesis: - Carbon Dioxide: - Up to 0.05% concentration increases rate; beyond this, no further effect. - Light: - Intensity, quality, duration affect rate. - Low intensity reduces rate; higher intensity increases it. - Effective in blue-violet (470–500 nm) and red (650–760 nm) spectrum; violet least effective. - Red light favors carbohydrate accumulation, blue light stimulates protein synthesis. - Visible light (380–760 nm) used; artificial light possible. - Water: - Small amount used in photosynthesis; scarcity reduces rate, closes stomata, limits CO₂ availability, causes wilting. - Temperature: - Affects dark reaction, not light reaction. - Optimal: - 10–25°C for C3 plants, 30–45°C for C4 plants. - Oxygen: - Small amount essential; high concentrations reduce rate (Warburg effect). - Plant Growth and Development: - Overview: - Initiates from a zygote, forming all structures (roots, stems, leaves, flowers, fruits, seeds) in an orderly sequence. - Growth is indeterminate (unlimited) due to continuous meristematic cell division (open growth). - Phases: - Cell division, cell enlargement, cell maturation. - Conditions/Factors for Growth: - Supply of nutrients. - C/N ratio (carbohydrates to nitrogenous compounds regulates growth patterns). - Temperature. - Water. - Light. - Oxygen. - Plant Growth Hormones (Phytohormones): - Definition: - Small, simple organic molecules produced naturally in higher plants, controlling growth and physiological functions in small amounts. - Functions: - Promotes cell elongation, longitudinal growth. - Stimulates cambium cell division. - Enhances root growth/initiation. - Prevents abscission of leaves/fruits. - Induces parthenocarpy (seedless fruit formation). - Inhibits flowering generally, but promotes uniform flowering in pineapple. - Gibberellins: - Discovered by Kurosawa (1926); GA3 (gibberellic acid) most studied. - Functions: - Promotes stem elongation. - Reverses dwarfism. - Breaks dormancy in potato tubers, promotes seed germination (lettuce, cereals). - Induces parthenocarpy in apple, pear. - Controls flowering in long-day plants. - Regulates fruit growth/development. - - Reproduction - Definition: - Biological process by which organisms produce offspring, ensuring species continuity through a cycle of birth, growth, and death. - Types of Reproduction: - Asexual Reproduction: - Definition: - Single parent produces offspring without gamete formation, offspring are exact copies (clones) of the parent. - Characteristics: - Uniparental, involves only one parent. - Occurs via mitotic division of somatic cells (somatogenic reproduction). - No gamete formation or fusion. - Offspring are genetically identical to parents (clones). - Examples: - Plants, protozoans, protists, sponges, coelenterates, Planaria. - Note: - Absent in higher non-vertebrates and all vertebrates. - Sexual Reproduction: - Definition: - Involves two parents of opposite sex, with fusion of male and female gametes. - Characteristics: - Biparental, involves male and female. - Meiotic division during gamete formation, mitotic division during development. - Offspring are genetically different from parents. - Slower process compared to asexual reproduction. - Examples: - Higher plants, animals. - Events in Sexual Reproduction: - Pre-Fertilization Events: - Gametogenesis: Formation of gametes. - Homogametes/Isogametes: - Male and female gametes similar in appearance. - Heterogametes: - Male and female gametes morphologically distinct. - Copulation/Gamete Transfer: - Close association of male and female for gamete fusion. - Fertilization (Syngamy): - Fusion of male and female gametes to form a diploid zygote. - Types: - External Fertilization: - Occurs outside the body, e.g., aquatic organisms (algae, fish, amphibians). - Internal Fertilization: - Occurs inside the body, e.g., fungi, higher animals, most plants (bryophytes, pteridophytes, gymnosperms, angiosperms). - Post-Fertilization Events: - Zygote formation and embryogenesis (embryo development from zygote). - Animal Categories Based on Zygote Development: - Oviparous: - Zygote develops outside female body, eggs laid with hard calcareous shell, e.g., reptiles, birds. - Everyday Science: - King Cobra builds nests, lays 20–40 eggs, guards them during 60–90 day incubation. - Viviparous: - Zygote develops inside female body, young delivered after growth, e.g., humans, monkeys, most mammals (except monotremes like platypus, echidna). - Note: - Viviparous animals have higher offspring survival due to embryonic care/protection. - - Human Respiratory System - - Overview: - Respiration is the process of releasing energy from food molecules through oxidation, producing ATP (energy currency), CO₂, and water as byproducts. - Involves five steps: 1. Intake of oxygen from the environment. 2. Stepwise oxidation of food using oxygen. 3. Elimination of CO₂ (byproduct). 4. Release of energy during oxidation. 5. Storage of energy as ATP. - Types of Respiration: - Aerobic Respiration: - Involves molecular oxygen intake and CO₂ release; performed by aerobes. - Types: - Direct Respiration: - Exchange of O₂ and CO₂ without respiratory organs or blood, e.g., aerobic bacteria, protists, plants, sponges, coelenterates, flatworms, roundworms, most arthropods. - Indirect Respiration: - Uses specialized organs (skin, buccopharyngeal cells, gills, lungs, blood), e.g., crustaceans, amphibians, annelids, molluscs, reptiles, birds, mammals. - Phases of Indirect Aerobic Respiration: - Breathing: - Physical process of air movement in/out of the body (not synonymous with respiration). - External Respiration: - O₂ intake by blood from air/water in respiratory organs; CO₂ removal. - Internal Respiration (Cellular Respiration): - O₂ uptake by tissue cells from blood via tissue fluid. - Food oxidation in cells, producing energy, water, CO₂. - Energy storage as ATP phosphate bonds. - CO₂ release into blood via tissue fluid. - Anaerobic Respiration (Fermentation): - Energy production without oxygen; performed by anaerobes. - Examples: - Yeast: - Glucose → Ethanol + CO₂ + Energy (C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂). - Bacteria: - Glucose → Lactic acid + Energy (C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2CH₃CHOHCOOH). - Flow Chart of Glucose Oxidation: - Glucose → Glycolysis (in cytoplasm) → Pyruvate + ATP - With O₂ (mitochondria): CO₂ + H₂O + Energy. - Without O₂ (human muscle): Lactic acid. - Without O₂ (yeast): Ethanol + CO₂. - - Mendel’s Laws of Inheritance: - Derived from monohybrid and dihybrid crosses via crossing and pollination. - Laws: 1. Law of Dominance: - When two alternative alleles are present, one (dominant) expresses in F1, while the other (recessive) is masked; e.g., tallness (T) dominant over dwarfness (t). 2. Law of Segregation (Purity of Gametes): - Alleles don’t blend; both traits recover in F2, though recessive is hidden in F1. Each gamete carries one allele. - Example Monohybrid Cross: - Parents: TT (tall) × tt (dwarf) → F1: Tt (all tall). - F1 selfing → F2: TT, Tt, Tt, tt (3 tall: 1 dwarf, phenotypic ratio). - Proves dominance (T expressed in Tt) and segregation (Tt gametes carry T or t). 3. Law of Independent Assortment: - Genes for different traits segregate independently during gamete formation. - Example Dihybrid Cross: - Parents: YYRR (yellow round) × yyrr (green wrinkled) → F1: YyRr (all yellow round). - F1 selfing → F2: 9 yellow round, 3 green round, 3 yellow wrinkled, 1 green wrinkled (9:3:3:1 phenotypic ratio; genotypic 1:2:2:4:1:2:1:2:1). - Shows independent segregation of seed color and shape genes. - Key Terms: - Gene: - Heredity unit, coined by W. Johannsen (1909); Mendel called it “factor.” - Phenotype: - Morphological appearance of an individual. - Genotype: - Genetic constitution of an individual. - Alleles: - Alternate forms of a gene at the same locus on homologous chromosomes. - Back Cross: - F1 hybrid crossed with either parent. - Test Cross: - F1 hybrid crossed with recessive parent. - Exceptions to Mendelism: 1. Incomplete Dominance: - F1 hybrid phenotype is intermediate between parents, not resembling either; e.g., snapdragon, four o’clock plant (red × white → pink flowers due to partial red dominance). 2. Codominance: - Both alleles express independently in heterozygotes; e.g., ABO blood groups (IAIB → AB blood type, both A and B antigens expressed). 3. Multiple Allelism: - More than two alleles for a gene in a population; e.g., ABO blood groups (IA, IB, i alleles). - ABO Genotypes: -

Maximum electrons in a shell = 2n². - K (n=1) → 2 electrons - L (n=2) → 8 electrons - M (n=3) → 18 electrons - N (n=4) → 32 electrons - Subshells (s, p, d, f, g, h …) - Each shell is divided into subshells. - Subshell type depends on azimuthal quantum number (l). - s → l = 0 - p → l = 1 - d → l = 2 - f → l = 3 - g → l = 4 - h → l = 5 (theoretical, very high energy, not needed in normal atoms). - Orbitals - Each subshell contains orbitals (specific regions where electrons are found). - Each orbital holds max 2 electrons (with opposite spins). - Distribution: - s → 1 orbital → 2 e⁻ - p → 3 orbitals → 6 e⁻ - d → 5 orbitals → 10 e⁻ - f → 7 orbitals → 14 e⁻ - g → 9 orbitals → 18 e⁻ - h → 11 orbitals → 22 e⁻ - Shell + Subshell Relation - K (n=1) → 1s → 2 e⁻ - L (n=2) → 2s, 2p → 8 e⁻ - M (n=3) → 3s, 3p, 3d → 18 e⁻ - N (n=4) → 4s, 4p, 4d, 4f → 32 e⁻ - Filling Order (Aufbau Principle) - Electrons do not always fill strictly by shell, but by increasing energy of orbitals. - General filling sequence: 1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p → 5s → 4d → 5p → 6s → 4f → 5d → 6p → 7s → 5f → 6d → … -
