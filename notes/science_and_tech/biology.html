This genomic instability is a hallmark of cancer. - Somatic mutations: - Random mutations can occur in any body cell during a person's lifetime. While most of these are harmless, an accumulation can sometimes lead to disease. - Cells with no DNA - Mature red blood cells: - As they mature, red blood cells in mammals expel their nucleus and mitochondria to maximize space for hemoglobin. As a result, they contain no nuclear or mitochondrial DNA. - Platelets: - These cell fragments, which aid in blood clotting, also lack a nucleus and DNA. - Dead skin, hair, and nail cells: - These cells are dead and have no functional organelles or DNA. - Cells with different sets of DNA - Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA): - Nearly every cell in your body (except mature red blood cells) contains a small amount of DNA within its mitochondria. This mtDNA is inherited exclusively from your mother and is separate from the nuclear DNA. - The primary function of mtDNA is to code for 37 genes that are essential for the production of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the cell's main energy currency. These genes are vital for the oxidative phosphorylation process that powers most cellular activity. - The genes on mtDNA produce 13 proteins involved in cellular respiration, along with 2 ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs) and 22 transfer RNAs (tRNAs) required for protein synthesis within the mitochondria. - Because mtDNA is inherited almost exclusively from the mother, it provides a powerful tool for tracing female ancestral lines. Genetic researchers use this to study human migration patterns, trace population movements, and investigate ancient DNA. - Chimeras: - In rare cases, an individual can have cells from two or more genetically distinct populations. A common example is feto-maternal microchimerism, where a mother and fetus exchange a small number of cells during pregnancy.
