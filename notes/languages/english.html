- English Alphabet - The English alphabet consists of 26 letters, divided into vowels and consonants. - Vowels: There are 5 vowels in the English alphabet: - A, E, I, O, U - Vowels are the letters that represent vocal sounds. - In English, vowels play a crucial role in word formation and pronunciation. They can have both short sounds (like “a” in “cat”) and long sounds (like “a” in “cake”). - Sometimes Y is treated as a vowel when it functions as one, such as in words like “cry” or “gym.” - Consonants: - The remaining 21 letters are consonants: - B, C, D, F, G, H, J, K, L, M, N, P, Q, R, S, T, V, W, X, Y, Z - Consonants are sounds produced by obstructing the flow of air, and these sounds are usually combined with vowels to form syllables and words. - Syllables - A syllable is a unit of sound that typically contains a vowel sound. It can also include consonants before or after the vowel. Every word can have one or more syllables. - One syllable word: cat, dog, sun - Two syllable word: table, water, pencil - Three syllable word: beautiful, elephant, important - Parts of Speech - Detailed Explanation - Nouns - Definition: - A noun is a word that names a person, place, thing, or idea. - Types of Nouns: - Common Nouns: - General names for people, places, or things (e.g., dog, city, car). - Proper Nouns: - Specific names of people, places, or things (e.g., John, Paris, Coca-Cola). - Abstract Nouns: - Name ideas, qualities, or feelings (e.g., love, freedom, beauty). - Concrete Nouns: - Can be touched or seen (e.g., table, house, apple). - Collective Nouns: - Refer to a group of people, animals, or things (e.g., family, team, flock). - Countable and Uncountable Nouns: - Countable Nouns can be counted (e.g., books, pens). - Uncountable Nouns cannot be counted (e.g., water, milk, air). - Rules: - Plural Form: - Usually, nouns are made plural by adding -s (e.g., books), but some irregular nouns change form (e.g., child → children). - Possessive Form: - To show possession, add an apostrophe (') and an s (e.g., John's book). - Pronouns - Definition: - A pronoun is a word that takes the place of a noun to avoid repetition. - Types of Pronouns: - Personal Pronouns: - Represent specific people or things (e.g., I, he, she, it, we, they). - Possessive Pronouns: - Show ownership (e.g., mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs). - Reflexive Pronouns: - Reflect the action back to the subject (e.g., myself, yourself, himself, herself). - Demonstrative Pronouns: - Point to specific things (e.g., this, that, these, those). - Interrogative Pronouns: - Used to ask questions (e.g., who, whom, what, which). - Indefinite Pronouns: - Refer to non-specific things (e.g., anyone, everyone, someone, none). - Rules: - Pronouns must agree in number and gender with the noun they replace. - E.g., The girl lost her book. (“Her” agrees with “girl.”) - Reflexive pronouns are used when the subject and object are the same. - E.g., She taught herself. - Verbs - Definition: - A verb is a word that expresses an action, occurrence, or state of being. - Types of Verbs: - Action Verbs: - Show physical or mental action (e.g., run, eat, think). - Linking Verbs: - Connect the subject to the complement (e.g., am, is, are, was, were). - Auxiliary (Helping) Verbs: - Help the main verb (e.g., is, have, will, can). - Modal Verbs: - Show possibility, necessity, or ability (e.g., can, could, should, may). - Transitive Verbs: - Need an object to complete the meaning (e.g., She eats an apple). - Intransitive Verbs: - Do not require an object (e.g., He sleeps). - Rules: - Subject-Verb Agreement: - The verb must agree in number with the subject. - E.g., He runs (singular) vs. They run (plural). - Tenses: - Verbs change form based on time (present, past, future). (More on tenses below). - Adjectives - Definition: - An adjective is a word that describes or modifies a noun or pronoun. - Types of Adjectives: - Descriptive Adjectives: - Describe qualities of a noun (e.g., big, blue, happy). - Quantitative Adjectives: - Show how much or how many (e.g., few, many, some). - Demonstrative Adjectives: - Point to specific things (e.g., this, that, these, those). - Demonstrative pronouns vs Demonstrative adjectives - Demonstrative pronouns stand alone. - Example: - This is delicious. - Those were mine. - Demonstrative adjectives modify a noun and are followed by a noun directly. - Examples: - This book is interesting. - Those shoes are expensive. - Possessive Adjectives: - Show ownership (e.g., my, your, his). - Possessive pronouns vs Possessive adjectives - Possessive pronouns stand alone. - Example: - This is delicious. - Those were mine. - Possessive adjectives modify a noun and are followed by a noun directly. - Examples: - This book is interesting. - Those shoes are expensive. - Rules: - Order of Adjectives (When you use multiple adjectives): - 1. Quantity/Number → 2. Opinion → 3. Size → 4. Age → 5. Shape → 6. Color → 7. Proper Adjective → 8. Purpose/Qualifier. - E.g., She has two beautiful big brown dogs. - Comparative and Superlative Forms: - For most adjectives, add -er for comparative (e.g., smaller) and -est for superlative (e.g., smallest). - For adjectives ending in -y, change -y to -ier or -iest (e.g., happier, happiest). - Adverbs - Definition: - An adverb is a word that modifies or describes a verb, adjective, or another adverb. - Types of Adverbs: - Adverbs of Manner: - Describe how something is done (e.g., quickly, happily). - Adverbs of Time: - Describe when something happens (e.g., now, yesterday). - Adverbs of Place: - Describe where something happens (e.g., here, there). - Adverbs of Frequency: - Describe how often something happens (e.g., always, never). - Adverbs of Degree: - Describe the intensity of the action (e.g., very, quite, too). - Rules: - Adverbs often end in -ly (e.g., quickly, carefully), but not always (e.g., always, never). - Adverbs usually come after the verb or before the adjective/adverb they modify. - E.g., She sings beautifully. (after verb) - E.g., She is very tall. (before adjective) - Prepositions - Definition: - Prepositions are words that show the relationship between a noun (or pronoun) and another word in the sentence. - Common Prepositions: - Place: - in, on, at, under, above, between, next to. - Time: - at, on, in, before, after. - Direction: - to, from, towards, into, through. - Rules: - Prepositions are typically followed by a noun or pronoun. - E.g., The book is on the table. (on + noun) - E.g., She walked towards me. (towards + pronoun) - Prepositional Phrases: - A preposition + its object (a noun/pronoun) form a prepositional phrase. - E.g., on the table, under the bed, in the box. - Conjunctions - Definition: - A conjunction is a word that connects words, phrases, clauses, or sentences. Conjunctions help to build a relationship between different parts of a sentence. - Types of Conjunctions: - Coordinating Conjunctions: - These connect words, phrases, or clauses that are of equal importance. - The main coordinating conjunctions are FANBOYS: - For (because) - And (adds one idea to another) - Nor (negation of either of two) - But (contrasts two ideas) - Or (gives an alternative) - Yet (shows contrast, like “but”) - So (indicates a consequence) - Examples: - She likes tea and coffee. - He went to the store, but it was closed. - Subordinating Conjunctions: - These connect a dependent (subordinate) clause with an independent clause. - Common subordinating conjunctions: - because, since, if, when, although, while, unless, as. - Examples: - Because I was late, I missed the bus. - He went to the party although he was tired. - Correlative Conjunctions: - These work in pairs to join equal elements in a sentence. - Both…and, Either…or, Neither…nor, Not only…but also, Whether…or. - Examples: - Both my brother and my sister like ice cream. - Either you can go or stay. - Rules: - Conjunctions are used to connect ideas. - Coordinating conjunctions join elements that are equal in importance (e.g., two nouns, two adjectives, two independent clauses). - Subordinating conjunctions are used to connect a dependent clause with an independent clause, indicating a relationship like cause, contrast, condition, etc. - Correlative conjunctions should be used in pairs to connect two equal elements in a sentence. - Interjections - Definition: - An interjection is a word or phrase used to express strong emotions or sudden feelings. Interjections are often followed by an exclamation mark. - Examples: - Wow! That's amazing! - Oh no! I missed the bus. - Hooray! We won the match! - Ouch! That hurt! - Rules: - Interjections are used to convey emotions, surprise, or exclamation. - They are not grammatically connected to the sentence structure. - They can stand alone or be followed by an exclamation mark. - Articles - Definition: - An article is a word that defines a noun as specific or unspecific. - There are two types of articles: - definite and indefinite. - Types of Articles: - Definite Article - The - Usage: - Refers to a specific or known item. - Examples: - The book on the table is mine. (Refers to a specific book that both the speaker and listener know about.) - She gave me the pen. (Refers to a specific pen, known to both.) - Indefinite Articles - A and An - Usage: - Used when the noun is unspecified or mentioned for the first time. - A is used before words that start with a consonant sound. - An is used before words that start with a vowel sound. - Examples: - I saw a car on the road. (Any car, not specific.) - She is an engineer. (Refers to any engineer, not specific.) - Rules: - Use “the” when the noun is specific or previously mentioned. - Use “a” or “an” for general or non-specific items. - “A” is used before words beginning with a consonant sound. - “An” is used before words beginning with a vowel sound (e.g., an apple, an hour). - Do not use articles with uncountable nouns or plural nouns in a general sense. - E.g., She loves music (uncountable), Books are useful (plural). - Sentence Structure - Basic Sentence Structure: - In English, sentences follow a basic Subject + Verb + Object (SVO) structure: - Subject (S): - The person or thing performing the action. - Verb (V): - The action or state of being. - Object (O): - The person or thing receiving the action. - Exceptions: - In questions, the word order changes: - Auxiliary Verb + Subject + Main Verb. - Example: - Do you like chocolate? (Here, “do” is the auxiliary verb.) - Examples: - She (S) reads (V) books (O). - They (S) play (V) football (O). - Complex Sentences: - A complex sentence contains an independent clause (main clause) and one or more dependent clauses. - Independent Clause: - A complete thought that can stand alone as a sentence. - Dependent Clause: - Cannot stand alone and depends on the independent clause for meaning. - Examples: - I stayed home because it was raining. - “I stayed home” is the independent clause. - “because it was raining” is the dependent clause. - Rules: - In complex sentences, the independent clause can usually stand alone, but the dependent clause cannot. - Subordinating conjunctions (e.g., because, although, if) are used to connect the dependent and independent clauses. - Punctuation: - Use a comma before the conjunction if the dependent clause comes first. - E.g., If it rains, we will stay home. - Tenses - Tenses are used to express when an action or state of being occurs. English tenses are divided into three main categories - Present, Past, and Future. Each category is further divided into Simple, Continuous (Progressive), Perfect, and Perfect Continuous forms. - Present Tense - Simple Present Tense: - Use: - Used to describe habitual actions, general truths, facts, and routines. - Rule: - The base form of the verb is used (for “he”, “she”, “it”, add -s or -es). - Examples: - I eat breakfast every day. (habit) - The Earth revolves around the Sun. (fact) - She works at a bank. (routine) - Exception: - Use “do/does” for questions and negations: - I do not like coffee. - Does she live here? - Present Continuous Tense: - Use: - Action that is happening right now or around the current moment. - It is temporary, ongoing, or in progress. - Rule: - am/is/are + verb + -ing. - Examples: - I am eating lunch right now. (action happening now) - She is studying for her exams. (temporary action) - Exception: - Some verbs (stative verbs) do not normally appear in the continuous form (e.g., know, like, love): - I know her very well. (Not “I am knowing her.”) - Present Perfect Tense: - Use: - Used to describe actions that were completed at some unspecified time before now, or actions that started in the past and are still relevant in the present. - Rule: - has/have + past participle. - Examples: - I have finished my homework. (action completed before now) - She has lived here for five years. (action started in the past and continues) - Present Perfect Continuous Tense: - Use: - Used to describe actions that started in the past and are still continuing or were happening recently and have just finished. - Emphasizes duration (for/since), ongoing effort, or repetition over time. - This is the main difference between present continuos and present perfect continuos - Rule: - has/have + been + verb + -ing. - Examples: - I have been studying for two hours. (action started in the past and is still continuing) - She has been working at the company for 10 years. (ongoing action) - Past Tense - Simple Past Tense: - Use: - Used to describe actions that were completed at a specific time in the past. - Rule: - Regular verbs end in -ed; irregular verbs have unique past forms. - Examples: - I visited Paris last summer. (specific time in the past) - She studied for the exam yesterday. (action completed) - Past Continuous Tense: - Use: - Used to describe actions that were happening at a particular moment in the past or to show that an action was interrupted by another event. - Rule: - was/were + verb + -ing. - Examples: - I was reading a book when the phone rang. (interrupted action) - She was working all day yesterday. (ongoing action at a specific time in the past) - Past Perfect Tense: - Use: - Used to describe actions that were completed before another action in the past. - Shows the "past of the past" — two events, and this one happened first. - This is the main difference between present perfect and past perfect - Rule: - had + past participle. - Examples: - I had already eaten when she arrived. (completed before another action) - She had finished the project before the deadline. - Past Perfect Continuous Tense: - Use: - Used to describe actions that were ongoing in the past up until a certain point. - Rule: - had + been + verb + -ing. - Examples: - I had been studying for two hours when you called. (action ongoing until a certain point in the past) - She had been working at the company for five years before she left. - Future Tense - Simple Future Tense: - Use: - Used to describe actions that will happen at a specific point in the future. - Rule: - will/shall + base form of the verb. - Examples: - I will visit the museum tomorrow. - She will go to college next year. - Use of “Will” and “Shall” in Future Tenses: - “Will” is generally used for all subjects (I, you, he, she, etc.) to indicate future actions. - “Shall” is more formal and often used for the first person (I/we) in formal speech, but in modern English, “will” is usually preferred. - Future Continuous Tense: - Use: - Used to describe actions that will be happening at a specific time in the future. - Rule: - will be + verb + -ing. - Examples: - I will be traveling to New York at this time tomorrow. (action happening at a particular future time) - She will be meeting her friends next weekend. - Future Perfect Tense: - Use: - Used to describe actions that will be completed before a specific point in the future. - Rule: - will have + past participle. - Examples: - I will have finished my homework by 5 PM tomorrow. (completed before a future time) - By next year, they will have completed the project. - Future Perfect Continuous Tense: - Use: - Used to describe actions that will be ongoing up until a certain point in the future. - Rule: - will have been + verb + -ing. - Examples: - By this time next year, I will have been studying for three years. (ongoing action in the future) - She will have been working at the company for 10 years by the time she retires. - Punctuation - Common Punctuation Marks: - Full stop (.): - Used to mark the end of a declarative sentence. - Comma (,): - Used to separate items in a list or clauses in a sentence. - Question mark (?): - Used at the end of a direct question. - Exclamation mark (!): - Used to express strong emotion or surprise. - Apostrophe ('): - Shows possession or contraction. - Examples: - Sarah's book (possession) - Don't (contraction of do not) - Conditionals - Definition: - Conditionals are sentences expressing “if” or “when” something happens, or is imagined to happen. - Types of Conditionals: - Zero Conditional: - Describes general truths. - If + Present Simple, Present Simple. - Example: - If you heat water, it boils. - First Conditional: - Describes real and possible situations in the future. - If + Present Simple, will + base verb. - Example: - If it rains, I will stay home. - Second Conditional: - Describes hypothetical situations in the present or future. - If + Past Simple, would + base verb. - Example: - If I were rich, I would travel the world. - Third Conditional: - Describes hypothetical situations in the past. - If + Past Perfect, would have + past participle. - Example: - If I had studied harder, I would have passed the exam. - Common mistakes - When to use -s or -es? - Rule: - Use -s or -es with the verb only in Present Simple Tense when the subject is singular and third person (i.e., he, she, it, or a singular name). - Examples: - Where -s/-es is used: - She reads books. - He watches TV. - Ravi plays football. - The dog barks loudly. - Where -s is not used: - I read books. (I is first person) - You read books. (You is second person) - They read books. (They is third person but plural) - We read books. (We is plural) - Children play outside. (Children is plural) - Sometimes "a/an/the" are required while other times skipped? - When to use? - 1. Singular Countable Nouns → Always need an article or determiner: - Example: - She reads a book. - He saw an elephant. - I closed the door. - 2. "The” for something specific or known: - Example: - She found the key (we both know which key). - He entered the room (a specific room). - We used to go to the temple every Sunday. - Add "the" before "temple", "market", etc, because when talking about visiting a place for its function (like temple, school, hospital), you usually need an article. - 3. “A” or “An” for something non-specific or general: - Exammple: - I saw a dog (any dog, not a particular one). - He ate an apple (any apple). - When not to use? - 1. - Start every sentence with a capital letter. - Example: - She is reading a book. - Pronoun "I" is always capitalized. - Example: - My brother and I went to the market. - Days, months, names, and proper nouns (India, Sunday, Ravi) should be capitalized. - Example: - We will meet on Monday in Delhi with Ravi. - Common nouns (book, homework, temple) are not capitalized unless they start a sentence. - Example: - He placed the book on the table. - Usage of would/may/might - Would is used for expected result of condition, it shows high certainty - Example: - If I studied, I would pass. - May is used for possibility, there is a small or formal change that something could happen - Example: - If you apply early, you may get selected. - Might is used for uncertain or weaker possibility - Example: - If I had the money, I might buy a car. - Vocabulary -
