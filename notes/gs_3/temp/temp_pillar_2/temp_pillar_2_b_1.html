- Pillar 2B1: Finance Commission - Definition: - The Finance Commission is a constitutional and quasi-judicial body established under Article 280 of the Indian Constitution. - It is constituted by the President of India every five years (or earlier) to ensure fiscal federalism by recommending: - The distribution of net proceeds of taxes between the Union and State governments. - The principles governing grants-in-aid to the states from the Consolidated Fund of India. - Measures to augment the Consolidated Fund of a State to supplement the resources of Panchayats and Municipalities. - Its role is to address imbalances in taxation and expenditure responsibilities between the Centre and States, thus promoting equitable financial governance. - Context: - Fiscal federalism involves dividing taxation and expenditure responsibilities across government levels (Union, States, Local Bodies). States have significant expenditure responsibilities under the 7th Schedule but limited taxation powers, making the Finance Commission critical for equitable revenue sharing. - Significance for India: - The Finance Commission promotes fiscal equity, supports state development, and ensures financial stability by recommending tax devolution, grants, and fiscal discipline measures, aligning with India’s goals of cooperative federalism and balanced regional growth. - Structure: - Comprises a Chairman and four members, appointed by the President, eligible for re-appointment. Recommendations are advisory but rarely rejected by the government. - Functions: - Recommends vertical tax devolution (Union to States) and horizontal distribution (among States). - Suggests grants-in-aid to States for revenue deficits, local bodies, and disaster management. - Advises on augmenting State Consolidated Funds for Panchayats and Municipalities. - Addresses additional matters specified in Terms of Reference (ToR) by the President, such as fiscal discipline or specific state needs. - 15th vs. 16th Finance Commission - Membership: -

- Tax Devolution - Definition: - The share of Union Government's gross tax revenue transferred to State Governments as per the recommendations of the Finance Commission. - Constitutional Basis: - Article 280: - Mandates constitution of Finance Commission to recommend distribution of tax revenue between Centre and States. - Article 270: - Governs distribution of net proceeds of taxes between Union and States (vertical devolution). - Vertical Tax Devolution - Definition: - Vertical tax devolution is the process of distributing the divisible pool of Union taxes (excluding cess, surcharge, and IGST) between the Union and States, as recommended by the Finance Commission. - Historical Trend: -

- Post-Devolution Revenue Deficit Grants - Definition: - Grants provided to States that continue to face revenue deficits even after receiving their share of Union taxes, in order to maintain fiscal stability. - Mechanism: - Calculated as: - Revenue Deficit = State’s Revenue Expenditure – Revenue Income – Share from Union Taxes - Example: - Andhra Pradesh (2020–21): - Revenue Expenditure: ₹41,000 crore - Union Tax Share: ₹35,000 crore - Revenue Deficit: ₹6,000 crore - Hence, receives a ₹6,000 crore grant to cover the deficit. - Eligibility: - 14 States are eligible. - Includes: - Assam, Himachal Pradesh, Manipur, Meghalaya, etc. - Special Grants - Definition: - One-time compensatory grants provided to States that received a lower devolution amount in the 15th Finance Commission (2020–21) compared to the 14th Finance Commission, to address concerns of perceived fiscal injustice. - Details: - 2020–21: - Total Allocation: - ₹6,764 crore - Beneficiary States: - Karnataka, Telangana, Mizoram - 2021–26: - No special grants recommended or announced during this period. - Local Bodies Grants - Definition: - Financial assistance provided to empower Panchayati Raj Institutions and Urban Local Bodies, aimed at enhancing grassroots governance and improving public service delivery mechanisms. - Structure: - Basic Grants: - Allocated for general developmental needs listed in the 11th Schedule of the Constitution (excluding expenditure on salaries). - Performance-Based Grants: - Awarded based on outcomes such as achieving Open Defecation-Free (ODF) status, effective solid waste management, and sanitation improvements. - - Why Planning Commission Had More Power (Despite No Constitutional Status) - It controlled Plan Expenditure, which used to form a large chunk of the central budget. - All Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS), Five-Year Plans, etc., went through it. - States had to approach it for plan grants and project approvals. - As a result, it had significant discretionary power to: - Allocate funds - Approve state plans - Guide national priorities - This made state governments highly dependent on it for funding beyond just the tax devolutions recommended by Finance Commission. - Special Category States (SCS) - Concept & Background - Created to recognize states facing unique structural disadvantages that hinder their development. - These include geographic challenges, demographic backwardness, strategic concerns, and poor finances. - Origin - 1952: - National Development Council (NDC) formed to approve Five-Year Plans. - NDC (now defunct post-NITI Aayog) was responsible for granting SCS status till recent years. - 1969: - 5th Finance Commission recommended special tax reliefs for disadvantaged states. - Criteria for Special Category Status - Difficult terrain and hilly regions (e.g. Himalayas, NE states) - Low population density and/or high tribal population - Strategic location along international borders - Economic and infrastructural backwardness - Non-viable finances (i.e., poor internal revenue generation) - States that Received SCS - 11 traditional SCS states: - 8 North-Eastern states - 3 Himalayan states: - Jammu & Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand - Telangana was briefly given SCS after its formation in 2014 (by UPA govt) - Post Article 370 abrogation (2019), J&K ceased to be an SCS due to reorganization into UT. - Benefits of Special Category Status - Higher Union share in Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS): - Normal States: - 60:40 (Centre:State) - SCS States: - 90:10 - Industrial incentives: - Income tax and excise duty exemptions to industries setting up in SCS regions. - More weightage in Finance Commission's formulae for fund devolution. - This practice was ended by 14th Finance Commission. - Decline of SCS System - 15th Finance Commission: - No provision for recommending SCS. - Some states (e.g., Andhra Pradesh, Bihar, West Bengal) continue to demand SCS, especially before elections. - As of now, no state officially classified as SCS for Finance Commission purposes. - Union Govt may still offer benefits at its discretion through schemes and incentives. -
