- Pillar 2D1: Capital Receipts & Expenditure - Definition: - Capital receipts and expenditure in the Union Budget involve funds that affect the government’s assets or liabilities, distinct from revenue receipts/expenditure, which support day-to-day operations. Capital receipts include borrowings and disinvestment proceeds, while capital expenditure creates long-term assets or reduces liabilities. - Context: - Capital receipts provide funds for infrastructure, debt repayment, and investments, crucial for economic growth and fiscal stability. Capital expenditure drives development through asset creation (e.g., highways, hospitals) and financial investments, aligning with initiatives like Atmanirbhar Bharat and Gati Shakti. - Significance for India: - Effective management of capital receipts and expenditure enhances infrastructure, boosts economic productivity, and reduces fiscal deficits. Strategic disinvestment and monetization policies under Modi’s regime optimize public sector resources, fostering private sector participation and economic efficiency. - Capital Receipts - Definition: - Capital receipts are funds that increase the government’s liabilities (e.g., borrowings) or reduce its assets (e.g., disinvestment). They are categorized into debt and non-debt receipts. - Types of Capital Receipts: - Capital Debt Receipts: - Internal Borrowing: - Funds raised from domestic sources like RBI, banks, NBFCs, small savings schemes (e.g., Post Office Savings Accounts, Kisan Vikas Patra), and Provident Funds (e.g., EPFO, PPF). - External Borrowing: - Loans from foreign countries and institutions like IMF, World Bank, and BRICS Bank, typically in foreign currencies (e.g., USD, Euro). - Loan Principal Recovered: - Repayments of principal (not interest) from loans given by the Union to states, foreign countries, or public sector companies. - Capital Non-Debt Receipts: - Disinvestment: - Proceeds from selling government shares in Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs) or Central Public Sector Enterprises (CPSEs). -
Type Examples
Internal Borrowing RBI, banks, NBFCs, Post Office Savings, PPF.
External Borrowing IMF, World Bank, BRICS Bank loans.
Loan Principal Recovered Repayments from states, CPSEs, foreign nations.
Disinvestment Sale of shares in CPSEs like ONGC, SAIL.
- Foreign Borrowing in Foreign Currency - Introduction: - In Budget 2019, Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman announced that India’s sovereign external debt-to-GDP ratio is low (~5%), allowing the government to raise part of its borrowing from external markets in foreign currencies (e.g., USD, Euro). - Arguments in Favor: - Reduces Crowding Out: - Domestic borrowing reduces funds available for private corporates. External borrowing frees up domestic capital for private sector growth, enabling factory expansion, job creation, and GDP growth. - Lower Interest Rates: - Advanced economies (e.g., USA, EU) offer lower interest rates, making foreign loans cheaper than domestic ones. - Manageable Debt Levels: - India’s low external debt ratio suggests that moderate foreign borrowing is unlikely to cause significant fiscal strain. - Arguments Against: - Exchange Rate Risk: - A weakening rupee (e.g., $1 = Rs. 60 to Rs. 70) increases the repayment burden in rupees, making loans more expensive than anticipated. - Risk of Over-Borrowing: - Future governments may excessively borrow abroad to fund populist schemes, risking a debt crisis if the rupee depreciates significantly. - Alternative Approach: - Increasing foreign investment limits in Government Securities (G-Secs) in INR attracts foreign capital without currency risk, preferable to borrowing in foreign currency. - Conclusion: - While foreign borrowing can reduce domestic crowding out and leverage low global interest rates, the exchange rate risk and potential for over-borrowing outweigh benefits. Economists like Raghuram Rajan advocate caution, suggesting more deliberation before expanding foreign currency borrowing. -
Arguments In Favor Against
Economic Impact Reduces crowding out, boosts private sector growth. Exchange rate risk increases repayment costs.
Interest Rates Lower rates in advanced economies. Future over-borrowing risks debt crisis.
Alternative - Increase G-Sec investment limits in INR.

The decline in 2025–26 reflects cautious borrowing strategies. - Disinvestment - Definition: - Disinvestment involves the government selling its shareholding in Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs) or Central Public Sector Enterprises (CPSEs) to raise funds, typically retaining at least 51% ownership to maintain control. - Types of Government-Owned Entities: - Departmental Undertakings: - Directly managed by ministries (e.g., Railways, Postal Services). No separate legal entity, high ministerial interference, audited by CAG. - Statutory Corporations: - Created by specific Acts (e.g., RBI, LIC, SBI, EPFO). Some have internal audits, excluding CAG. Moderate operational flexibility. - Government Companies (CPSEs): - Registered under the Companies Act with ≥51% government shareholding (e.g., Coal India, NTPC). Audited by CAG-empaneled private auditors, higher operational flexibility. -
Type Examples Features

Statutory Corporations RBI, LIC, SBI Moderate flexibility, some exclude CAG.
Government Companies Coal India, NTPC High flexibility, private auditors.
- Objectives: - Promote public welfare through affordable services, develop infrastructure, ensure regional balance, and prevent economic concentration in private hands. - Challenges: - Political interference, lack of innovation, employee union resistance, and loss-making operations hinder efficiency. - CPSE vs. PSU vs. PSB - Public Sector Banks (PSBs): - Banks with majority government shareholding (e.g., SBI, PNB). - Central Public Sector Enterprises (CPSEs): - Government companies under the Companies Act with ≥51% Union shareholding, excluding PSBs, public sector insurance companies, and NBFCs (e.g., ONGC, SAIL). - Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs): - Collective term for all government-owned entities, including CPSEs, PSBs, and state/local government companies. -

PSU All government-owned entities (Union, state, local). CPSEs, PSBs, state companies.
- Ratna Companies - Definition: - Ratna status, granted by the Department of Public Enterprises (DPE, shifted to Finance Ministry in 2021), provides operational autonomy to high-performing CPSEs (not PSBs, statutory corporations, or private companies). - Categories and Conditions: - Miniratna (Category I & II): - CPSEs with continuous profits for three years, subdivided based on profit levels (e.g., NHPC, Mazagaon Dock). - Navratna: - Miniratna or other CPSEs meeting criteria like manpower cost ratios (e.g., Hindustan Aeronautics, Bharat Electronics). - Maharatna: - Navratna CPSEs with ≥Rs. 5,000 crore annual profit for three years, listed on stock exchanges, and significant global presence (e.g., Coal India, NTPC). - Benefits: - Autonomy in hiring, acquisitions, and decision-making without government approval for minor decisions. - Examples (as of 1/1/2020): - Miniratna: - National Film Development Corporation, WAPCOS. - Navratna: - Oil India, Container Corporation. - Maharatna: - Bharat Petroleum, GAIL, ONGC. -
Category Conditions Examples
Miniratna Profits for 3 years. NHPC, Mazagaon Dock.
Navratna Miniratna or specific criteria. Bharat Electronics, Oil India.
Maharatna Navratna, ≥Rs. 5,000 crore profit, global presence. Coal India, NTPC.
- Government Policy Towards Disinvestment (Pre-2021) - Disinvestment: - Reducing government shareholding in CPSEs while retaining ≥51% to maintain control. - Privatization/Strategic Disinvestment: - Selling ≥50% shareholding with management control transfer, as defined by DIPAM. - Historical Developments: - 1951: - First Constitution Amendment Act allowed nationalization by imposing restrictions on private industries for public interest. - 1953: - Air Corporations Act nationalized nine private airlines into Indian Airlines and Air India International. - 1991: - Interim Budget introduced disinvestment, with PM Narasimha Rao announcing shareholding reduction in government companies. - 1998–99 (Vajpayee Era): - No disinvestment in strategic sectors (e.g., railways, defense, atomic energy). - Phased disinvestment in non-strategic sectors; established Department of Disinvestment (1999), privatizing Maruti Udyog, VSNL, etc. - UPA-1 (2004–09): - No disinvestment due to Leftist coalition pressure; focus on reviving sick government companies. - UPA-2 (2009–14): - Allowed disinvestment up to 49%, retaining 51% control. Proceeds went to National Investment Fund (NIF) for bank recapitalization, metro rail, etc. - Launched CPSE-Exchange Traded Funds (ETFs). - Arguments for Disinvestment: - Private investors enhance efficiency, innovation, and autonomy. - Proceeds fund welfare schemes and reduce fiscal deficits. - Arguments Against: - Risks MNC monopolies, worker exploitation, and job losses. -

- Disinvestment & Privatization in Modi Raj (2014–) - Methods of Disinvestment: - Initial Public Offerings (IPOs): - Converting private limited CPSEs to public companies (e.g., IRCTC, RVNL). - Exchange Traded Funds (ETFs): - CPSE-ETF, Bharat-22-ETF for diversified share sales. - Institutional Placement Programme (IPP): - Shares offered to non-retail investors (e.g., institutions). - Offer for Sale (OFS): - Shares sold to both retail and non-retail investors. - Share Buyback: - CPSEs buy back government-held shares, reducing government shareholding. - Key Actions: - Closed loss-making CPSEs (e.g., HMT Watches, Hindustan Photo Film). - 2016: - Renamed Department of Disinvestment to Department of Investment and Public Asset Management (DIPAM). - Privatization Examples: - Budget 2022: - Neelanchal Ispat Nigam Limited (NINL) privatized. - Economic Survey 2023: - 10 CPSEs privatized, including HPCL, REC, Air India. - 2024: - Ferro Scrap Nigam Limited (FSNL) sold to a Japanese company for Rs. 320 crore. -

- New Public Sector Enterprise (PSE) Policy (2021) - Strategic Sectors: - Atomic energy, space, defense, transport, telecom, power, petroleum, coal, minerals, banking, insurance, financial services. - Policy: - Retain at least one government company; others may be merged, privatized, or closed. - Non-Strategic Sectors: - All other sectors (e.g., manufacturing, hospitality). - Policy: - All government companies to be privatized or closed. - Terminology: - NITI Aayog prefers “strategic disinvestment” or “strategic sale” over “privatization” to avoid political backlash. -
Sector Policy Examples
Strategic Retain ≥1 company; merge/privatize others. Defense, telecom, banking.
Non-Strategic Privatize/close all companies. Manufacturing, hospitality.

Capital Expenditure - Definition: - Capital expenditure involves government spending to create long-term assets or reduce liabilities, such as infrastructure projects, loan repayments, or investments in PSUs. - Notable Components: - Capital Assets: - Expenditure on buildings, vehicles, and equipment for schemes, ministries, and departments. - Debt/Equity Financing: - Investments in PSUs, foreign institutions, or loans to state/foreign governments. - Loan Principal Repayments: - Repaying principal for internal and external debts (interest payments are revenue expenditure). - Indian Development and Economic Assistance Scheme (IDEAS): - Managed by the Department of Economic Affairs (DEA), provides loans to foreign nations for development projects. -

Capital Assets Buildings, highways, hospitals, vehicles.
Debt/Equity Financing Loans to states, PSUs, foreign nations.
Loan Repayments Principal for internal/external debts.
IDEAS Loans for foreign development projects.
- State CAPEX Loans - Special Assistance to States for Capital Expenditure (CAPEX Scheme): - Introduced in 2020, provides interest-free, 50-year loans to states for capital projects (e.g., health, rural development, water supply, irrigation, power, transport, education, urban development). - Budget 2020: - Allocated ~Rs. 12,000 crore. - Budget 2025: - Increased to Rs. 1.5 lakh crore. - Linked Reforms (Budget 2023): - Scrapping old government vehicles to reduce emissions. - Urban planning reforms to improve city infrastructure. - Strengthening urban local bodies to issue municipal bonds. - Housing for policemen to enhance security infrastructure. - Constructing Unity Malls to promote regional products (refer to Pillar 3A). - Libraries and digital infrastructure for children and adolescents. - Significance: - Enhances state-level infrastructure, promotes fiscal federalism, and aligns with national development goals. - Effective Capital Expenditure - Definition: - Effective capital expenditure includes direct Union capital expenditure and grants-in-aid to states for capital projects, reflecting the total capital investment impact. - Significance: - Measures the government’s contribution to asset creation and economic growth, critical for infrastructure development and job creation. - Note: - Detailed explanation available in referenced video lectures, focusing on budget allocations and economic impact. - Capital Expenditure for a Company - Definition: - Capital expenditure by a company involves investments in income-generating assets, categorized as tangible or intangible. - Types of Assets: - Tangible Assets: - Land, buildings, vehicles, equipment, raw materials, unsold inventory. - Intangible Assets: - Patents, copyrights, brand logos, trade secrets (e.g., KFC recipe), licenses (e.g., telecom spectrum), investments in shares/bonds, client databases, accounts receivable, goodwill, website domains. - Subcategories: - Current Assets: - Short-term assets like cash, inventory. - Financial Assets: - Investments in shares, bonds. - Fixed Assets: - Long-term assets like buildings, equipment. -
