- Pillar 2D2: Deficits and Fiscal Policy - Introduction: - Deficits occur when government expenditure exceeds its receipts, impacting fiscal stability and economic growth. Understanding types of deficits, their financing, and related policies is crucial for assessing India’s fiscal health and compliance with the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003. - Types of Budget - Surplus Budget: - Occurs when government receipts (revenue and capital) exceed expenditure, allowing debt repayment or reserve creation. - Balanced Budget: - Receipts equal expenditure, ensuring no borrowing is required. - Deficit Budget: - Expenditure exceeds receipts, necessitating borrowing to bridge the gap. -
Budget Type Definition
Surplus Receipts > Expenditure
Balanced Receipts = Expenditure
Deficit Expenditure > Receipts
- Budget Deficit - Definition: - Excess of total expenditure (revenue and capital) over total receipts (revenue and capital). Since 1997–98, the Union Budget has ceased reporting budget deficit, focusing instead on fiscal deficit. - Example Data (Budget 2025): - Not explicitly reported due to discontinuation; fiscal deficit is now the primary metric. - Fiscal Deficit - Definition: - Excess of total expenditure over total receipts, excluding borrowings. It represents the amount the government must borrow to meet its financial obligations. - Formulas: - Tamilnadu Textbook: - Fiscal Deficit = Budget Deficit + Borrowings (internal, RBI, external). - NCERT Textbook: - Fiscal Deficit = Total Expenditure – (Revenue Receipts + Non-Debt Creating Capital Receipts). - Alternatively: - Difference between total expenditure and total receipts, excluding borrowings. - Note: - Sukhmoy Chakravarti Committee (1997–98) emphasized fiscal deficit as a key indicator. - Example Data (Budget 2025): - Fiscal Deficit Target: - 4.5% of GDP (as per 15th Finance Commission recommendation). -
Source Formula

NCERT Total Expenditure – (Revenue Receipts + Non-Debt Capital Receipts)
- Gross Fiscal Deficit (GFD) vs. Net Fiscal Deficit - Gross Fiscal Deficit (GFD): - Total borrowings (internal, RBI, external). - Net Fiscal Deficit: - GFD minus net lending by the central government (e.g., loans to states or PSUs). - Note: - RBI is listed separately in formulas per NCERT, despite being domestic, for clarity in source differentiation. -

- Primary Deficit - Definition: - Fiscal Deficit minus interest payments on previous loans, indicating borrowing for new programs rather than servicing past debt. - Significance: - High interest payments from accumulated debt increase borrowing needs, creating a vicious cycle. Primary deficit isolates borrowing for current expenditures. - Introduced: - 1993 Budget Speech by Finance Minister Manmohan Singh. - Example Data (Budget 2025): - Primary Deficit Target: - Shifted to 0% by 2022–23, not achieved due to COVID-19. - Gross Primary Deficit (GPD) - Definition: - Gross Fiscal Deficit minus net interest liabilities. - Net Primary Deficit: - Net Fiscal Deficit minus net interest liabilities. - Note: - Formulas vary by context (A-B or B-A), but the focus is on isolating non-interest borrowing. - Revenue Deficit - Definition: - Excess of revenue expenditure (e.g., salaries, subsidies, interest payments) over revenue receipts (e.g., taxes, non-tax revenue). - Formula: - Revenue Deficit = Revenue Expenditure – Revenue Receipts. - Significance: - High revenue deficit forces borrowing for operational expenses, reducing funds for capital expenditure (e.g., schools, hospitals), hindering human development and economic growth. -
Deficit Formula
Revenue Deficit Revenue Expenditure – Revenue Receipts
- Effective Revenue Deficit - Definition: - Revenue Deficit minus grants-in-aid to states/local bodies used for capital asset creation (e.g., panchayat buildings, disaster management infrastructure). - Introduced: - Budget 2011 by Finance Minister P. Chidambaram. - Significance: - Adjusts revenue deficit to account for productive expenditure, providing a clearer picture of non-productive borrowing. - Formula: - Effective Revenue Deficit = Revenue Deficit – Grants for Capital Assets. -

- Ricardian Equivalence: - Theory: - High fiscal deficit → Government borrows more → At maturity (G-Sec redemption), government must repay principal + interest → This may lead to future tax hikes. - Anticipating this, rational citizens may increase savings and reduce consumption → Lower demand → Inventory buildup → Slows economic growth. - Economist David Ricardo proposed this theory: called Ricardian Equivalence Proposition (REP). - Invalid for India (Economic Survey 2021): - REP assumes people are rational, forward-looking, and understand future liabilities → Not always true in India. - Reasons for invalidity: - Citizens are not perfectly rational; decisions often influenced by emotional/personal needs (e.g., weddings, iPhones, foreign trips). - High prevalence of tax​ evasion, black​ money, and a parallel economy → Not all citizens bear the tax burden equally. - Large informal sector and financial illiteracy weaken the theoretical assumptions of REP in the Indian context. - Crowding Out Effect: - Concept: - When the government borrows heavily from households and financial institutions (LIC, EPFO, banks via SLR), less money is left for private corporate borrowers → Crowds out private investment → Slows factory expansion and job creation. - Financial Repression: - Forcing public institutions (e.g., SBI, LIC, EPFO) to buy G-Secs using depositors’ money → deprives households of optimal returns. - Example: Govt forced NABARD to buy ₹15,000 crore Swachh Bharat Mission (Gramin) bonds (10-year maturity). - Govt pressures RBI and others to pay higher dividends → reduces their operational freedom. - ES21 Observations (1950s–1990s): - Found some evidence of crowding out due to: - Very high CRR and SLR → PSBs had limited loanable funds. - Absence of large private banks. - Low earnings and savings among public. - Underdeveloped capital markets. - ES21 Findings (Post-1990): - No evidence of crowding out (1990–2019) due to structural changes: - Economic growth ↑ → household income ↑ → savings ↑ → more money available to both govt and private borrowers. - Rise in banking habits, NBFCs, mutual funds, and capital market participation. - Demographic dividend → more working population → improved financial behavior. - Implication: - If govt borrowing is for productive infrastructure (health, education, transport, etc.), it raises GDP and incomes → boosts overall savings → the 'size of the dish' increases, so both govt and private sectors get enough funds. - Thus, "crowding out" is less likely when savings are not fixed but expand with growth. - Monetization of Deficit: - When central bank prints more money to pay for government's loans → Monetizing the Deficit. - It can result in hyperinflation and ↓↓ purchasing power of currency (if supply of goods like onion, tomato is not increased sufficiently). Example: Germany after Treaty of Versailles (1919). - Therefore, Monetizing the Deficit is also called "inflating away of debt". - FRBM Act prevents monetization of deficit with following provisions: - RBI must not subscribe to G-Sec in primary market from 2006-07 onwards. - Govt shall not borrow from RBI, except for Ways and Means Advances (WMA). (Refer: Pillar 3) - However, ES21 said not to worry too much about it. - Countercyclical Fiscal Policy - Introduction and Historical Context: - Historically, governments have used public spending during times of economic distress to provide employment and stimulate growth. - In ancient economies, rulers invested in infrastructure like roads, canals, and public buildings during famines to create jobs. - In modern macroeconomics, this principle is institutionalized as countercyclical fiscal policy. - Definition: - A countercyclical fiscal policy involves increasing public expenditure and/or reducing taxes during economic slowdowns, and reducing spending and/or increasing taxes during economic booms. - The objective is to smoothen economic fluctuations, support aggregate demand, and maintain macroeconomic stability. - How it Works: - During Economic Slowdown (Recessionary Phase): - Government increases capital and revenue expenditure. - Objectives: - Stimulate demand through infrastructure projects. - Generate employment and raise household income. - Boost private sector confidence and investment. - Effect: Crowding-In - Increased government investment leads to complementary private sector activity. - Example: - Government constructs national highways → private sector builds logistics parks, fuel stations, hotels → more jobs and tax revenue. - During Economic Boom (Expansionary Phase): - Government reduces expenditure and increases savings. - Objectives: - Control inflationary pressures. - Accumulate fiscal buffers (surpluses) to deploy during future downturns. - Avoid overheating of the economy. - Overheating of an economy refers to a situation where economic growth is unsustainably high, leading to inflationary pressures, resource shortages, and imbalances. - Strategic Benefits: - Ensures long-term fiscal sustainability. - Enhances economic resilience to external shocks (e.g., global financial crises, pandemics). - Improves sovereign credit ratings and investor confidence. - Pro-cyclical Fiscal Policy: - Opposite of countercyclical approach. - Government increases expenditure during booms and cuts spending during slowdowns. - Consequences: - Amplifies economic volatility. - Worsens inflation during booms and unemployment during recessions. - Example: - India’s historical fiscal stance has often been pro-cyclical, as highlighted by Economic Surveys. - Real-World Examples: - Chile: - Uses a structural fiscal rule based on long-term copper prices to avoid pro-cyclicality. - United States: - Large fiscal stimulus packages during the 2008 Global Financial Crisis and COVID-19 pandemic. - India: - National Infrastructure Pipeline (NIP) and PM Gati Shakti are attempts to institutionalize countercyclical investment. - Key Terminology: - Crowding-In: - When public investment encourages private sector investment. - Fiscal Multipliers: - The ratio of change in output to the change in government spending. - IRGD (Interest Rate – Growth Rate Differential) and Debt Sustainability - Concept: - IRGD measures the difference between the interest rate on government borrowing and the nominal GDP growth rate. - Indicator of whether public debt is sustainable over the medium to long term. - Formula: - Version 1: IRGD = Interest Rate – GDP Growth Rate - If IRGD is negative (e.g., 6% – 9% = –3%), it indicates strong debt sustainability. - Version 2: IRGD = GDP Growth Rate – Interest Rate - If IRGD is positive (e.g., 9% – 6% = +3%), it implies faster growth than borrowing cost → sustainable. -
IndicatorFormulaInterpretation
IRGD (Interest Rate – Growth Rate) 6% – 9% = –3% Negative IRGD is favourable; debt burden reduces over time.
GRID (Growth Rate – Interest Rate) 9% – 6% = +3% Positive GRID also indicates sustainability.
- Domar Condition of Debt Sustainability - Concept Origin: - Proposed by economist Evsey Domar in 1944. - Provides a theoretical foundation for assessing long-term debt sustainability. - Domar's Rule: - If the growth rate of nominal GDP is higher than the interest rate on public debt, then public debt as a percentage of GDP will decline over time. - Formula: - If g > r → debt-to-GDP ratio stabilizes or declines. - Where g = GDP growth rate, r = interest rate on debt. - Implications: - Countries with high GDP growth can afford higher deficits without increasing their debt burden. - Countries with low growth need to either raise taxes or cut spending to maintain fiscal stability. - Indian Scenario: - India’s nominal GDP growth post-COVID recovery has remained higher than the average cost of borrowing. - Indicates that fiscal expansion (within limits) can be sustainable if accompanied by growth-enhancing reforms. - Automatic Stabilizers - Definition: - Mechanisms in the government budget that automatically increase spending or decrease tax liability during economic slowdowns without requiring changes in tax laws or parliamentary approval. - Examples: - Income tax, corporation tax, unemployment allowance, food subsidies, and government schemes targeting the poor/lower-middle class. - Note: - Countercyclical fiscal policy/fiscal stimulus requires new budgets/laws/parliamentary approval, making them discretionary stabilizers, not automatic. - Disposable Income & Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC): - Disposable Income: - Personal income minus personal income taxes; available for consumption and saving. - MPC: - Fraction of additional disposable income spent on consumption. - Tax Multiplier: - Negative multiplier: - Increase in taxes reduces disposable income, decreasing consumption and GDP. - Autonomous Expenditure Multiplier (AEM) & Government Expenditure Multiplier: -

- Methods for Repaying Public Debt: - Redemption (Terminal Annuity): - Meaning: - Refers to the repayment of both principal and interest at regular intervals. - It concludes the debt obligation over time. - Notes: - Common in long-term borrowing arrangements. - Ensures gradual reduction in outstanding liabilities. - Sinking Fund: - Meaning: - A special fund created by the government wherein money is deposited regularly. - At the time of maturity of Government Securities (G-secs), this fund is used to repay the debt. - Significance: - Reduces pressure on budget at the time of redemption. - Enhances creditworthiness of the government. - History: - First introduced in England as a fiscal prudence measure. - Conversion / Restructuring: - Meaning: - Old debt is replaced with new debt having modified terms such as lower interest rate or longer tenure. - Example: - Lowering the interest burden by converting old high-interest debt into new low-interest debt. - Context: - 2020: - Zambia became the first African nation to default on Eurobond payments during the COVID-19 pandemic. - Experts recommended restructuring for debt sustainability. - Evergreening of Loans: - Meaning: - Taking a new loan to repay the old one. - Risks: - Creates a debt trap situation if repeated frequently. - May hide the true fiscal health of the government. - Repudiation: - Meaning: - Government refuses to acknowledge and repay its debt obligations. - Example: - After the Russian Revolution (1917), Lenin's regime repudiated the loans taken by the previous Czarist regime from Britain and France. - Implication: - Considered a poor strategy in modern economics. - Leads to loss of investor confidence and denial of future credit. - Fiscal Consolidation / Prudence - Definition: - Fiscal Consolidation refers to policies aimed at reducing government deficits and debt accumulation. - It is primarily achieved by rationalizing expenditures and improving efficiency in revenue collection. - Objectives: - Reduce fiscal deficit. - Improve macroeconomic stability. - Control inflation. - Maintain investor confidence. - Prevent sovereign credit downgrade. - Measures for Fiscal Consolidation: - Reduction in Leakages: - Targeted delivery of subsidies and welfare schemes via DBT (Direct Benefit Transfer). - - Fiscal Stimulus - Definition: - Fiscal Stimulus refers to a government’s use of reduced taxes and/or increased public expenditure to boost economic demand and revive growth, especially during a slowdown or crisis. - It involves direct intervention by the government to increase disposable income, create employment, and restore investor and consumer confidence. - Key Mechanisms: - Tax cuts to increase consumer and business spending. - Increased government expenditure (capital and revenue). - Direct cash transfers or subsidies. - Credit guarantees and support to MSMEs. - Fiscal Stimulus by Dr. Manmohan Singh (2008) - Context: - Triggered by the Global Financial Crisis (2007–08) originating in the US subprime lending market. - India’s exports and industrial growth were adversely impacted. - Key Measures: - Reduction in excise duty and customs duty on exports. - Tax concessions to industries purchasing new commercial vehicles (to support auto sector). - Hike in Minimum Support Prices (MSPs) for key crops to boost rural demand. - Outcome: - Provided short-term relief to industry and rural sector. - However, led to widened fiscal deficit in the following years. - Raised concerns in Economic Surveys regarding sustainability and efficiency of blanket stimulus. - Atma-Nirbhar Bharat Economic Stimulus Package (2020) - Context: - 2020: - COVID-19 pandemic-induced nationwide lockdown. - Economic disruption affected all sectors — from corporates to daily wage earners. - Objective: - Revive demand and supply chains. - Provide liquidity and credit access. - Promote self-reliance and structural reforms. - Launch: - Announced in May 2020 by the Prime Minister of India. - Built on 5 pillars: - Economy, Infrastructure, System, Demand, Vibrant Demography. - Components and Allocations: -

Vertical Imbalance: - Tax powers are concentrated with the Centre, but spending responsibilities are higher at the state level. - Creates dependence of states on central transfers (Finance Commission, GST compensation, grants). - Example: - States have limited access to income tax or customs, but must fund health, education, etc. - 2. Horizontal Imbalance: - Fiscal capacity varies widely among states due to differences in resource endowment, development level. - Richer states like Maharashtra generate more revenue, while poorer states depend more on transfers. - 3. Delayed and Unpredictable Transfers: - States face uncertainty in fund flows from Centre (e.g. GST compensation delays). - Hinders planning and execution of welfare schemes and infrastructure projects. - 4.
