- Pillar 2A2: Indirect Taxes - Definition: - Indirect taxes are levies imposed on goods and services, collected by intermediaries (e.g., traders, manufacturers) and passed on to consumers through the price of the product, administered by the Ministry of Finance, Department of Revenue, through the Central Board of Indirect Taxes and Customs (CBIC), renamed from Central Board of Excise and Customs (CBEC) in Budget-2018. - Context: - Indirect taxes are a critical revenue source for the Union and State governments, funding public services and infrastructure while influencing consumption patterns and economic behavior through targeted taxation. - Significance for India: - Indirect taxes, particularly GST, have unified India’s tax structure, reduced cascading effects, and promoted ease of doing business, aligning with India’s goals of economic integration, transparency, and global competitiveness under initiatives like Make in India and Atmanirbhar Bharat. - Indirect Taxes Types: - Definition: - Indirect taxes are categorized based on their method of imposition, either on the value of goods/services or on their quantity. - Types: - Ad-Valorem Tax: - Levied as a percentage of the value of goods or services (e.g., 35% customs duty on imported orange juice worth ₹1000 results in ₹350 tax). - Advantages: - Easier to administer due to standardized valuation methods. - Disadvantages: - Leads to inspector-raj and litigation; small rate increases significantly impact consumer prices. - Specific Tax per Unit: - Levied based on quantity or volume (e.g., ₹260 excise duty per 1000 cigarettes of 65-70mm length, regardless of price). - Advantages: - Effective in reducing harmful consumption by passing greater burden to consumers. - Disadvantages: - Difficult to administer due to quantity-based assessments. -

- Burden of Indirect Tax – Forward vs. Backward Shifting - Definition: - The burden of indirect taxes can shift to consumers (forward) or suppliers/producers (backward), depending on market dynamics. - Forward Shifting: - Sellers increase product prices to maintain profit margins (e.g., Vadilal raises ice cream prices after GST increase from 18% to 28%). - Backward Shifting: - Sellers absorb tax increases by cutting costs (e.g., Vadilal reduces worker salaries to maintain ice cream prices and market share). - Factors Influencing Shifting: - Determined by supply and demand elasticity (e.g., forward shifting is common for essential medicines due to inelastic demand). - Merits and Demerits - Merits: - Convenient to collect as traders act as unpaid tax collectors, ensuring a wide tax base (e.g., 18% GST on biscuits). - Elastic, generating significant revenue from small rate increases, impacting all consumers. - Reduces harmful consumption through high taxes on sin goods (e.g., tobacco, alcohol, soft drinks). - Demerits: - Regressive, disproportionately affecting the poor, who pay a higher income share (e.g., same GST rate on biscuits for all income groups). - Hidden in product prices, reducing civic awareness of tax contributions. - Increases product costs, creating demand uncertainty and variable government revenue. - High risk of corruption and tax​ evasion without input tax credit, leading to cascading effects. -

additional cess violates constitutional taxation provisions. - Ruling (Himachal HC, 2024): - Water cess declared unconstitutional. - Cascading Effect of Indirect Taxes - Definition: - It refers to the "tax on tax" phenomenon where a tax is levied on a value that already includes tax from a previous stage of the supply chain. - Happens in absence of Input Tax Credit (ITC) under older tax regimes (like excise, VAT, service tax). - Cause: - No mechanism to offset taxes paid on inputs/services. - Each intermediary adds value and charges tax on the full amount (including previously paid tax). - Example: - Manufacturer pays excise on raw materials. - Wholesaler pays VAT on a price that includes excise. - Retailer then charges tax again on this compounded price. - Impact: - Increases cost of goods/services for the final consumer. - Reduces tax transparency and complicates accounting. - Hurts competitiveness of Indian products (esp. in exports). - GST Reform: - GST allows seamless ITC, eliminating cascading by enabling tax credit across goods/services and interstate supply chains. - Ensures tax is levied only on the value addition at each stage. - A Timeline of Reforms - Evolution: -

- Implementation: - Announced 2020, postponed due to COVID, effective 2022. - Compensation to States - Why? - Definition: - GST is a destination-based indirect tax on consumption of goods and services. - Union Government: - Largest tax sources: - Corporate tax and personal income tax (direct taxes, excluded from GST). - State Governments: - Largest tax source: - VAT, subsumed under GST along with other indirect taxes, cess, and surcharges. - States feared revenue loss due to GST replacing VAT. - Destination-Based Tax: - Industrialized states (e.g., Gujarat, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, Haryana) concerned about lower SGST revenue compared to VAT regime. - Example: - Nano car manufactured in Gujarat, sold in Uttar Pradesh → UP gets SGST, Gujarat gets nothing. Reverse also true (e.g., UP’s bicycle sold in Gujarat). - How? - GST Compensation to States Act 2017: - GST Compensation Cess imposed on luxury and demerit goods: - Pan masala (60%) - Tobacco products (varies by product) - Aerated water & caffeinated beverages (12%) - Coal/lignite (₹400 per tonne) - Motor vehicles, aircraft, yachts (3-22% depending on type) - Cess collected used to compensate states for revenue losses during first five years of GST (July 1, 2017 – June 30, 2022). - Compensation Formula (Section 7): - Projected Revenue: - 14% annual compound growth rate on 2015 VAT collection (base year). - If current SGST collection < Projected Revenue, Union compensates the shortfall. - Extension (Section 8): - GST Council can recommend extending cess beyond five years. - Eligibility: - Compensation only for SGST (not for UTGST, e.g., Ladakh). - State liquor taxes outside GST, so states with liquor prohibition (e.g., Bihar, Gujarat) cannot claim compensation for liquor revenue loss. - GST Compensation Cess Collections: -

- Controversy: - Pre-COVID Period (from August 2019 onwards): - Several states began reporting delays in receiving GST compensation from the Centre. - GST arrears started piling up, despite the Centre continuing to collect Compensation Cess. - This led to a political and fiscal dispute, with states demanding timely compensation as per the GST (Compensation to States) Act, 2017. - GST Compensation & Back-to-Back Loans - 2020: - COVID lockdown reduced SGST collections, states demanded ₹2.35 Lakh Cr in compensation. - Union’s Initial Response: - Claimed not legally obliged to pay ₹2.35 Lakh Cr due to “Act of God” (COVID). - Offered to pay only estimated amount (₹97,000 Cr). - Attorney General’s Opinion: - GST Compensation Act does not allow such exceptions; Union must pay full ₹2.35 Lakh Cr. - Solution: - Due to low cess collection, Union offered two borrowing options. - States accepted modified Option #1, receiving ₹1.10 Lakh Cr. - Beneficiary States - Eligible: - States & 3 UTs with legislature (e.g., Andhra, Assam, Bihar, Goa, Gujarat, Haryana, Kerala, Delhi, Jammu and Kashmir, Puducherry). - The cess collected was meant to bridge the shortfall in state revenues due to GST rollout, based on a 14% annual revenue growth promise over 2015-16 base year. - Extension to March 31, 2026: - While the compensation payout period to states ended in June 2022, the levy of GST Compensation Cess was extended till March 31, 2026. - Enhances investor confidence and aligns India’s tax administration with global best practices. - GST Benefits - Structural Reform: - Replaced a complex web of indirect taxes (VAT, CST, Service Tax, Excise, etc.) with a single, unified tax system. - Ensures "One Nation, One Tax, One Market". - Standardization & Transparency: - Uniform tax rates across goods/services reduce classification disputes. - Fewer cesses and surcharges ensure predictability for businesses. - GST Council’s cooperative federalism ensures balanced rate decisions across Centre and States. - Digital Governance: - GSTN (Goods and Services Tax Network) enables full-digitization of registration, filing, payment, and refund. - E-way Bills ensure real-time tracking of goods, reducing check-post delays and inspector raj. - Automated compliance helps curb discretion and corruption at the ground level. - Input Tax Credit (ITC): - Eliminates cascading effect ("tax on tax") across the value chain. - Encourages firms to procure only from GST-registered suppliers, formalizing the economy. - Reduces overall production cost and improves tax compliance. - Anti-Profiteering Mechanism: - Ensures that tax rate cuts or ITC gains are passed to the consumer through MRP reduction. - Monitored by the National Anti-Profiteering Authority (NAA). - National Market Integration: - Common rates across states prevent rate arbitrage, reduce compliance costs for inter-state trade. - Encourages free movement of goods and services across India. - Boost to Manufacturing & Employment: - Promotes ancillarisation (SMEs as suppliers to large industries). - Reduces logistic and compliance burden for industries under Make in India. - Enhances competitiveness of Indian products through tax-efficient production. - GST Benefit: Zero-Rated Exports - Exports treated as zero-rated supplies: - 0% IGST charged on exports. - Input tax credit on raw materials and input services fully refundable. - Ensures Indian exporters remain competitive in global markets by removing embedded taxes. - GST Challenges - Technological Issues: - Initial glitches in the GSTN portal during rollout caused delays in return filing and refunds. - SMEs and traders faced difficulty in adapting to digital-only compliance, especially in Tier-2/3 towns. - Compliance Burden: - Complex return filing with multiple forms: GSTR-1, GSTR-3B, etc. - Monthly filings for each GSTIN create high compliance costs, especially for businesses operating in multiple states. - Input Tax Credit (ITC) Issues: - Frequent changes in ITC rules (e.g., 5% mismatch allowance) create confusion. - Delays in refund of ITC especially hurt exporters and working capital for SMEs. - Fake ITC scams led to tighter scrutiny, affecting genuine taxpayers too. - Multiple Tax Slabs: - Four main tax slabs (5%, 12%, 18%, 28%) + special rates for gold (3%) and real estate (1%, 5%) make the system complex. - Frequent rate revisions create uncertainty for businesses and tax planning. - Classification Disputes: - Ambiguity in classifying goods/services under different tax slabs leads to frequent litigation. - Example: Paratha vs. Roti, Ice cream parlours vs. restaurants. - Advanced Ruling Authorities (ARAs) give inconsistent rulings across states. - State Revenue Dependence: - States lost power to independently vary indirect tax rates. - Reliance on GST Compensation Cess led to Centre–State disputes, especially post-COVID when cess collections dropped. - Compensation Cess Controversy: - Centre delayed payment of GST compensation during COVID-19. - States faced fiscal stress; debates on borrowing responsibility (Centre vs. States). - Trust deficit in fiscal federalism. - Impact on Informal Sector: - GST formalizes supply chains, making it hard for small, unregistered businesses to survive. - Many traders dropped out of the value chain to avoid compliance burden. - Sectoral Concerns: - Petroleum products (crude oil, petrol, diesel, ATF, natural gas) still outside GST → leads to cascading effect. - Alcohol also excluded → breaks the value chain for related industries (e.g., hospitality). - Anti-Profiteering Mechanism: - Criticized for lack of transparency and standard methodology to determine profiteering. - Businesses find it arbitrary; NAA lacks appellate mechanism. - E-Commerce Tensions: - Platforms face TCS compliance under GST. - Sellers face blocked ITC and delays in refunds, especially small online sellers. - Constitutional Limitation: - GST is levied on “supply,” but legal disputes continue over intermediary services, cross-border software supply, etc. - Frequent Policy Changes: - Over 1,000 notifications issued in early years → confusion and lack of policy stability. - Mid-year rate changes affect inventory planning and accounting.
