- Why was OBC data not published (1951–2011)? - Post-Independence Policy Shift: - After 1947, the Indian state moved away from caste enumeration (except SC/ST) to avoid reinforcing caste divisions. - Emphasis was on promoting equality and treating caste as a private/social identity, not a public administrative category (beyond SC/ST). - OBC Identification Remained Politically Sensitive: - OBCs were not constitutionally recognized as a separate group until the Mandal Commission (1979–80). - Post-Mandal (1990), OBC reservations were implemented but no national-level caste census was taken for OBCs due to: - Political backlash and polarisation risks - Fear of inflaming identity-based politics - Disputes over methodology and accuracy - SECC 2011 - Data Collected but Not Released - Socio-Economic and Caste Census (SECC) 2011 was the first post-independence attempt to gather caste data for all. - However: - The data was found to be error-prone, inconsistent, and politically controversial - The Rangarajan Committee flagged its poor data quality - So, while economic data from SECC was used, the caste component was never officially released to the public. - Census 2021 (Postponed) - Funding: - ₹8,700 crore allocated by Union Cabinet in December 2019. - Data Points: - Housing conditions, amenities/assets, demography, religion, SC/ST, language, literacy, economic activity, migration, fertility. - Innovation: - First-time use of a mobile app for data collection. - Phases: - House Listing and Housing Census: - April to September 2020. - Population Enumeration: - February 9 to February 28, 2021. - National Population Register (NPR): - The National Population Register (NPR) is a register of the usual residents of the country. - Usual resident: - A person who has resided in a local area for the past 6 months or intends to reside there for the next 6 months or more. - Updated concurrently (except in Assam) under Citizenship Act, 1955. - Why is NPR updated concurrently with Census? - Operational Efficiency: - Since both the Census and NPR require house-to-house visits and demographic data collection, it is cost-effective and time-saving to update NPR along with the Census House Listing phase. - Data Synergy: - NPR and Census collect overlapping information like name, age, gender, address, etc. - Updating both simultaneously avoids duplication and ensures data consistency. - Legal & Administrative Requirements: - The Census is governed by the Census Act, 1948 while NPR is governed by Citizenship Rules, 2003. - Updating NPR helps the government prepare for any future National Register of Citizens (NRC), though the NRC is a separate and controversial exercise. - Why Assam is excluded? - Assam already completed an NRC exercise under Supreme Court supervision. - NRC (National Register of Citizens) in Assam was published in August 2019. - Its purpose: - Identify illegal immigrants from Bangladesh. - Over 19 lakh people were excluded from Assam NRC. - To avoid duplication/conflict between NRC and NPR: - The Union Home Ministry decided to exclude Assam from NPR rollout. - NPR is not a Supreme Court-mandated exercise, but NRC in Assam was. - Status: - Postponed due to COVID-19; rescheduled for 2024-25. - Demand for OBC Caste Census - Background: - Mandal Commission (1979) estimated OBC population at 52%. Socio-Economic Caste Census (SECC) 2011 collected caste data but not released publicly. - Need: - Lack of OBC population, education, and income data hinders effective policy-making for OBC welfare. - Demand: - Political groups advocate including OBC enumeration in the next census. - Government Response (2021): - No caste-wise enumeration except for SC/ST in Census (Lok Sabha statement). - State Initiatives (2023-25): - Bihar, Karnataka, and others began state-level caste censuses. - Caste Census Inclusion (April 2025) - Announcement: - Modi government decided to include caste enumeration in the 2025 Census. - Rationale: - Census is a Union subject (Article 246, Seventh Schedule, Item 69). - State-level caste surveys lack transparency and are politically motivated, risking social fabric. - Centralized caste enumeration in the main census ensures credibility and avoids political bias. - Demographic Transition (Census Perspective) -

- Malthusian Theory of Population Growth - Proposed by: - Thomas Robert Malthus (1798). - Theory: - Population grows geometrically (exponential), while food production grows arithmetically (linear). - Natural equilibrium achieved through "Positive Checks" (e.g., famines, epidemics, earthquakes) or "Preventative Checks" (e.g., family planning, celibacy, late marriages). - Family Planning - Definition: - Spacing or preventing births to control population growth and improve women’s health. - Methods: - Awareness campaigns. - Free contraceptives. - Tax disincentives for large families (e.g., China’s former one-child policy tax). - Cases: - Sikkim's Incentives for Government Employees (2023) - Context: - Sikkim, India’s least populous state (~7 lakh), has the lowest Total Fertility Rate (TFR) of 1.1. - Policy: - From January 1, 2023, state government employees receive a bonus salary for having a second or third child to boost population growth. - Assam Two-Child Policy - Background: - Announced by Assam Government in 2017 to control population growth. - Came into effect from 1 January 2021. - Influenced by similar laws in other Indian states like Rajasthan, Madhya Pradesh. - Provisions: - Employment Restrictions: - Individuals with more than two children: - Ineligible for government jobs. - May be denied promotions in government services. - Panchayat Elections: - Under Assam Panchayat (Amendment) Act, 2018: - Individuals with more than two children barred from contesting panchayat elections. - Government Schemes: - People with more than two children may be disqualified from: - Benefiting from state-funded welfare schemes. - Receiving subsidies and housing assistance. - Exemptions: - Twins in second pregnancy not counted as violating the norm. - Policy not applied retroactively. - Certain tribal communities may be exempt (subject to notification). - Objectives: - Population control for better resource allocation. - Promote access to healthcare, education, employment. - Encourage small family norms. - Criticism: - Rights and Welfare Concerns: - May violate reproductive rights (Article 21). - Disproportionately affects women and marginalized groups. - Effectiveness: - Experts (e.g., UNFPA) argue coercive population policies are ineffective. - Emphasis should be on: - Women's education and empowerment. - Access to family planning services. - Demographic Indicators - Overview: - Demographic indicators provide statistical data about a population. - Help analyze population structure, health, growth trends, and social development. - Additional topics: - Demographic Dividend - Definition: - A period when the dependency ratio declines, and the working-age population (15-64) is proportionately larger, boosting economic productivity. - In economics and finance, a "dividend" refers to a return or reward received from an investment. - Analogy: - When a country has a high proportion of working-age people, it's like having a high-yield investment opportunity. - If the government and society invest well in education, health, skills, and jobs: - The large workforce becomes productive. - Generates higher economic output and tax revenues. - Leads to accelerated economic growth, just like receiving a "reward" (dividend) from the demographic investment. - Temporality: - Temporary in nature; the demographic dividend is not permanent. - The working-age population will eventually age and become dependent again. - Repercussions of Wasted Demographic Dividend: - Rising Unemployment: - If adequate jobs are not created, a large youth population remains idle. - Leads to economic stagnation and under-utilization of human capital. - Social Unrest: - Frustrated unemployed youth may turn to crime, extremism, or violence. - Increases law-and-order issues and weakens social fabric. - Demographic Burden: - When the large working-age cohort ages without retirement security: - Burdens healthcare, pensions, and social security systems. - Shrinking workforce must support a growing elderly population. - Missed Economic Growth: - Countries that fail to invest in education, skills, and infrastructure: - Miss the chance to boost productivity and GDP growth. - Fall into the "middle-income trap" or remain underdeveloped. - Comparative Loss: - Other nations (e.g., South Korea, China) that harnessed their demographic window: - Gained rapid economic advancement. - Countries like India risk falling behind if the window closes without capitalizing. - Silver Dividend - Definition: - Refers to the economic and social gains that can be derived from the ageing population — especially active and healthy senior citizens — who continue to contribute meaningfully to society. - Seen as a potential counterbalance to the challenges of population ageing (e.g., pension burden, healthcare costs). - Rationale: - With improvements in healthcare and longevity, many elderly remain physically fit, mentally alert, and professionally capable beyond retirement age. - Aging population is often seen as a liability, but with the right ecosystem, they can become an asset. - Contributions: - Economic: - Elderly professionals offering consulting, mentoring, part-time, or freelance services. - Entrepreneurship by retirees (second careers). - Reduced burden on pension systems if older citizens continue earning. - Social: - Participation in voluntary activities, local governance, and social welfare programs. - Transfer of values, experience, and institutional memory to younger generations. - Familial: - Grandparental caregiving reduces pressure on nuclear families. - Elderly often play key roles in child-rearing, especially in dual-income households. - Examples: - Japan: - Senior citizens work in retail, teaching, and small-scale enterprises. - Community-led initiatives for elderly engagement in rural areas. - India: - Government promoting voluntary retirement re-engagement programs. - NGOs involving seniors in educational and health awareness campaigns. - Some states offer incentives for employing elderly in low-risk sectors. - Policy Implications: - Need for: - Flexible retirement and pension policies. - Lifelong learning and reskilling programs for elderly. - Age-friendly infrastructure and digital literacy. - Promotion of health and wellness among seniors to ensure "active aging". - Significance: - Complements demographic dividend by ensuring intergenerational contribution. - Supports inclusive growth and social cohesion in an ageing society. - Helps mitigate negative effects of rising old-age dependency ratio. - It reflects India’s evolving policy focus on inclusive growth, aligning with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) and the vision of Viksit Bharat by 2047. - Three foundations: - Live a healthy and creative life (e.g., access to healthcare and cultural expression). - Be knowledgeable (e.g., universal education and skill development). - Achieve a decent standard of living (e.g., adequate income and housing). - Four pillars: - Equity (reducing disparities across gender, caste, and regions). - Sustainability (balancing development with environmental conservation). - Productivity (enhancing human capital for economic output). - Empowerment (fostering agency and participation in governance). - Four approaches (NCERT): -
Approach Description
Income Approach Oldest method; assumes higher income correlates with better human development, criticized for ignoring non-monetary well-being (e.g., India’s GDP growth vs. poverty persistence).
Welfare Approach Views people as passive beneficiaries, necessitating state intervention via schemes like Ayushman Bharat and Samagra Shiksha, prevalent in post-colonial welfare states.
Basic Needs Approach Proposed by the International Labour Organisation (ILO) in the 1970s post-oil crisis, emphasizes six needs: health, education, food, water supply, sanitation, housing, influencing India’s rural development programs.
Capability Approach Developed by Amartya Sen, focuses on expanding human capabilities (e.g., health, education, resource access) rather than mere outcomes, underpinning India’s focus on skill development and women’s empowerment.
