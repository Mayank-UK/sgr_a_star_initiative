- Pillar 2A1-i: Budget - Fiscal Policy - Definition: - Fiscal policy involves government decisions on taxation, expenditure, subsidies, and other financial operations to influence savings, investment, and consumption, achieving national goals like income redistribution, socio-economic welfare, economic development, and inclusive growth. - Etymology: - Derived from Greek “fiscus” (basket), symbolizing the public purse. - Significance: - Employment: - Funds welfare schemes (e.g., MGNREGA, Pillar#6) to create jobs. - Inflation Control: - Higher income taxes reduce disposable income, curbing demand; lower taxes during deflation boost demand (Pillar#4). - Economic Growth: - Tax benefits on savings (e.g., LIC, Mutual Funds) provide capital for industries, leading to factory expansion, jobs, and GDP growth (Pillar#4). - Inclusive Growth: - Higher taxes on the rich fund health, education, and poverty alleviation (Pillar#6). - Regional Balance: - Tax incentives for industries in backward areas (e.g., North East, Naxal-affected regions). - Exchange Rate Stability: - Tax benefits for exporters and higher import duties reduce Current Account Deficit (CAD), stabilizing INR:USD rates (Pillar#3). - Budget - Definition: - Annual financial statement detailing estimated revenues and expenditures for the next financial year, serving as the primary tool for implementing fiscal policy. - Constitutional Basis: - Article 112 mandates the Annual Financial Statement (AFS). - Funds - Three Constitutional Funds related to Budget - 1. Article 266: Consolidated Fund of India (CFI) - Contains: - All incoming tax revenues (both direct and indirect), - All money raised via borrowings (internal and external loans), - Recoveries of loans previously given by the government. - Withdrawal requires Parliamentary approval. - Exception: - Charged Expenditure (e.g. salaries of President, Judges, CAG) – automatic withdrawal without vote. - This is the largest and most important fund for the Government of India. - 2. Article 266: Public Account of India - Includes: - Transactions where the government acts as a banker or trustee. - Examples: - Provident Fund, Postal Savings, Small Savings, Income Tax Refunds, etc. - Since the money does not belong to the government, Parliamentary approval is not required for withdrawal. - However, if a new fund is being created (e.g., Central Road Fund via Act of 2000), Parliament’s approval is needed to create it. - Government only manages this account, transfers in and out as needed. - 3. Article 267: Contingency Fund of India - Purpose: - To meet unforeseen and urgent expenditure (natural disasters, emergencies, pandemics, etc.). - Held by: - Finance Secretary (Dept of Economic Affairs) on behalf of the President. - Process: - Government spends first → then takes ex post-facto approval from Parliament. - The amount is later refilled from the Consolidated Fund of India (CFI). - Budget 2021 Reforms: - Corpus raised from ₹500 crore to ₹30,000 crore. - Distribution: - 40% kept with Department of Expenditure, - 60% with Department of Economic Affairs (DEA). - Non-budgetary / Extra-budgetary / Donation funds - Definition - Funds that operate outside the Consolidated Fund of India. - Not voted or discussed in Parliament; hence, not part of the Union Budget. - Typically formed as public charitable trusts under the Indian Trusts Act, 1882. - Mainly used for emergency relief, public welfare, or disaster assistance. - Financed via voluntary donations from individuals, corporates, and institutions. - No direct budgetary allocation or disbursement from government expenditure. - Some enjoy income tax exemption benefits and CSR eligibility. - Features - Operate outside Government accounts like Consolidated/Contingency Fund. - Administered directly by the PM or designated committees. - Not audited by the CAG by default unless voluntarily submitted. - Transparent reporting may be voluntary or court-directed, not mandatory. - No parliamentary oversight unless judicially compelled. - Examples - Prime Minister’s National Relief Fund (PMNRF) - Set up in 1948 by PM Nehru, initially to aid Partition refugees. - Now used for assistance to victims of: - Natural disasters (floods, earthquakes) - Accidents - Ac‌id attacks - Major illnesses (e.g., cancer, kidney failure) - Accepts individual and corporate donations. - Donations eligible for 100% Income Tax deduction under Section 80G. - Does not receive funds from Consolidated Fund of India. - PM CARES Fund - Prime Minister’s Citizen Assistance and Relief in Emergency Situations - Set up in 2020 to deal with COVID-19 pandemic. - For disaster management and research funding (e.g., ventilators, vaccines). - Donations: - 100% tax-deductible under Section 80G. - Eligible under CSR (Corporate Social Responsibility) norms. - Audit: - Not audited by CAG; audited by private Chartered Accountants appointed by trustees. - Criticism: - Lack of transparency. - Overlap with existing funds like PMNRF. - Not subject to RTI as per PMO’s response. - CM Relief Funds - State-level counterparts to PMNRF. - e.g., Maharashtra CM’s Relief Fund, Tamil Nadu CM Relief Fund - Accept public donations for natural calamities, accidents, medical emergencies. - Eligibility for tax deduction and CSR support varies by state and notification. - Notes - These funds are not extra-budgetary resources (EBRs) used for capital expenditure. - Should not be confused with statutory non-lapsable funds like NDRF (which are part of Public Account of India). - Courts (e.g., SC in 2020) have recognized PM CARES as a separate public charitable trust distinct from state accountability mechanisms. - A separate National Defence Fund (created in 1962) also exists under PM’s office for welfare of armed forces and paramilitary forces. - Both PMNRF and PM CARES are considered private charitable trusts under Indian Trust Act, 1882, but there is debate over whether they should be treated as public accounts for transparency. - RTI Controversy: - PM CARES is not currently subject to Right to Information (RTI) disclosures; this has led to criticism and ongoing legal debate regarding public accountability. - Three Documents Related to Budget (Constitutional and Legal Basis) - Definition: - The Indian Constitution mandates the Union Government to present a set of financial documents before Parliament every financial year. These are not just formalities but are required for legal authorization of revenue collection and expenditure. - Origin: - The term "Budget" comes from the French word *bougette*, meaning a leather bag or small pouch. - Traditionally, the Finance Minister would carry the budget documents in a suitcase. - In 2019, Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman replaced the suitcase with a traditional red cloth (called 'Bahi-Khata') to shed colonial symbolism. - Key Budget-Related Documents: - 1. Annual Financial Statement (AFS) – Article 112 - Constitutionally mandated budget document. - Contains: - Actual receipts and expenditures of the previous financial year. - Estimated receipts and expenditures for the current financial year. - Revenue expenditure must be shown separately from other expenditures (like capital expenditure). - There is no requirement to: - Present a separate Railway Budget. - Show plan vs. non-plan expenditure (a practice now obsolete). - 2. Finance Bill – Articles 110 & 117 - Required to give legal effect to the government’s taxation proposals. - Parliament's approval is necessary to impose, modify, or remove taxes. - Parliament can: - Reduce or abolish a proposed tax. - Parliament cannot: - Increase a tax beyond what is proposed in the Finance Bill. - E.g., If government proposes 35% import duty on shoes, MPs can vote to reduce or retain it, not raise it to 45%. - Finance Bill is a Money Bill: - Passed only by Lok Sabha. - Rajya Sabha can only suggest changes within 14 days—Lok Sabha is not bound to accept them. - 3. Appropriation Bill – Article 114 - Authorizes the government to withdraw funds from the Consolidated Fund of India (CFI). - Covers all government spending. - Two types of expenditures: - a. Charged Expenditures (non-votable): - Automatically approved without voting. - E.g., salaries of Supreme Court Judges, CAG, President. - b. Votable Expenditures: - Government schemes, departmental spending, etc. - Subject to discussion and vote in Lok Sabha. - Appropriation Bill is also a Money Bill (Art. 110), so same Rajya Sabha limitations apply. - Special Notes: - Sometimes, to bypass Rajya Sabha, the ruling party may include non-financial provisions (like regulatory changes) inside the Finance Bill. - Whether a Bill is classified as a Money Bill is solely decided by the Lok Sabha Speaker. - [Art. 110(3)] — Speaker's decision is final. - [Art. 122] — Courts cannot question this decision. - Six Stages of Passing the Budget in Parliament - Stages: - 1. Presentation of Budget. - 2. General Discussion. - 3. Scrutiny by Departmental Committees. - 4. Voting on Demands for Grants, Cut Motions, Guillotine. - 5. Passing of Appropriation Bill. - 6. Passing of Finance Bill. - Reference: - Self-study from Indian Polity by M. Laxmikanth (Parliament chapter). - Financial Year (FY) - The concept of Financial Year in India dates back to 1867 when the British Indian Government adopted the 1st April to 31st March cycle to align with the financial year followed in Britain. - Although the Constitution of India does not prescribe any specific dates or months for the Financial Year, we have continued the colonial-era legacy of April–March. - In 2016-17, the Ministry of Finance constituted the Shankar Acharya Committee to evaluate whether India should shift the financial year to alternatives like: - January to December (calendar year), or - Cropping seasons such as Rabi–Kharif cycle, with the goal of improving estimation and management of tax revenues and expenditures. - In 2017, the proposal for change was dropped because most states opposed it. The change would have required major overhauls in accounting systems, reporting software, training, and budget cycles — and the anticipated benefits were not substantial enough to justify this transition. - In 2020, during the COVID-19 pandemic, there were widespread rumors that the Government had shifted the financial year to a different period — however, these were confirmed to be false. The financial year remains unchanged: 1st April to 31st March. - Vote on Account - Definition: - A temporary legislative authorization for the government to withdraw money from the Consolidated Fund of India (CFI) to meet essential expenses until the full Budget is passed. - Required when the Appropriation Bill for the new financial year is not enacted before 31st March. - Background: - The Constitution does not fix a mandatory date for presenting the Union Budget. - Before 2017, the Budget was presented on the last working day of February. - But the entire Budget process (6 stages) took until May to complete. - Meanwhile, the financial year would end on 31st March. - After 31st March, the previous year’s Appropriation Act expires. - Hence, government would not have legal authority to withdraw funds, even for routine expenses like salaries, pensions, electricity bills, etc. - Purpose: - To avoid a financial vacuum or shutdown of routine government operations. - Parliament (practically Lok Sabha) grants temporary permission through a Vote on Account to withdraw funds from the CFI. - It typically authorizes spending for two months, usually one-sixth of the estimated annual expenditure. - Duration and Limit: - Usually valid for two months. - Amount approved is generally around one-sixth of the total budgeted expenditure. - Can be extended in case of extraordinary situations like elections or emergencies. - Not a substitute for the full budget: - Vote on Account only allows withdrawal for essential services. - It does not include provisions for major policy changes or new schemes. - Post-2017 Developments: - From 2017 onwards, the government began presenting the Budget on the 1st working day of February. - This ensured that the entire process — including Appropriation Bill — is completed before 31st March. - Hence, Vote on Account became unnecessary in regular years. - Exceptions: - In 2019 and 2024, interim budgets were presented before general elections. - Government sought a Vote on Account instead of full budget, as the new government post-election might want to make significant changes or present a fresh budget. - This also gave the incumbent government flexibility to introduce a large Appropriation Bill if re-elected. - FAQ / Counterpoints: - Why did Modi Govt use Vote on Account in 2019 and 2024 even after early Budget timing reform? - No detailed justification was publicly given. - No PILs were filed in Supreme Court challenging this. - Neither NITI Aayog nor Economic Survey commented on it. - Columnists and experts also did not raise major issues. - Practically, it was seen as a politically strategic and legally permissible move. - Interim Budget - Our Constitution does not define or mandate the concept of an Interim Budget. - However, during an election year or in extreme situations (e.g. when a coalition government collapses prematurely), it is considered unethical for the outgoing government to make drastic or populist changes in the budget, like announcing "free gold for BPL girls' marriages". - Hence, while such governments still present a budget in the regular format (3 documents: Annual Financial Statement, Finance Bill, Appropriation Bill; and undergo 6 stages of passing), they generally avoid grand populist announcements. - Such budgets are called Interim Budgets. - Examples of Interim Budgets: - 2004 - Yashwant Sinha - 2009 - Pranab Mukherjee - 2014 - P. Chidambaram - 2019 - Piyush Goyal - 2024 - Nirmala Sitharaman - Just like a regular General Budget, an Interim Budget is also valid for the whole financial year. - However, if a new government is formed in between, it may present another (full/general) budget to change provisions. - Example: - In February 2014, FM Chidambaram presented the Interim Budget in the 15th Lok Sabha. After the elections, the BJP-led government presented the full General Budget in July 2014 (16th Lok Sabha) via FM Arun Jaitley. - Similarly, in both 2019 and 2024, Interim Budgets were presented in February, followed by full budgets in July after elections. - Note: - For easier revision and visualization, the term "Full Budget" is used here to distinguish it from "Interim Budget". But in UPSC Mains, use the correct term: General Budget. - Budget Theme - Although the Constitution doesn’t require it, sometimes Finance Ministers include a specific "theme" in their Budget speech to create media attention. -

- Burden of Indirect Tax – Forward vs. Backward Shifting - Definition: - The burden of indirect taxes can shift to consumers (forward) or suppliers/producers (backward), depending on market dynamics. - Forward Shifting: - Sellers increase product prices to maintain profit margins (e.g., Vadilal raises ice cream prices after GST increase from 18% to 28%). - Backward Shifting: - Sellers absorb tax increases by cutting costs (e.g., Vadilal reduces worker salaries to maintain ice cream prices and market share). - Factors Influencing Shifting: - Determined by supply and demand elasticity (e.g., forward shifting is common for essential medicines due to inelastic demand). - Merits and Demerits - Merits: - Convenient to collect as traders act as unpaid tax collectors, ensuring a wide tax base (e.g., 18% GST on biscuits). - Elastic, generating significant revenue from small rate increases, impacting all consumers. - Reduces harmful consumption through high taxes on sin goods (e.g., tobacco, alcohol, soft drinks). - Demerits: - Regressive, disproportionately affecting the poor, who pay a higher income share (e.g., same GST rate on biscuits for all income groups). - Hidden in product prices, reducing civic awareness of tax contributions. - Increases product costs, creating demand uncertainty and variable government revenue. - High risk of corruption and tax​ eva‌sion without input tax credit, leading to cascading effects. -

Definition: - The Finance Commission is a constitutional and quasi-judicial body established under Article 280 of the Indian Constitution. - It is constituted by the President of India every five years (or earlier) to ensure fiscal federalism by recommending: - The distribution of net proceeds of taxes between the Union and State governments. - The principles governing grants-in-aid to the states from the Consolidated Fund of India. - Measures to augment the Consolidated Fund of a State to supplement the resources of Panchayats and Municipalities. - Its role is to address imbalances in taxation and expenditure responsibilities between the Centre and States, thus promoting equitable financial governance. - Context: - Fiscal federalism involves dividing taxation and expenditure responsibilities across government levels (Union, States, Local Bodies). States have significant expenditure responsibilities under the 7th Schedule but limited taxation powers, making the Finance Commission critical for equitable revenue sharing. - Significance for India: - The Finance Commission promotes fiscal equity, supports state development, and ensures financial stability by recommending tax devolution, grants, and fiscal discipline measures, aligning with India’s goals of cooperative federalism and balanced regional growth. - Structure: - Comprises a Chairman and four members, appointed by the President, eligible for re-appointment. Recommendations are advisory but rarely rejected by the government. - Functions: - Recommends vertical tax devolution (Union to States) and horizontal distribution (among States). - Suggests grants-in-aid to States for revenue deficits, local bodies, and disaster management. - Advises on augmenting State Consolidated Funds for Panchayats and Municipalities. - Addresses additional matters specified in Terms of Reference (ToR) by the President, such as fiscal discipline or specific state needs. - 15th vs. 16th Finance Commission - Membership: -

- Pillar 2B2: Black money & Allied Issues - Definitions and concepts: - Black money: - Black money refers to income or transactions that are taxable but deliberately concealed from tax authorities to evade taxes. It operates within a parallel economy, undermining fiscal integrity and reducing government revenue for welfare and development. Unlike legal tax planning (e.g., investing in LIC or PPF for deductions), which is ethical and lawful, black money involves illegal practices like tax​ eva‌sion or money​ laund‌ering. - Tax Planning / Tax Mitigation: - Legally reducing tax liability using provisions like LIC, PPF, pension funds, etc.; it is legal and ethical. - tax​ eva‌sion: - Illegally hiding income or transactions to avoid tax (e.g., not declaring rental income); punishable under law. - Tax Avoidance: - Legally disclosing income but exploiting loopholes to reduce tax (e.g., profit shifting via sh‌ell comp‌anies); legal but often unethical. - Tax Haven: - Countries with low/zero tax and secrecy laws (e.g., Panama, Mauritius, Cayman Islands) that attract foreign individuals or firms seeking to avoid/evade taxes. - Money​ laund‌ering: - Definition: - The process of converting “dirty money” (illegally earned) into “clean money” (legally usable). - Involves disguising the origin, ownership, or destination of money obtained through criminal activities. - money​ laund‌ering generally presumes criminal origin, especially under Indian and international laws. - Stages: - Placement: - Introducing illegal money into the financial system (e.g., via casinos, deposits). - Layering: - Complex transactions to obscure the money trail (e.g., sh‌ell comp‌anies). - Integration: - Reintroducing laundered money into the economy as clean (e.g., real estate, luxury purchases). - Tools: - Real estate, sh‌ell comp‌anies, ha‌wala, foreign accounts, gold. - Ha‌wala: - An informal and illegal system of money transfer that operates outside conventional banking or financial channels. - Money is transferred without physical movement; instead, it relies on a network of brokers or agents (known as ha‌waladars). - Context: - Black money distorts economic equity, fuels corruption, and hampers India’s ability to fund public services. India has implemented stringent laws, international agreements, and digital reforms to combat black money, aligning with global efforts to enhance financial transparency. - Significance for India: - Tackling black money is crucial for increasing tax compliance, boosting revenue for infrastructure and social programs, and strengthening India’s global financial standing. It supports economic stability and aligns with initiatives like Atmanirbhar Bharat by fostering a transparent, inclusive economy. - Notable Organisations - Definition: - Several specialized agencies in India and globally work to detect, prevent, and penalize activities related to black money, tax​ eva‌sion, and money​ laund‌ering, ensuring compliance with financial regulations. - Key Organisations: -

- Foreign Borrowing in Foreign Currency - Introduction: - In Budget 2019, Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman announced that India’s sovereign external debt-to-GDP ratio is low (~5%), allowing the government to raise part of its borrowing from external markets in foreign currencies (e.g., USD, Euro). - Arguments in Favor: - Reduces Crowding Out: - Domestic borrowing reduces funds available for private corporates. External borrowing frees up domestic capital for private sector growth, enabling factory expansion, job creation, and GDP growth. - Lower Interest Rates: - Advanced economies (e.g., USA, EU) offer lower interest rates, making foreign loans cheaper than domestic ones. - Manageable Debt Levels: - India’s low external debt ratio suggests that moderate foreign borrowing is unlikely to cause significant fiscal strain. - Arguments Against: - Exchange Rate Risk: - A weakening rupee (e.g., $1 = Rs. 60 to Rs. 70) increases the repayment burden in rupees, making loans more expensive than anticipated. - Risk of Over-Borrowing: - Future governments may excessively borrow abroad to fund populist schemes, risking a debt crisis if the rupee depreciates significantly. - Alternative Approach: - Increasing foreign investment limits in Government Securities (G-Secs) in INR attracts foreign capital without currency risk, preferable to borrowing in foreign currency. - Conclusion: - While foreign borrowing can reduce domestic crowding out and leverage low global interest rates, the exchange rate risk and potential for over-borrowing outweigh benefits. Economists like Raghuram Rajan advocate caution, suggesting more deliberation before expanding foreign currency borrowing. -

- Ricardian Equivalence: - Theory: - High fiscal deficit → Government borrows more → At maturity (G-Sec redemption), government must repay principal + interest → This may lead to future tax hikes. - Anticipating this, rational citizens may increase savings and reduce consumption → Lower demand → Inventory buildup → Slows economic growth. - Economist David Ricardo proposed this theory: called Ricardian Equivalence Proposition (REP). - Invalid for India (Economic Survey 2021): - REP assumes people are rational, forward-looking, and understand future liabilities → Not always true in India. - Reasons for invalidity: - Citizens are not perfectly rational; decisions often influenced by emotional/personal needs (e.g., weddings, iPhones, foreign trips). - High prevalence of tax​ eva‌sion, black money, and a parallel economy → Not all citizens bear the tax burden equally. - Large informal sector and financial illiteracy weaken the theoretical assumptions of REP in the Indian context. - Crowding Out Effect: - Concept: - When the government borrows heavily from households and financial institutions (LIC, EPFO, banks via SLR), less money is left for private corporate borrowers → Crowds out private investment → Slows factory expansion and job creation. - Financial Repression: - Forcing public institutions (e.g., SBI, LIC, EPFO) to buy G-Secs using depositors’ money → deprives households of optimal returns. - Example: Govt forced NABARD to buy ₹15,000 crore Swachh Bharat Mission (Gramin) bonds (10-year maturity). - Govt pressures RBI and others to pay higher dividends → reduces their operational freedom. - ES21 Observations (1950s–1990s): - Found some evidence of crowding out due to: - Very high CRR and SLR → PSBs had limited loanable funds. - Absence of large private banks. - Low earnings and savings among public. - Underdeveloped capital markets. - ES21 Findings (Post-1990): - No evidence of crowding out (1990–2019) due to structural changes: - Economic growth ↑ → household income ↑ → savings ↑ → more money available to both govt and private borrowers. - Rise in banking habits, NBFCs, mutual funds, and capital market participation. - Demographic dividend → more working population → improved financial behavior. - Implication: - If govt borrowing is for productive infrastructure (health, education, transport, etc.), it raises GDP and incomes → boosts overall savings → the 'size of the dish' increases, so both govt and private sectors get enough funds. - Thus, "crowding out" is less likely when savings are not fixed but expand with growth. - Monetization of Deficit: - When central bank prints more money to pay for government's loans → Monetizing the Deficit. - It can result in hyperinflation and ↓↓ purchasing power of currency (if supply of goods like onion, tomato is not increased sufficiently). In ancient economies, rulers invested in infrastructure like roads, canals, and public buildings during famines to create jobs. - In modern macroeconomics, this principle is institutionalized as countercyclical fiscal policy. - Definition: - A countercyclical fiscal policy involves increasing public expenditure and/or reducing taxes during economic slowdowns, and reducing spending and/or increasing taxes during economic booms. - The objective is to smoothen economic fluctuations, support aggregate demand, and maintain macroeconomic stability. - How it Works: - During Economic Slowdown (Recessionary Phase): - Government increases capital and revenue expenditure. - Objectives: - Stimulate demand through infrastructure projects. - Generate employment and raise household income. - Boost private sector confidence and investment. - Effect: Crowding-In - Increased government investment leads to complementary private sector activity. - Example: - Government constructs national highways → private sector builds logistics parks, fuel stations, hotels → more jobs and tax revenue. - During Economic Boom (Expansionary Phase): - Government reduces expenditure and increases savings. - Objectives: - Control inflationary pressures. - Accumulate fiscal buffers (surpluses) to deploy during future downturns. - Avoid overheating of the economy. - Overheating of an economy refers to a situation where economic growth is unsustainably high, leading to inflationary pressures, resource shortages, and imbalances. - Strategic Benefits: - Ensures long-term fiscal sustainability. - Enhances economic resilience to external shocks (e.g., global financial crises, pandemics). - Improves sovereign credit ratings and investor confidence. - Pro-cyclical Fiscal Policy: - Opposite of countercyclical approach. - Government increases expenditure during booms and cuts spending during slowdowns. - Consequences: - Amplifies economic volatility. - Worsens inflation during booms and unemployment during recessions. - Example: - India’s historical fiscal stance has often been pro-cyclical, as highlighted by Economic Surveys. - Real-World Examples: - Chile: - Uses a structural fiscal rule based on long-term copper prices to avoid pro-cyclicality. - United States: - Large fiscal stimulus packages during the 2008 Global Financial Crisis and COVID-19 pandemic. - India: - National Infrastructure Pipeline (NIP) and PM Gati Shakti are attempts to institutionalize countercyclical investment. - Key Terminology: - Crowding-In: - When public investment encourages private sector investment. - Fiscal Multipliers: - The ratio of change in output to the change in government spending. - IRGD (Interest Rate – Growth Rate Differential) and Debt Sustainability - Concept: - IRGD measures the difference between the interest rate on government borrowing and the nominal GDP growth rate. - Indicator of whether public debt is sustainable over the medium to long term. - Formula: - Version 1: IRGD = Interest Rate – GDP Growth Rate - If IRGD is negative (e.g., 6% – 9% = –3%), it indicates strong debt sustainability. - Version 2: IRGD = GDP Growth Rate – Interest Rate - If IRGD is positive (e.g., 9% – 6% = +3%), it implies faster growth than borrowing cost → sustainable. -

- Domar Condition of Debt Sustainability - Concept Origin: - Proposed by economist Evsey Domar in 1944. - Provides a theoretical foundation for assessing long-term debt sustainability. - Domar's Rule: - If the growth rate of nominal GDP is higher than the interest rate on public debt, then public debt as a percentage of GDP will decline over time. - Formula: - If g > r → debt-to-GDP ratio stabilizes or declines. - Where g = GDP growth rate, r = interest rate on debt. - Implications: - Countries with high GDP growth can afford higher deficits without increasing their debt burden. - Countries with low growth need to either raise taxes or cut spending to maintain fiscal stability. - Indian Scenario: - India’s nominal GDP growth post-COVID recovery has remained higher than the average cost of borrowing. - Indicates that fiscal expansion (within limits) can be sustainable if accompanied by growth-enhancing reforms. - Automatic Stabilizers - Definition: - Mechanisms in the government budget that automatically increase spending or decrease tax liability during economic slowdowns without requiring changes in tax laws or parliamentary approval. - Examples: - Income tax, corporation tax, unemployment allowance, food subsidies, and government schemes targeting the poor/lower-middle class. - Note: - Countercyclical fiscal policy/fiscal stimulus requires new budgets/laws/parliamentary approval, making them discretionary stabilizers, not automatic. - Disposable Income & Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC): - Disposable Income: - Personal income minus personal income taxes; available for consumption and saving. - MPC: - Fraction of additional disposable income spent on consumption. - Tax Multiplier: - Negative multiplier: - Increase in taxes reduces disposable income, decreasing consumption and GDP. - Autonomous Expenditure Multiplier (AEM) & Government Expenditure Multiplier: -

- General Budget vs. Railway Budget - Historical Context: - 1920–21: - Acworth Committee recommended a separate Railway Budget due to railways’ significant revenue contribution during British rule. - 1924: - Separation Convention formalized this practice. - Post-independence, the Railway Budget was presented before the General Budget. - Merger (2017): - Modi government merged the Railway Budget with the General Budget, based on NITI Aayog’s Bibek Debroy Committee recommendations: - No constitutional requirement for a separate budget. - Populist measures (e.g., low fares) during coalition governments eroded railway profitability. - Railway revenue became a small fraction of the General Budget, not justifying separate presentation. - Impact: - Streamlined budgeting, reduced populism, and aligned railway finances with national fiscal goals. - Plan vs. Non-Plan Expenditure Budget - Definition: - Plan Expenditure: - Linked to Five-Year Plans (e.g., development projects). - Non-Plan Expenditure: - Routine expenses (e.g., salaries, maintenance). - Discontinuation (Budget 2017): - Modi government ended the distinction due to: - No constitutional mandate. - Dissolution of the Planning Commission (2014–15). - End of the 12th Five-Year Plan (2012–17). - Impact: - Simplified budget presentation, focusing on revenue and capital classifications. - Budgeting Methods - Traditional/Line-Item Budgeting: - Focuses on listing income and expenditure without evaluating benefits (e.g., Rs. 10,000 for hospital beds). - Lacks performance measurement. - Performance Budgeting: - Ties expenditure to measurable outcomes (e.g., Rs. 50,000 for computers to achieve 30% faster RTI processing). - Enhances cost-benefit analysis and efficiency. - Zero-Based Budgeting (ZBB): - Requires departments to justify all expenditure from scratch, not incrementally (e.g., reviewing education schemes’ effectiveness before reallocation). - Prevents automatic budget increases. - Sunset Budgeting: - Schemes have predefined deadlines (e.g., a subsidy for two years), ensuring automatic termination unless renewed. -
