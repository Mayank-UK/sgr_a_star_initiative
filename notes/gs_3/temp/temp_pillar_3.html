- Pillar 3A: Balance of Payment (BoP) - Definition and overview: - Definition: - The Balance of Payments (BoP) is a systematic record of all economic transactions between residents of a country and the rest of the world over a specific period, typically a year. - It reflects the inflow and outflow of money, influencing exchange rates and economic stability. - Currency exchange involves the conversion of one currency into another, determined by exchange rates, which are critical for international trade and financial stability. - Context: - BoP and currency exchange are pivotal for India’s economic policy, affecting trade, investment, and monetary stability. - Managed by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), these elements ensure India can meet external obligations, stabilize the rupee, and foster economic growth. - Compiled by: - Reserve Bank of India (RBI), following IMF’s Balance of Payments Manual-6 (BPM6). - Reporting Currency: - US Dollars (USD) - Why US Dollar? - USD is the dominant international reserve currency. - Most global trade (especially oil, gold, etc.) is invoiced in USD. - Using a common benchmark ensures global comparability and consistency. - Principle: - Globally, BoP is always balanced (double-entry system) - one country's debit is another's credit. - Significance: - Indicates a country's economic position vis-à-vis the rest of the world. - Persistent BoP deficit can impact exchange rate, reserves, and investor confidence. - A stable BoP supports India’s economic resilience, enhances global creditworthiness, and mitigates external shocks (e.g., oil price volatility, global financial crises). - Robust foreign exchange reserves and remittances strengthen India’s position in the global economy. - India BoP Example (2018–19): - Current Account Deficit: - Amount: - -$57 billion - Meaning: - India imported more goods/services/income than it exported. - Indicates a net outflow of $57 billion from the country. - Capital Account Surplus: - Amount: - +$54 billion - Meaning: - India received net inflows of capital through: - FDI (Foreign Direct Investment) - FPI (Foreign Portfolio Investment) - ECBs (External Commercial Borrowings) - NRI deposits and other capital receipts - Indicates $54 billion came into the country. - Balanced using: - Drawdown of forex reserves - Amount covered: - $3 billion (approx.) - Nature: - Accommodating transaction - Meaning: - RBI used part of its foreign currency reserves to finance the remaining BoP gap. - Types of Transactions: - Autonomous Transactions (also called "Above the Line"): - Definition: - Transactions done for economic considerations or commercial motives. - These occur regardless of BoP condition. - Examples: - Exports, imports, FDI inflow/outflow, remittances, tourism spending, etc. - Impact: - These affect the BoP position directly, causing surplus or deficit. - Accommodating Transactions (also called "Below the Line"): - Definition: - Transactions done specifically to bridge the BoP deficit/surplus caused by autonomous flows. - Managed by: - Primarily the Central Bank (RBI in India). - Examples: - RBI selling forex reserves to bridge a current account deficit. - Borrowing from IMF to manage BoP crisis. - Purpose: - To ensure BoP always balances as per accounting rule. - RBI Classification of BoP (IMF BPM6-Aligned): - Current Account: - Goods (BoT - Balance of Trade): - Export and import of physical goods. - Services: - Software exports, tourism, shipping, consulting, etc. - Primary Income: - Wages, interest, dividends received from abroad. - Secondary Income: - Remittances from NRIs, grants, donations. - Capital & Financial Account: - FDI (Foreign Direct Investment): - Long-term investment involving management/ownership control (≥10% equity stake). - FPI (Foreign Portfolio Investment): - Investment in financial assets (stocks, bonds) without management control (<10% equity). - ECBs (External Commercial Borrowings): - Loans from foreign lenders to Indian corporates or government. - NRI Deposits: - Non-Resident Indian savings in Indian banks: - FCNR (Foreign Currency Non-Resident) - NRE (Non-Resident External) - NRO (Non-Resident Ordinary) - Reserve Assets: - Foreign exchange reserves managed by RBI (includes gold, SDRs, foreign securities). - Errors and Omissions: - Balancing entry to account for discrepancies due to data gaps, lags, or unreported flows. -

- Circular Flow of Income & Import/Export: - Exports: - Inject foreign income into the economy, increasing household income, business revenues, and government tax collections (e.g., $208B service exports in 2018-19 boosted IT sector wages). - Imports: - Represent leakages, as domestic income is spent abroad (e.g., $510B goods imports in 2018-19 reduced domestic liquidity). - Impact: - Trade balances affect GDP, employment, and inflation. - HELP Policy: - Hydrocarbon Exploration and Licensing Policy (2016) replaced NELP. - Features: - Single Uniform License: - Applicable to all types of hydrocarbons including oil, natural gas, shale gas, and coal bed methane (CBM). - Simplifies the regulatory framework and reduces delays. - Revenue-Sharing Model: - The government receives a fixed percentage of gross revenue from the contractor. - Replaces the earlier cost-recovery model under NELP. - Enhances transparency, reduces disputes, and ensures predictable revenue for the government. - Market and Pricing Freedom: - No control on pricing or marketing of crude oil and natural gas. - Promotes competition and enhances ease of doing business. - Open Acreage Licensing Policy (OLAP): - Companies are allowed to choose exploration blocks based on their own assessment. - No need to wait for formal bidding rounds. - Over 50 blocks awarded under this provision by 2023. - Relaxed Offshore Norms: - Reduced number of environmental and regulatory clearances for offshore exploration activities. - Encourages offshore investment and faster project implementation. - Impact: - Increased private sector participation in upstream exploration. - For example, Vedanta’s operations in Rajasthan. - Boost to domestic energy production and reduction in import dependency. - India’s Strategic Oil Reserves: - Objective: - Ensure energy security during crises (war, supply cuts). - Locations: - Visakhapatnam (1.33M tonnes), Chandikhol (1M tonnes), Padur (2.5M tonnes), Mangalore (1.5M tonnes). - Storage: - Underground rock caverns (cost-effective, secure). - Operator: - Indian Strategic Petroleum Reserves Ltd (ISPRL), under Ministry of Petroleum. - Capacity: - 5.33M tonnes (15 days of India’s oil needs, 2024). - Leasing Out: - Feb 2024, ISPRL leased 1M tonnes of vacant storage to private firms (e.g., ADNOC) due to funding constraints, generating revenue. -

- Sterilization - Definition: - Sterilization is the process by which the RBI neutralizes the impact of foreign exchange interventions on the domestic money supply to prevent inflation or deflation. - Mechanism: - When RBI sells dollars to stabilize the rupee, it injects rupees into the market, increasing money supply and risking inflation. To counter this, RBI conducts Open Market Operations (OMO), selling government securities to absorb excess rupees. - Step-by-step: - Suppose rupee is depreciating sharply (e.g., ₹85 per USD). - To stabilize the rupee, RBI sells US dollars from its forex reserves in the open market (to banks, importers, etc.). - How Rupee Weakening is Stabilized by Selling Dollars? - When rupee weakens (e.g., ₹85/$ from ₹82/$), it means: - High demand for dollars, or - Low supply of dollars, or - Low demand for rupees. - Causes: - oil import bills, FPI outflow, global dollar strengthening, etc. - These market participants pay rupees to RBI in exchange for USD. - Hence, RBI gives dollars and receives rupees from buyers. - But when RBI gives dollars to the banks, those dollars become bank assets, and the equivalent rupee amount is credited back into the banking system as liquidity. - In effect, the overall rupee liquidity in the system rises. - Why Liquidity Rises: - The commercial bank now has more dollar assets it can use to settle forex deals or lend to importers. - RBI’s forex reserve (USD) is reduced, and rupees are effectively injected into the system to finance these deals. - Importers who need USD get them → pay rupees to banks → banks now have more rupees circulating. - Why this is considered "injection" of rupees: - Though RBI receives rupees, they are not retired or removed. - The transaction increases the supply of rupees in circulation because: - The buyer of USD (say, a bank) now has dollars and has passed rupees into circulation (say, paid on behalf of an importer). - This stimulates money supply and may cause inflationary pressure if not sterilized. - Example: - In 2018-19, RBI sold $3 billion to balance BoP, requiring sterilization to manage rupee liquidity. - Significance: - Prevents inflationary pressure from excess rupee circulation, ensuring monetary stability while maintaining exchange rate targets. - Mundell-Fleming Trilemma - Definition: - The Mundell-Fleming Trilemma, proposed by economists Robert Mundell and Marcus Fleming in the 1960s, states that a country cannot simultaneously achieve a fixed exchange rate, free capital mobility, and an independent monetary policy. - RBI’s Currency Swaps/FX-Swap - Definition: - Currency Swap: - An agreement between two parties (e.g., central banks or financial institutions) to exchange principal and interest payments in one currency for equivalent amounts in another currency, typically to manage liquidity, stabilize exchange rates, or access foreign currency during crises. - FX-Swap: - A short-term transaction involving the exchange of two currencies with an agreement to reverse the exchange at a future date, often used to manage foreign exchange liquidity. - Currency Swap Agreement with Japan: - Agreement: - India (RBI) and Japan (Bank of Japan) signed a $75 billion bilateral currency swap agreement. - Mechanism: - Either central bank can access up to $75 billion from the other’s foreign exchange (forex) reserves, primarily in USD, during economic crises. - Purpose: - Stabilizes currency (INR or JPY) by providing access to foreign currency to meet balance of payments needs or prevent depreciation. - Example: - If INR weakens significantly, RBI can use Japan’s USD reserves to intervene in forex markets, buying INR to stabilize its value. - RBI Currency Swap with Indian Bankers: - Event (March 2022): - RBI conducted a $5 billion dollar-rupee swap with Indian banks and Non-Banking Financial Companies (NBFCs). - Objective: - Increase USD supply in the Indian market to prevent INR depreciation, especially during global economic stress (e.g., post-US Fed Tapering). - Mechanism: - RBI sells USD to banks/NBFCs in exchange for INR, with an agreement to reverse the transaction later, injecting dollars into the system. - FAQ: - Does this affect money supply, inflation, or loan interest rates? - The swap is a targeted liquidity tool, not a broad money supply change. Avoid overcomplicating the analysis. - Measures to Attract Dollars and Prevent INR Depreciation (Jul-Aug 2022): - Exemptions for Foreign Deposits: - Action: - In July 2022, amid rising external pressures, the RBI exempted incremental foreign currency non-resident (FCNR) and non-resident external (NRE) deposits from the Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) and Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR) requirements, effective till November 4, 2022. - Normally, even foreign currency deposits (though held in USD or other currencies) are counted in a bank’s Net Demand and Time Liabilities (NDTL), increasing the need to maintain CRR and SLR in rupees.
