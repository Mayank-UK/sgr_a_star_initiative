- Pillar 5A1: Infrastructure - Mining and Basic Industries - Definition: - Infrastructure refers to the set of basic facilities that enable an economy to function and grow, including energy, irrigation, roads, railways, and telecommunications. - Characteristics of Infrastructure: - Natural Monopoly: - Infrastructure sectors often display natural monopoly features due to high fixed costs and low marginal costs, economies of scale making a single provider most efficient. - What are fixed and marginal costs? - Fixed Cost: - Definition: - Cost that does not change with the level of output. - Examples: - Rent for a factory - Salaries of permanent staff - Cost of installing railway tracks, airport runways, power grids - Marginal Cost: - Definition: - The cost of producing one additional unit of output. - Examples: - Fuel cost for running one extra train - Cost of producing one more unit of electricity - Examples: - Railways (track laying, signaling). - Aviation (pre-LPG era dominated by Indian Airlines). - Telecom (before liberalization, monopolized by BSNL/MTNL). - High Sunk Costs: - Capital-intensive investments with low or zero resale value. - Once invested, funds cannot be recovered if project is abandoned. - Examples: - Airport runways, ports, tunnels, power transmission lines. - Implication: - Leads to entry barriers; requires long-term commitment and planning. - Non-Tradable Output: - Infrastructure services are location-bound and cannot be exported/imported. - Examples: - Roads, bridges, irrigation canals, metro systems. - Implication: - Regional development depends on local infrastructure provisioning. - Intangible Nature: - Some infrastructure assets are non-physical but critical. - Examples: - Radio spectrum for telecom. - Electricity as a flow (cannot be stored easily). - Implication: - Requires strong regulatory oversight and pricing mechanisms. - Non-Rival Consumption: - One person's use does not diminish availability to others (up to a limit). - Examples: - Street lighting, highways, public broadcasting. - Implication: - Justifies public funding or subsidies for universal access. - Price Exclusion Difficulty: - Hard to exclude non-payers from usage. - Examples: - Rural or village roads, public parks, street lighting. - Implication: - Market-based provisioning inefficient → government intervention necessary. - Positive Externalities: - Infrastructure stimulates broader economic and social benefits. - Examples: - A new railway station leads to: - Rise in local commerce. - Better job opportunities. - Enhanced connectivity to nearby towns. - Implication: - Public investment in infrastructure leads to multiplier effects on the economy. - - Royalty Rates for Critical Minerals Reduced (2023) - Context: - India is rich in certain critical minerals essential for future technologies (EVs, renewables, electronics). - Earlier high royalty rates (12%) discouraged private sector mining and foreign investment. - Royalty rates refer to the percentage of revenue or profit that mining companies must pay to the government for extracting natural resources such as minerals, oil, or gas from public or government-owned land. - When a private company extracts a mineral (like lithium or iron) from Indian soil, they don’t own it — it belongs to the nation. So they must pay the government a fee — this is called royalty. - To boost domestic exploration and reduce import dependence, the government slashed royalty rates in October 2023. - Revised Royalty Rates: -

Public-Private Partnership (PPP) - Definition: - A long-term contract between a public sector entity (Union/State/Local Body/PSU) and a private sector company to develop public infrastructure (e.g., highways, ports) or provide public utility services (e.g., electricity, water, transport). - Unlike privatization, where ownership is fully transferred to the private sector, PPP involves shared ownership, risks, and rewards. - Types of PPP Projects: - Greenfield: - New infrastructure projects (e.g., GMR Group building Hyderabad International Airport). - Brownfield: - Upgrading existing infrastructure (e.g., private companies modernizing Delhi and Mumbai airports). - PPP Implementation: - Joint Venture (JV): - Government and private sector form a company with shared equity (e.g., 50:50 ownership). - Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV): - A dedicated company created for a specific project, with equity from public and private sectors. - Concession/Lease/License: - Government grants legal rights to a private company (concessionaire) to design, finance, construct, operate, and maintain infrastructure assets. - PPP Model Description Payment Mechanism Risk Bearer (if Revenue Falls)
Design-Build-Finance-Operate-Transfer (DBFOT) Private entity designs, builds, finances, operates, and transfers the asset after the concession period. User fees or government payments. Private entity or shared.
Rehabilitate-Operate-Transfer (ROT) Private entity rehabilitates existing infrastructure, operates it, and transfers it back. User fees or government payments. Private entity or shared.
Hybrid Annuity Model (HAM) Government funds 40% during construction; private entity finances 60%, operates, and recovers costs via annuities. Government pays fixed annuities + interest. Government bears revenue risk.
Toll-Operate-Transfer (TOT) Private entity operates and collects tolls on existing infrastructure, then transfers it back. Tolls collected from users. Private entity.
Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT) - Toll Private entity builds, operates, and collects tolls, then transfers the asset. User tolls. Private entity.
Build-Operate-Transfer (BOT) - Annuity Private entity builds and operates; government pays fixed amounts. Government pays fixed annuities. Government.
