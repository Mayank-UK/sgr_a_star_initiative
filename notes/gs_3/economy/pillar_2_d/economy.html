- Pillar 2D1: Capital Receipts & Expenditure - Definition: - Capital receipts and expenditure in the Union Budget involve funds that affect the government’s assets or liabilities, distinct from revenue receipts/expenditure, which support day-to-day operations. Capital receipts include borrowings and disinvestment proceeds, while capital expenditure creates long-term assets or reduces liabilities. - Simplification: - Capital Receipt - Money the government receives - Mainly by raising capital - borrowing, loans, disinvestment - Capital Expenditure - Money the government spends - Mainly for creating capital (assets) - roads, buildings, machines, infrastructure - Context: - Capital receipts provide funds for infrastructure, debt repayment, and investments, crucial for economic growth and fiscal stability. Capital expenditure drives development through asset creation (e.g., highways, hospitals) and financial investments, aligning with initiatives like Atmanirbhar Bharat and Gati Shakti. - Significance for India: - Effective management of capital receipts and expenditure enhances infrastructure, boosts economic productivity, and reduces fiscal deficits. Strategic disinvestment and monetization policies under Modi’s regime optimize public sector resources, fostering private sector participation and economic efficiency. - Capital Receipts - Definition: - Capital receipts are funds that increase the government’s liabilities (e.g., borrowings) or reduce its assets (e.g., disinvestment). They are categorized into debt and non-debt receipts. - Types of Capital Receipts: - Capital Debt Receipts: - Internal Borrowing: - Funds raised from domestic sources like RBI, banks, NBFCs, small savings schemes (e.g., Post Office Savings Accounts, Kisan Vikas Patra), and Provident Funds (e.g., EPFO, PPF). - External Borrowing: - Loans from foreign countries and institutions like IMF, World Bank, and BRICS Bank, typically in foreign currencies (e.g., USD, Euro). - Capital Non-Debt Receipts: - Disinvestment: - Proceeds from selling government shares in Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs) or Central Public Sector Enterprises (CPSEs). - Loan Principal Recovered: - Repayments of principal (not interest) from loans given by the Union to states, foreign countries, or public sector companies. - Why the loan principal recovered does not come under capital expenditure, as it is an asset for the government? - Anything that brings cash in = Receipt - Foreign Borrowing in Foreign Currency - Introduction: - In Budget 2019, Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman announced that India’s sovereign external debt-to-GDP ratio is low (~5%), allowing the government to raise part of its borrowing from external markets in foreign currencies (e.g., USD, Euro). - Arguments in Favor: - Reduces Crowding Out: - Domestic borrowing reduces funds available for private corporates. External borrowing frees up domestic capital for private sector growth, enabling factory expansion, job creation, and GDP growth. - Lower Interest Rates: - Advanced economies (e.g., USA, EU) offer lower interest rates, making foreign loans cheaper than domestic ones. - Manageable Debt Levels: - India’s low external debt ratio suggests that moderate foreign borrowing is unlikely to cause significant fiscal strain. - Arguments Against: - Exchange Rate Risk: - A weakening rupee (e.g., $1 = Rs. 60 to Rs. 70) increases the repayment burden in rupees, making loans more expensive than anticipated. - Risk of Over-Borrowing: - Future governments may excessively borrow abroad to fund populist schemes, risking a debt crisis if the rupee depreciates significantly. - Alternative Approach: - Increasing foreign investment limits in Government Securities (G-Secs) in INR attracts foreign capital without currency risk, preferable to borrowing in foreign currency. - Conclusion: - While foreign borrowing can reduce domestic crowding out and leverage low global interest rates, the exchange rate risk and potential for over-borrowing outweigh benefits. Economists like Raghuram Rajan advocate caution, suggesting more deliberation before expanding foreign currency borrowing. - Foreign/External Borrowing Data - Economic Survey 2023: - Most of India’s external debt is in INR, minimizing currency risk. - Data (in Rs. The decline in 2025–26 reflects cautious borrowing strategies. - Disinvestment - Definition: - Disinvestment involves the government selling its shareholding in Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs) or Central Public Sector Enterprises (CPSEs) to raise funds, typically retaining at least 51% ownership to maintain control. - Types of Government-Owned Entities: - Departmental Undertakings: - Directly managed by ministries (e.g., Railways, Postal Services). Moderate operational flexibility. - Government Companies (CPSEs): - Registered under the Companies Act with ≥51% government shareholding (e.g., Coal India, NTPC). Audited by CAG-empaneled private auditors, higher operational flexibility. - Objectives: - Promote public welfare through affordable services, develop infrastructure, ensure regional balance, and prevent economic concentration in private hands. - Challenges: - Political interference, lack of innovation, employee union resistance, and loss-making operations hinder efficiency. - CPSE vs. PSU vs. PSB - Public Sector Banks (PSBs): - Banks with majority government shareholding (e.g., SBI, PNB). - Central Public Sector Enterprises (CPSEs): - Government companies under the Companies Act with ≥51% Union shareholding, excluding PSBs, public sector insurance companies, and NBFCs (e.g., ONGC, SAIL). - Public Sector Undertakings (PSUs): - Collective term for all government-owned entities, including CPSEs, PSBs, and state/local government companies. - Ratna Companies - Definition: - Ratna status, granted by the Department of Public Enterprises (DPE, shifted to Finance Ministry in 2021), provides operational autonomy to high-performing CPSEs (not PSBs, statutory corporations, or private companies). - Categories and Conditions: - Miniratna (Category I & II): - CPSEs with continuous profits for three years, subdivided based on profit levels (e.g., NHPC, Mazagaon Dock). - Navratna: - Miniratna or other CPSEs meeting criteria like manpower cost ratios (e.g., Hindustan Aeronautics, Bharat Electronics). - Maharatna: - Navratna CPSEs with ≥Rs. 5,000 crore annual profit for three years, listed on stock exchanges, and significant global presence (e.g., Coal India, NTPC). - Benefits: - Autonomy in hiring, acquisitions, and decision-making without government approval for minor decisions. - Examples (as of 1/1/2020): - Miniratna: - National Film Development Corporation, WAPCOS. - Navratna: - Oil India, Container Corporation. - Maharatna: - Bharat Petroleum, GAIL, ONGC. - Government Policy Towards Disinvestment (Pre-2021) - Pillar 2D2: Deficits and Fiscal Policy - Introduction: - Deficits occur when government expenditure exceeds its receipts, impacting fiscal stability and economic growth. Understanding types of deficits, their financing, and related policies is crucial for assessing India’s fiscal health and compliance with the Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act, 2003. - Various types of deficits: - Budget Deficit - Definition: - Excess of total expenditure (revenue and capital) over total receipts (revenue and capital), excluding borrowings. - Since 1997–98, the Union Budget has ceased reporting budget deficit, focusing instead on fiscal deficit. - Types of Budget based on deficit - Surplus Budget: - Occurs when government receipts (revenue and capital) exceed expenditure, allowing debt repayment or reserve creation. - Balanced Budget: - Receipts equal expenditure, ensuring no borrowing is required. - Deficit Budget: - Expenditure exceeds receipts, necessitating borrowing to bridge the gap. - Fiscal Deficit - Definition: - Excess of total expenditure over total receipts, excluding borrowings. It represents the amount the government must borrow to meet its financial obligations. - Formulas: - Tamilnadu Textbook: - Fiscal Deficit = Budget Deficit + Borrowings (internal, RBI, external). - NCERT Textbook: - Fiscal Deficit = Total Expenditure – (Revenue Receipts + Non-Debt Creating Capital Receipts). - Alternatively: - Difference between total expenditure and total receipts, excluding borrowings. - Note: - Sukhmoy Chakravarti Committee (1997–98) emphasized fiscal deficit as a key indicator. - Example Data (Budget 2025): - Fiscal Deficit Target: - 4.5% of GDP (as per 15th Finance Commission recommendation). - Different forms of fiscal deficit: - Gross Fiscal Deficit (GFD): - Definition: - Excess of total government expenditure over total non-debt receipts in a financial year. - Formula: - GFD = Total Expenditure - Revenue Receipts - Non-Debt Capital Receipts - Meaning: - Total borrowing requirement of the government in that year. - Includes borrowing from: - Market loans - Treasury bills - External loans - Small savings, etc. - Net Fiscal Deficit (NFD): - Definition: - Fiscal deficit after subtracting net lending by the government. - Formula: - NFD = Gross Fiscal Deficit - Net Lending - Where: - Net Lending = Loans given by government − Loan recoveries - Meaning: - Actual borrowing need after adjusting for loans given onward by government. - Note: - RBI is listed separately in formulas per NCERT, despite being domestic, for clarity in source differentiation. - Primary Deficit - Definition: - Fiscal Deficit minus interest payments on previous loans, indicating borrowing for new programs rather than servicing past debt. - Formula: - Primary Deficit = Fiscal Deficit - Interest Payments - Significance: - High interest payments from accumulated debt increase borrowing needs, creating a vicious cycle. Primary deficit isolates borrowing for current expenditures. - Introduced: - 1993 Budget Speech by Finance Minister Manmohan Singh. - Example Data (Budget 2025): - Primary Deficit Target: - Shifted to 0% by 2022–23, not achieved due to COVID-19. - Different forms of primary deficit: - Gross Primary Deficit (GPD) - Definition: - Gross Fiscal Deficit minus net interest liabilities. - Formula: - Gross Primary Deficit = Gross Fiscal Deficit - Interest Payments - Net Primary Deficit (NPD): - Net Fiscal Deficit minus net interest liabilities. - Formula: - NPD = Net Fiscal Deficit - Interest Payments - Revenue Deficit - Definition: - Excess of revenue expenditure (e.g., salaries, subsidies, interest payments) over revenue receipts (e.g., taxes, non-tax revenue). - Formula: - Revenue Deficit = Revenue Expenditure – Revenue Receipts. - Significance: - High revenue deficit forces borrowing for operational expenses, reducing funds for capital expenditure (e.g., schools, hospitals), hindering human development and economic growth. - Different forms of revenue deficit: - Effective Revenue Deficit - Definition: - Revenue Deficit minus grants-in-aid to states/local bodies used for capital asset creation (e.g., panchayat buildings, disaster management infrastructure). - Formula: - Effective Revenue Deficit = Revenue Deficit - Grants for Capital Asset Creation - Introduced: - Budget 2011 by Finance Minister P. Chidambaram. - Significance: - Adjusts revenue deficit to account for productive expenditure, providing a clearer picture of non-productive borrowing. - Fiscal Glide - Definition: - Gradual reduction in fiscal deficit over time to achieve economic stability without abrupt cuts. - Example: - Budget 2018: - Finance Minister Arun Jaitley aimed for a 3% fiscal deficit by 2020–21, likened to a glider descending smoothly. Temporarily relaxed in 2020 due to structural reforms (FRBM trigger). - Fiscal Drag - Automatic increase in tax burden due to inflation and rising incomes, even when tax rates don’t change. - Fiscal drag is sometimes called a built-in stabilizer, because during inflationary booms it automatically pulls money out of the economy without new policy action. - Mechanism: - Income rises -> tax bracket rises -> more tax -> government revenue rises automatically - Effects on economy - Acts like a contractionary fiscal force - Reduces consumption demand - Increases government revenue without changing tax rates - Can slow growth if too strong - Extra-Budgetary Resources (EBR) - Definition: - Loans raised by public sector undertakings (PSUs) or government entities, repaid from the Union Budget, often bypassing immediate fiscal deficit calculations. - Examples: - Food Corporation of India (FCI) borrowing from National Small Savings Fund (NSSF) for food subsidies (discontinued in Budget 2021). - Data (in Rs. - Ricardian Equivalence Proposition (REP) theory: - When the government spends by borrowing instead of taxing, people expect higher taxes in the future and save more now, so total spending in the economy does not increase. - Theory: - High fiscal deficit → Government borrows more → At maturity (G-Sec redemption), government must repay principal + interest → This may lead to future tax hikes. - Anticipating this, rational citizens may increase savings and reduce consumption → Lower demand → Inventory buildup → Slows economic growth. - Economist David Ricardo proposed this theory. - Invalid for India (Economic Survey 2021): - REP assumes people are rational, forward-looking, and understand future liabilities → Not always true in India. - Reasons for invalidity: - Citizens are not perfectly rational; decisions often influenced by emotional/personal needs (e.g., weddings, iPhones, foreign trips). - High prevalence of tax​ evasion, black​ money, and a parallel economy → Not all citizens bear the tax burden equally. - Large informal sector and financial illiteracy weaken the theoretical assumptions of REP in the Indian context. - Crowding Out Effect: - Concept: - When the government borrows heavily from households and financial institutions (LIC, EPFO, banks via SLR), less money is left for private corporate borrowers → Crowds out private investment → Slows factory expansion and job creation. - Economists decided to use word “crowding out” because it sounded nicer than “financial elbowing.” by the government. - Financial Repression: - Forcing public institutions (e.g., SBI, LIC, EPFO) to buy G-Secs using depositors’ money → deprives households of optimal returns. - Example: Govt forced NABARD to buy ₹15,000 crore Swachh Bharat Mission (Gramin) bonds (10-year maturity). - Govt pressures RBI and others to pay higher dividends → reduces their operational freedom. - ES21 Observations (1950s–1990s): - Found some evidence of crowding out due to: - Very high CRR and SLR → PSBs had limited loanable funds. - Absence of large private banks. - Low earnings and savings among public. - Underdeveloped capital markets. - ES21 Findings (Post-1990): - No evidence of crowding out (1990–2019) due to structural changes: - Economic growth ↑ → household income ↑ → savings ↑ → more money available to both govt and private borrowers. - Rise in banking habits, NBFCs, mutual funds, and capital market participation. - Demographic dividend → more working population → improved financial behavior. - Implication: - If govt borrowing is for productive infrastructure (health, education, transport, etc.), it raises GDP and incomes → boosts overall savings → the 'size of the dish' increases, so both govt and private sectors get enough funds. - Thus, "crowding out" is less likely when savings are not fixed but expand with growth. - Monetization of Deficit: - When central bank prints more money to pay for government's loans → Monetizing the Deficit. - It can result in hyperinflation and ↓↓ purchasing power of currency (if supply of goods like onion, tomato is not increased sufficiently). Example: Germany after Treaty of Versailles (1919). - Therefore, Monetizing the Deficit is also called "inflating away of debt". - FRBM Act prevents monetization of deficit with following provisions: - RBI must not subscribe to G-Sec in primary market from 2006-07 onwards. - Govt shall not borrow from RBI, except for Ways and Means Advances (WMA). (Refer: Pillar 3) - However, ES21 said not to worry too much about it. - Countercyclical Fiscal Policy - Introduction and Historical Context: - Historically, governments have used public spending during times of economic distress to provide employment and stimulate growth. - In ancient economies, rulers invested in infrastructure like roads, canals, and public buildings during famines to create jobs. - In modern macroeconomics, this principle is institutionalized as countercyclical fiscal policy. - Definition: - A countercyclical fiscal policy involves increasing public expenditure and/or reducing taxes during economic slowdowns, and reducing spending and/or increasing taxes during economic booms. - The objective is to smoothen economic fluctuations, support aggregate demand, and maintain macroeconomic stability. - How it Works: - During Economic Slowdown (Recessionary Phase): - Government increases capital and revenue expenditure. - Objectives: - Stimulate demand through infrastructure projects. - Generate employment and raise household income. - Boost private sector confidence and investment. - Leads to Crowding-In effect: - Increased government investment leads to complementary private sector activity. - Example: - Government constructs national highways → private sector builds logistics parks, fuel stations, hotels → more jobs and tax revenue. - During Economic Boom (Expansionary Phase): - Government reduces expenditure and increases savings. - Objectives: - Control inflationary pressures. - Accumulate fiscal buffers (surpluses) to deploy during future downturns. - Avoid overheating of the economy. - Overheating of an economy refers to a situation where economic growth is unsustainably high, leading to inflationary pressures, resource shortages, and imbalances. - Strategic Benefits: - Ensures long-term fiscal sustainability. - Enhances economic resilience to external shocks (e.g., global financial crises, pandemics). - Improves sovereign credit ratings and investor confidence. - Pro-cyclical Fiscal Policy: - Opposite of countercyclical approach. - Government increases expenditure during booms and cuts spending during slowdowns. - Consequences: - Amplifies economic volatility. - Worsens inflation during booms and unemployment during recessions. - Example: - India’s historical fiscal stance has often been pro-cyclical, as highlighted by Economic Surveys. - Real-World Examples: - Chile: - Uses a structural fiscal rule based on long-term copper prices to avoid pro-cyclicality. - United States: - Large fiscal stimulus packages during the 2008 Global Financial Crisis and COVID-19 pandemic. - India: - National Infrastructure Pipeline (NIP) and PM Gati Shakti are attempts to institutionalize countercyclical investment. - Key Terminology: - Crowding-In: - When public investment encourages private sector investment. - Fiscal Multipliers: - Ratio of the change in national income to the initial change in government spending or taxation. - Types - Government spending multiplier - Change in GDP due to change in government expenditure - Tax multiplier - Change in GDP due to change in taxes - Usually smaller in absolute value than spending multiplier - Transfer payment multiplier - Based on change in government transfers (like subsidies, pensions) - Interest Rate – Growth Rate Differential (IRGD) and Debt Sustainability - Concept: - IRGD measures the difference between the interest rate on government borrowing and the nominal GDP growth rate. - Indicator of whether public debt is sustainable over the medium to long term. - Formula: - IRGD = Interest Rate – GDP Growth Rate - If IRGD is negative (e.g., 6% – 9% = –3%), it indicates strong debt sustainability. - Domar Condition of Debt Sustainability - Concept Origin: - Proposed by economist Evsey Domar in 1944. - Provides a theoretical foundation for assessing long-term debt sustainability. - Domar's Rule: - If the growth rate of nominal GDP is higher than the interest rate on public debt, then public debt as a percentage of GDP will decline over time. - Formula: - If g > r → debt-to-GDP ratio stabilizes or declines. - Where g = GDP growth rate, r = interest rate on debt. - Implications: - Countries with high GDP growth can afford higher deficits without increasing their debt burden. - Countries with low growth need to either raise taxes or cut spending to maintain fiscal stability. - Indian Scenario: - India’s nominal GDP growth post-COVID recovery has remained higher than the average cost of borrowing. - Indicates that fiscal expansion (within limits) can be sustainable if accompanied by growth-enhancing reforms. - Additional concepts - Automatic Stabilizers - Definition: - Mechanisms in the government budget that automatically increase spending or decrease tax liability during economic slowdowns without requiring changes in tax laws or parliamentary approval. - Examples: - Income tax, corporation tax, unemployment allowance, food subsidies, and government schemes targeting the poor/lower-middle class. - Note: - Countercyclical fiscal policy/fiscal stimulus requires new budgets/laws/parliamentary approval, making them discretionary stabilizers, not automatic. - Disposable Income & Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC): - Disposable Income: - Personal income minus personal income taxes; available for consumption and saving. - MPC: - Fraction of additional disposable income spent on consumption. - In simple words: - When your income increases, how much of that extra income you actually spend instead of saving. That share is called Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC). - Negative Tax multiplier: - Increase in taxes reduces disposable income, decreasing consumption and GDP. - Autonomous Expenditure Multiplier (AEM) & Government Expenditure Multiplier: - Autonomous Expenditure Multiplier (AEM) - Measures change in GDP/income/output due to change in autonomous spending (e.g., food, shelter, medicine, regardless of income level) - Government Expenditure Multiplier - Measures change in GDP/income/output due to change in government expenditure - Balanced Budget Multiplier - Measures change in GDP/income/output due to equal changes in taxes and government expenditure - Effects of Budget/Fiscal Policy: - Impacts demand (consumption) and savings through changes in taxes and government spending. - Three Functions of Government Budget: - Allocation - Provides public goods not supplied by markets (e.g., national defense, police, roads, railways) - Redistribution - Collects taxes from the rich to fund welfare for the poor - Stabilisation - Increases spending during economic slowdowns to stabilize the economy (see counter-cyclical fiscal policy) - Methods for Repaying Public Debt: - Redemption (Terminal Annuity): - Meaning: - Refers to the repayment of both principal and interest at regular intervals. - It concludes the debt obligation over time. - Notes: - Common in long-term borrowing arrangements. - Ensures gradual reduction in outstanding liabilities. - Sinking Fund: - Meaning: - A special fund created by the government wherein money is deposited regularly. - At the time of maturity of Government Securities (G-secs), this fund is used to repay the debt. - Significance: - Reduces pressure on budget at the time of redemption. - Enhances creditworthiness of the government. - History: - First introduced in England as a fiscal prudence measure. - Conversion / Restructuring: - Meaning: - Old debt is replaced with new debt having modified terms such as lower interest rate or longer tenure. - Example: - Lowering the interest burden by converting old high-interest debt into new low-interest debt. - Context: - 2020: - Zambia became the first African nation to default on Eurobond payments during the COVID-19 pandemic. - Experts recommended restructuring for debt sustainability. - Evergreening of Loans: - Meaning: - Taking a new loan to repay the old one. - Risks: - Creates a debt trap situation if repeated frequently. - May hide the true fiscal health of the government. - Repudiation: - Meaning: - Government refuses to acknowledge and repay its debt obligations. - Example: - After the Russian Revolution (1917), Lenin's regime repudiated the loans taken by the previous Czarist regime from Britain and France. - Implication: - Considered a poor strategy in modern economics. - Leads to loss of investor confidence and denial of future credit. - Fiscal Consolidation / Prudence - Definition: - Fiscal Consolidation refers to policies aimed at reducing government deficits and debt accumulation. - Vertical Imbalance: - Tax powers are concentrated with the Centre, but spending responsibilities are higher at the state level. - Creates dependence of states on central transfers (Finance Commission, GST compensation, grants). - Example: - States have limited access to income tax or customs, but must fund health, education, etc. - 2. It is now merged with the General Budget (since 2017). - General Budget - The General Budget is the annual financial statement of the government showing estimated receipts and expenditures of all ministries and departments for a financial year. - Historical Context: - 1920–21: - Acworth Committee recommended a separate Railway Budget due to railways’ significant revenue contribution during British rule. - 1924: - Separation Convention formalized this practice. - Post-independence, the Railway Budget was presented before the General Budget. - Merger (2017): - Modi government merged the Railway Budget with the General Budget, based on NITI Aayog’s Bibek Debroy Committee recommendations: - No constitutional requirement for a separate budget. - Populist measures (e.g., low fares) during coalition governments eroded railway profitability. - Railway revenue became a small fraction of the General Budget, not justifying separate presentation. - Impact: - Streamlined budgeting, reduced populism, and aligned railway finances with national fiscal goals. - On the basis of planning - Classification: - Plan Expenditure: - Linked to Five-Year Plans (e.g., development projects). - Non-Plan Expenditure: - Routine expenses (e.g., salaries, maintenance). - Discontinuation (Budget 2017): - Modi government ended the distinction due to: - No constitutional mandate. - Dissolution of the Planning Commission (2014–15). - End of the 12th Five-Year Plan (2012–17). - Impact: - Simplified budget presentation, focusing on revenue and capital classifications. - Budgeting Methods - - Traditional/Line-Item Budgeting: - Focuses on listing income and expenditure without evaluating benefits (e.g., Rs. 10,000 for hospital beds). - Lacks performance measurement. - Performance Budgeting: - Ties expenditure to measurable outcomes (e.g., Rs. 50,000 for computers to achieve 30% faster RTI processing). - Enhances cost-benefit analysis and efficiency. - Zero-Based Budgeting (ZBB): - Requires departments to justify all expenditure from scratch, not incrementally (e.g., reviewing education schemes’ effectiveness before reallocation). - Prevents automatic budget increases. - Sunset Budgeting: - Schemes have predefined deadlines (e.g., a subsidy for two years), ensuring automatic termination unless renewed. - Gender Budget - Definition: - A statement within the General Budget (since 2005) highlighting allocations for women-centric schemes, though not constitutionally mandated. - Structure (Budget 2025): - Parts: - Part A: - 100% women-focused schemes (e.g., SAMBAL, SAMARTHYA under Women and Child Development Ministry) – >Rs. 1 lakh crore. - Part B: - Schemes with at least 30% allocation for women (e.g., school education) – >Rs. 3 lakh crore. - Part C (since Budget 2024–July): - Schemes with less than 30% for women (e.g., PM-KISAN, Rs. 6,000 to farmers) – >Rs. 16,000 crore. - Total: - >Rs. 4 lakh crore. - Criticism: - Three-part system (introduced in 2024) accused of data manipulation to exaggerate women-focused spending. -
