- Pillar 2B1: Finance Commission - Definition: - The Finance Commission is a constitutional and quasi-judicial body established under Article 280 of the Indian Constitution. - It is constituted by the President of India every five years (or earlier) to ensure fiscal federalism by recommending: - The distribution of net proceeds of taxes between the Union and State governments. - The principles governing grants-in-aid to the states from the Consolidated Fund of India. - Measures to augment the Consolidated Fund of a State to supplement the resources of Panchayats and Municipalities. - Its role is to address imbalances in taxation and expenditure responsibilities between the Centre and States, thus promoting equitable financial governance. - Context: - Fiscal federalism involves dividing taxation and expenditure responsibilities across government levels (Union, States, Local Bodies). States have significant expenditure responsibilities under the 7th Schedule but limited taxation powers, making the Finance Commission critical for equitable revenue sharing. - Significance for India: - The Finance Commission promotes fiscal equity, supports state development, and ensures financial stability by recommending tax devolution, grants, and fiscal discipline measures, aligning with India’s goals of cooperative federalism and balanced regional growth. - Structure: - Comprises a Chairman and four members, appointed by the President, eligible for re-appointment. Recommendations are advisory but rarely rejected by the government. - Functions: - Recommends vertical tax devolution (Union to States) and horizontal distribution (among States). - Suggests grants-in-aid to States for revenue deficits, local bodies, and disaster management. - Advises on augmenting State Consolidated Funds for Panchayats and Municipalities. - Addresses additional matters specified in Terms of Reference (ToR) by the President, such as fiscal discipline or specific state needs. - 15th vs. 16th Finance Commission - Membership: -

- Post-Devolution Revenue Deficit Grants - Definition: - Grants provided to States that continue to face revenue deficits even after receiving their share of Union taxes, in order to maintain fiscal stability. - Mechanism: - Calculated as: - Revenue Deficit = State’s Revenue Expenditure – Revenue Income – Share from Union Taxes - Example: - Andhra Pradesh (2020–21): - Revenue Expenditure: ₹41,000 crore - Union Tax Share: ₹35,000 crore - Revenue Deficit: ₹6,000 crore - Hence, receives a ₹6,000 crore grant to cover the deficit. - Eligibility: - 14 States are eligible. - Includes: - Assam, Himachal Pradesh, Manipur, Meghalaya, etc. - Special Grants - States had to approach it for plan grants and project approvals. - As a result, it had significant discretionary power to: - Allocate funds - Approve state plans - Guide national priorities - This made state governments highly dependent on it for funding beyond just the tax devolutions recommended by Finance Commission. - Special Category States (SCS) - Concept & Background - Created to recognize states facing unique structural disadvantages that hinder their development. - These include geographic challenges, demographic backwardness, strategic concerns, and poor finances. - Origin - 1952: - National Development Council (NDC) formed to approve Five-Year Plans. - NDC (now defunct post-NITI Aayog) was responsible for granting SCS status till recent years. - 1969: - 5th Finance Commission recommended special tax reliefs for disadvantaged states. - Criteria for Special Category Status - Difficult terrain and hilly regions (e.g. Himalayas, NE states) - Low population density and/or high tribal population - Strategic location along international borders - Economic and infrastructural backwardness - Non-viable finances (i.e., poor internal revenue generation) - States that Received SCS - 11 traditional SCS states: - 8 North-Eastern states - 3 Himalayan states: - Jammu & Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh, Uttarakhand - Telangana was briefly given SCS after its formation in 2014 (by UPA govt) - Post Article 370 abrogation (2019), J&K ceased to be an SCS due to reorganization into UT. - Benefits of Special Category Status - Higher Union share in Centrally Sponsored Schemes (CSS): - Normal States: - 60:40 (Centre:State) - SCS States: - 90:10 - Industrial incentives: - Income tax and excise duty exemptions to industries setting up in SCS regions. - - Pillar 2B2: Black​ money & Allied Issues - Definitions and concepts: - Black​ money: - Black​ money refers to income or transactions that are taxable but deliberately concealed from tax authorities to evade taxes. It operates within a parallel economy, undermining fiscal integrity and reducing government revenue for welfare and development. Unlike legal tax planning (e.g., investing in LIC or PPF for deductions), which is ethical and lawful, black​ money involves illegal practices like tax​ evasion or money​ laundering. - Tax Planning / Tax Mitigation: - Legally reducing tax liability using provisions like LIC, PPF, pension funds, etc.; it is legal and ethical. - Tax​ evasion: - Illegally hiding income or transactions to avoid tax (e.g., not declaring rental income); punishable under law. - Tax Avoidance: - Legally disclosing income but exploiting loopholes to reduce tax (e.g., profit shifting via shell companies); legal but often unethical. - Tax Haven: - Countries with low/zero tax and secrecy laws (e.g., Panama, Mauritius, Cayman Islands) that attract foreign individuals or firms seeking to avoid/evade taxes. - Money​ laundering: - Definition: - The process of converting “dirty money” (illegally earned) into “clean money” (legally usable). - Involves disguising the origin, ownership, or destination of money obtained through criminal activities. - Money​ laundering generally presumes criminal origin, especially under Indian and international laws. - - Stages: - Placement: - Introducing illegal money into the financial system (e.g., via casinos, deposits). - Layering: - Complex transactions to obscure the money trail (e.g., shell companies). - Integration: - Reintroducing laundered money into the economy as clean (e.g., real estate, luxury purchases). - Tools: - Real estate, shell companies, hawala, foreign accounts, gold. - Hawala: - An informal and illegal system of money transfer that operates outside conventional banking or financial channels. - Money is transferred without physical movement; instead, it relies on a network of brokers or agents (known as hawaladars). - - Context: - Black​ money distorts economic equity, fuels corruption, and hampers India’s ability to fund public services. India has implemented stringent laws, international agreements, and digital reforms to combat black​ money, aligning with global efforts to enhance financial transparency. - Significance for India: - Tackling black​ money is crucial for increasing tax compliance, boosting revenue for infrastructure and social programs, and strengthening India’s global financial standing. It supports economic stability and aligns with initiatives like Atmanirbhar Bharat by fostering a transparent, inclusive economy. - Notable Organisations - Definition: - Several specialized agencies in India and globally work to detect, prevent, and penalize activities related to black​ money, tax​ evasion, and money​ laundering, ensuring compliance with financial regulations. - Key Organisations: -

- PMLA Amendments via Finance Bill (2015-2019) - Overview: - Amendments to Prevention of money​ laundering Act (PMLA-2002) increased Enforcement Directorate (ED) powers for summons, arrests, and raids. - Introduced reverse burden of proof, making bail nearly impossible for accused. - Method: - Amendments passed via Finance Bill (classified as Money Bill) to bypass Rajya Sabha scrutiny. - Legal Challenge: - Challenged in Supreme Court for violating fundamental rights due to reverse burden of proof. - 2022: - Supreme Court upheld government's actions, dismissing opposition and PIL claims. - 2023: - CJI D.Y. Chandrachud announced a seven-judge Constitution Bench to revisit the issue. - Undisclosed Foreign Income & Assets Act (UFIA-2015) - Definition: - Requires Indian residents to disclose foreign assets (e.g., Dubai bungalow, Swiss bank account) and income from foreign sources (e.g., Cayman Islands shell company) in income tax returns. - Tax and Penalties: - Foreign income taxed at 30% with no deductions, exemptions, or rebates. - Violation: - Penalty + up to 10 years imprisonment. - Corporate Liability: - Company violations hold responsible individuals liable unless they prove lack of knowledge. - International Cooperation: - Empowers Union government to enter agreements with other countries for tax information exchange. - Benefits: - Increases transparency, reduces black​ money, and enhances tax compliance. - Benami Transactions Prohibition Act (BTPA-1988, Amended 2016) - Definition: - Targets properties registered in the name of relatives, staff (e.g., driver, gardener), or fictitious persons to evade tax authorities. - Example: - 2018, Misa Bharti Yadav allegedly bought a farmhouse in her brother-in-law Nilesh Kumar’s name. - Evolution: - 1988 Act ineffective; amended in 2016 for stronger enforcement. - Main Agency: - Income Tax Department. - Judicial Process: - Cases heard by PMLA Adjudicating Authority and Appellate Tribunal. - Violation: - Property confiscation, penalty, and imprisonment. - Govt Announcements - Tax Surrender Schemes - Definition: - Allow tax evaders to declare undisclosed income, pay taxes and penalties, and avoid prosecution by the Income Tax Department (except for income from criminal activities like narcotics, kidnapping, or extortion). - Older schemes: -

- Laffer Curve: - Concept: - By Arthur Laffer; high tax rates reduce revenue by discouraging work or encouraging evasion; tax cuts can increase revenue. - - Applications: - Modi Budgets (2017+): - Lowest income tax slab cut from 10% to 5%; corporation tax for small companies reduced from 30% to 25% (phased). - Budget 2020: - Optional income tax slabs introduced. - USA Budget 2017: - Corporation tax cut from 35% to 15%. - Tax Buoyancy & Elasticity: - Tax Elasticity: - Measures the responsiveness of tax revenue to changes in GDP, assuming no change in tax rates or tax policy. - Elasticity = responsiveness of one variable to change in another - Formula: - \( \text{Tax Elasticity} = \frac{\%\ \Delta \text{Tax Revenue (adjusted for tax rate changes)}}{\%\ \Delta \text{GDP}} \) - Tax Buoyancy: - Measures the degree to which tax revenue increases in response to an increase in GDP. - - Context: - Buoyancy in physics = how strongly something rises when the surrounding medium rises. - Tax buoyancy = how strongly tax revenue rises when the economy (GDP) rises. - Formula: - \( \text{Tax Buoyancy} = \frac{\%\ \Delta \text{Tax Revenue}}{\%\ \Delta \text{GDP}} \) - Example (ES23): - GST buoyancy = 1.12 (high; grows faster than GDP); pre-GST indirect taxes = 0.99 (low). - Net Tax Revenue of Union: -

- Note: - National Disaster Response Fund (statutory under Disaster Management Act, 2005; formerly National Calamity Contingency Fund). - Pillar 2B3: Revenue Expenditure - Definition: - Revenue expenditure refers to government spending on the day-to-day operations and maintenance of state functions, which does not create long-term income-generating or permanent assets. It includes expenses like salaries, pensions, subsidies, and interest payments on loans, critical for sustaining administrative, social, and economic services. - Context: - Revenue expenditure forms a significant portion of India’s Union Budget, ensuring the smooth functioning of executive, judiciary, legislature, and statutory bodies while supporting welfare schemes through subsidies and grants. Efficient management is vital to balance fiscal health and public welfare. - Significance for India: - Revenue expenditure supports essential services like defense, education, and healthcare, and funds welfare programs like subsidies for food, fuel, and fertilizers. It is crucial for maintaining governance and social equity but must be controlled to prevent fiscal deficits and ensure sustainable economic growth. - Overview - Characteristics: - Does not result in income-generating or permanent assets (unlike capital expenditure, which creates assets like roads or buildings). - Funds the operational costs of executive, judiciary, legislature, constitutional bodies (e.g., Election Commission), and statutory bodies (e.g., SEBI). - Includes payments for social services (health, education), economic services (agriculture, transport), and administrative functions (police, external affairs). - Key Components: - Interest Payments: - Payments on interest for loans (principal repayment is capital expenditure). This is the largest revenue expenditure, reflecting India’s debt servicing obligations. - Subsidies: - Financial assistance to reduce the burden on citizens or industries (e.g., food, fertilizer, fuel subsidies). - Salaries and Pensions: - Compensation for government employees and retirees, including dearness allowances to offset inflation. - Grants-in-Aid: - Funds provided to states, local bodies, or institutions (e.g., disaster management, Panchayati Raj) without repayment obligations. - Defense Expenditure: - Salaries, fuel, and maintenance for armed forces (e.g., fuel for tanks, soldier salaries). - Union Territories without Legislature: - Operational expenses for UTs like Chandigarh and Lakshadweep. - Subsidies - Definition: - Subsidies are financial benefits provided by the government to individuals, farmers, or industries to reduce costs, promote welfare, or support economic activities. Unlike taxes, subsidies are voluntary and not punishable if refused. - Comparison with Taxes: -

- Types of Subsidies - Subsidies are categorized based on their delivery mechanism and purpose, aimed at promoting affordability, equity, or economic growth. - Types and Examples: - Direct Cash Subsidies: - Transferred directly to beneficiaries’ bank accounts (e.g., PM-KISAN: Rs. 6,000 annually for farmers; DBT-PAHAL: ~Rs. 200 per LPG cylinder). - In-Kind Subsidies: - Non-monetary benefits like free school bags, uniforms, books for poor students, free medicines in public hospitals, or free insurance schemes (e.g., Ayushman Bharat). - Indirect Subsidies: - Government pays organizations to offer goods/services at reduced rates (e.g., low fees in government colleges, subsidized kerosene, urea, or crop insurance premiums). - Implicit Subsidies: - Government policies suppress supply to raise prices for a sector’s benefit (e.g., banning American poultry imports creates a shortage, allowing Indian poultry farmers to charge higher prices, indirectly subsidizing them). - Cross-Subsidization: - One group’s higher payments offset another’s lower costs (e.g., Indian Railways charges higher freight rates to keep passenger fares affordable for the poor). - Regulatory Subsidies: - Mandated price caps by regulators (e.g., State Electricity Regulatory Commission limits electricity rates for farmers to Rs. 2–4 per unit). - Procurement Subsidies: - Government purchases at above-market prices (e.g., Food Corporation of India buys food grains at Minimum Support Price to support farmers). - Interest Subsidies (Subvention): - Government pays part of the interest on loans for farmers, MSMEs, or affordable housing (e.g., LIC’s Vaya Vandana Yojana for senior citizens). - Impact of Subsidies - Positive Impacts: - Merit Goods: - Subsidies on healthcare, education, LPG, solar panels, and windmills increase access and generate positive externalities (e.g., LPG subsidies reduce firewood use, preserving trees and reducing indoor air pollution). - Economic Support: - Subsidies like PM-KISAN and fertilizer subsidies stabilize farmers’ incomes, enhancing agricultural productivity and rural welfare. - Social Equity: - In-kind subsidies (e.g., free school supplies) and indirect subsidies (e.g., cheap college fees) promote access to education and healthcare for the poor. - Negative Impacts: - Environmental Harm: - Subsidies on diesel, kerosene, and urea lead to negative externalities like air pollution, soil degradation, water pollution, and algae blooms due to overuse. - Subsidy Leakage: - Inefficiencies like ghost beneficiaries (non-existent recipients created by corrupt officials) and ineligible beneficiaries (e.g., wealthy individuals receiving subsidies) waste resources. - Fiscal Burden: - High subsidy costs strain government budgets, potentially increasing fiscal deficits (e.g., food subsidies alone cost ~Rs. 2 lakh crore in Budget 2024–25). - Market Distortion: - Implicit subsidies (e.g., poultry import bans) and procurement subsidies (e.g., MSP) can distort market prices, affecting economic efficiency. - Salaries - Definition: - Salaries and related allowances for central government employees and retirees form a significant portion of revenue expenditure, ensuring workforce compensation and inflation protection. - 7th Pay Commission (2014–16): - Overview: - Constituted by the Ministry of Finance (Department of Expenditure) under Justice A.K. Mathur, effective from 1 January 2016. The first commission was led by Srinivasa Varadachariar (1946). - Key Recommendations: - Introduced Pay Matrix, replacing pay bands and grade pay for simplified salary structures. - Increased minimum salary to Rs. 18,000/month (Group D) and maximum to Rs. 2.25 lakh/month (Apex scale, e.g., Department Secretary) and Rs. 2.5 lakh/month (Cabinet Secretary). - Regulatory body salaries raised (e.g., Chairman: Rs. 4.5 lakh/month). - Adopted Dr. Aykroyd’s formula to adjust wages based on commodity price changes, potentially eliminating the need for future pay commissions. - Strengthened Modified Assured Career Progression (MACP) to ensure merit-based promotions, curbing automatic promotions for underperforming officials. - Reforms for defense and Central Armed Police Forces (CAPF), including better allowances and service conditions. - 8th Pay Commission (Budget 2025) - Overview: - Announced in Budget 2025, but chairman details were unavailable as of 1 February 2025. Aims to further refine salary structures and address inflation and employee welfare. - Context: - Likely to build on the 7th Pay Commission’s Pay Matrix and Aykroyd formula, ensuring dynamic salary adjustments. - Expected to address concerns like rising living costs and pension disparities. - Dearness Allowance (DA) and Dearness Relief (DR) - Definition: - Dearness Allowance (DA) is a cost-of-living adjustment for serving central government employees to offset inflation. - Dearness Relief (DR) is a similar allowance for retired employees. - Mechanism: - Calculated as a percentage of basic salary/pension, based on the All India Consumer Price Index (AICPI). - Revised biannually (January and July) by the Department of Expenditure. - Key Developments: - 2020: - DA/DR frozen due to COVID-19 fiscal constraints. - 2021 (July): - Resumed at 28% (from 17%), with arrears paid to employees and pensioners. - 2024: - DA increased to 50% of basic salary for ~1 crore employees and pensioners, reflecting inflation adjustments. -

- Bonus to Government Employees - Definition: - Bonuses are additional payments beyond regular salaries, given occasionally to boost morale or reward performance. - Types: - Productivity-Linked Bonus (PLB): - Tied to measurable output (e.g., files cleared, trees planted, or sales improved in postal/railways departments). - Non-Productivity-Linked (Ad-Hoc) Bonus: - Given regardless of performance, e.g., Diwali bonus (up to Rs. 7,000 for non-gazetted employees in 2024). - Example: - 2024: - Productivity-linked bonuses for railway employees based on train operation efficiency; ad-hoc Diwali bonuses for Group C and D staff. - Miscellaneous Terms - House Rent Allowance (HRA): - Provided to cover accommodation costs, varying by city classification (e.g., 27% of basic salary in metro cities like Delhi, 9% in smaller towns under 7th Pay Commission). - Gratuity: - Lump-sum payment for employees with continuous service (typically 5+ years), governed by the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972. Maximum limit: Rs. 20 lakh (2024). - One Rank One Pension (OROP): - Introduced in 2015 for military personnel to ensure equal pensions for the same rank and service length, regardless of retirement date. Criticisms include disputes over base year and calculation formulas, with ex-servicemen seeking revisions.
