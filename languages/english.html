- Parts of Speech - Detailed Explanation
    - Nouns
        - Definition: 
            - A noun is a word that names a person, place, thing, or idea.
        - Types of Nouns:
            - Common Nouns: 
                - General names for people, places, or things (e.g., dog, city, car).
            - Proper Nouns: 
                - Specific names of people, places, or things (e.g., John, Paris, Coca-Cola).
            - Abstract Nouns: 
                - Name ideas, qualities, or feelings (e.g., love, freedom, beauty).
            - Concrete Nouns: 
                - Can be touched or seen (e.g., table, house, apple).
            - Collective Nouns: 
                - Refer to a group of people, animals, or things (e.g., family, team, flock).
            - Countable and Uncountable Nouns:
                - Countable Nouns can be counted (e.g., books, pens).
                - Uncountable Nouns cannot be counted (e.g., water, milk, air).
        - Rules:
            - Plural Form: 
                - Usually, nouns are made plural by adding -s (e.g., books), but some irregular nouns change form (e.g., child → children).
            - Possessive Form: 
                - To show possession, add an apostrophe (') and an s (e.g., John's book).
    - Pronouns
        - Definition: 
            - A pronoun is a word that takes the place of a noun to avoid repetition.
        - Types of Pronouns:
            - Personal Pronouns: 
                - Represent specific people or things (e.g., I, he, she, it, we, they).
            - Possessive Pronouns: 
                - Show ownership (e.g., mine, yours, his, hers, ours, theirs).
            - Reflexive Pronouns: 
                - Reflect the action back to the subject (e.g., myself, yourself, himself, herself).
            - Demonstrative Pronouns: 
                - Point to specific things (e.g., this, that, these, those).
            - Interrogative Pronouns: 
                - Used to ask questions (e.g., who, whom, what, which).
            - Indefinite Pronouns: 
                - Refer to non-specific things (e.g., anyone, everyone, someone, none).
        - Rules:
            - Pronouns must agree in number and gender with the noun they replace.
            - E.g., The girl lost her book. (“Her” agrees with “girl.”)
            - Reflexive pronouns are used when the subject and object are the same.
            - E.g., She taught herself.
    - Verbs
        - Definition: 
            - A verb is a word that expresses an action, occurrence, or state of being.
        - Types of Verbs:
            - Action Verbs: 
                - Show physical or mental action (e.g., run, eat, think).
            - Linking Verbs: 
                - Connect the subject to the complement (e.g., am, is, are, was, were).
            - Auxiliary (Helping) Verbs: 
                - Help the main verb (e.g., is, have, will, can).
            - Modal Verbs: 
                - Show possibility, necessity, or ability (e.g., can, could, should, may).
            - Transitive Verbs: 
                - Need an object to complete the meaning (e.g., She eats an apple).
            - Intransitive Verbs: 
                - Do not require an object (e.g., He sleeps).
        - Rules:
            - Subject-Verb Agreement: 
                - The verb must agree in number with the subject.
                - E.g., He runs (singular) vs. They run (plural).
            - Tenses: 
                - Verbs change form based on time (present, past, future). (More on tenses below).
    - Adjectives
        - Definition: 
            - An adjective is a word that describes or modifies a noun or pronoun.
        - Types of Adjectives:
            - Descriptive Adjectives: 
                - Describe qualities of a noun (e.g., big, blue, happy).
            - Quantitative Adjectives: 
                - Show how much or how many (e.g., few, many, some).
            - Demonstrative Adjectives: 
                - Point to specific things (e.g., this, that, these, those).
            - Possessive Adjectives: 
                - Show ownership (e.g., my, your, his).
        - Rules:
            - Order of Adjectives: When you use multiple adjectives, they follow a specific order:
                - 1. Quantity/Number → 2. Opinion → 3. Size → 4. Age → 5. Shape → 6. Color → 7. Proper Adjective → 8. Purpose/Qualifier.
                - E.g., She has two beautiful big brown dogs.
            - Comparative and Superlative Forms:
                - For most adjectives, add -er for comparative (e.g., smaller) and -est for superlative (e.g., smallest).
                - For adjectives ending in -y, change -y to -ier or -iest (e.g., happier, happiest).
    - Adverbs
        - Definition: 
            - An adverb is a word that modifies or describes a verb, adjective, or another adverb.
        - Types of Adverbs:
            - Adverbs of Manner: 
                - Describe how something is done (e.g., quickly, happily).
            - Adverbs of Time: 
                - Describe when something happens (e.g., now, yesterday).
            - Adverbs of Place: 
                - Describe where something happens (e.g., here, there).
            - Adverbs of Frequency: 
                - Describe how often something happens (e.g., always, never).
            - Adverbs of Degree: 
                - Describe the intensity of the action (e.g., very, quite, too).
        - Rules:
            - Adverbs often end in -ly (e.g., quickly, carefully), but not always (e.g., always, never).
            - Adverbs usually come after the verb or before the adjective/adverb they modify.
            - E.g., She sings beautifully. (after verb)
            - E.g., She is very tall. (before adjective)
    - Prepositions
        - Definition: 
            - Prepositions are words that show the relationship between a noun (or pronoun) and another word in the sentence.
        - Common Prepositions:
            - Place: 
                - in, on, at, under, above, between, next to.
            - Time: 
                - at, on, in, before, after.
            - Direction: 
                - to, from, towards, into, through.
        - Rules:
            - Prepositions are typically followed by a noun or pronoun.
                - E.g., The book is on the table. (on + noun)
                - E.g., She walked towards me. (towards + pronoun)
            - Prepositional Phrases: A preposition + its object (a noun/pronoun) form a prepositional phrase.
                - E.g., on the table, under the bed, in the box.
    - Conjunctions
        - Definition:
            - A conjunction is a word that connects words, phrases, clauses, or sentences. Conjunctions help to build a relationship between different parts of a sentence.
        - Types of Conjunctions:
            - Coordinating Conjunctions: These connect words, phrases, or clauses that are of equal importance. The main coordinating conjunctions are FANBOYS:
                - For (because)
                - And (adds one idea to another)
                - Nor (negation of either of two)
                - But (contrasts two ideas)
                - Or (gives an alternative)
                - Yet (shows contrast, like “but”)
                - So (indicates a consequence)
                - Examples:
                    - She likes tea and coffee.
                    - He went to the store, but it was closed.
            - Subordinating Conjunctions: These connect a dependent (subordinate) clause with an independent clause.
                - Common subordinating conjunctions: because, since, if, when, although, while, unless, as.
                - Examples:
                    - Because I was late, I missed the bus.
                    - He went to the party although he was tired.
            - Correlative Conjunctions: These work in pairs to join equal elements in a sentence.
                - Both…and, Either…or, Neither…nor, Not only…but also, Whether…or.
                - Examples:
                    - Both my brother and my sister like ice cream.
                    - Either you can go or stay.
        - Rules:
            - Conjunctions are used to connect ideas.
            - Coordinating conjunctions join elements that are equal in importance (e.g., two nouns, two adjectives, two independent clauses).
            - Subordinating conjunctions are used to connect a dependent clause with an independent clause, indicating a relationship like cause, contrast, condition, etc.
            - Correlative conjunctions should be used in pairs to connect two equal elements in a sentence.
    - Interjections
        - Definition:
            - An interjection is a word or phrase used to express strong emotions or sudden feelings. Interjections are often followed by an exclamation mark.
        - Examples:
            - Wow! That's amazing!
            - Oh no! I missed the bus.
            - Hooray! We won the match!
            - Ouch! That hurt!
        - Rules:
            - Interjections are used to convey emotions, surprise, or exclamation.
            - They are not grammatically connected to the sentence structure.
            - They can stand alone or be followed by an exclamation mark.
    - Articles
        - Definition:
            - An article is a word that defines a noun as specific or unspecific. There are two types of articles: definite and indefinite.
        - Types of Articles:
            - Definite Article: The
                - Usage: 
                    - Refers to a specific or known item.
                - Examples:
                    - The book on the table is mine. (Refers to a specific book that both the speaker and listener know about.)
                    - She gave me the pen. (Refers to a specific pen, known to both.)
            - Indefinite Articles: A and An
                - Usage: 
                    - Used when the noun is unspecified or mentioned for the first time.
                    - A is used before words that start with a consonant sound.
                    - An is used before words that start with a vowel sound.
                - Examples:
                    - I saw a car on the road. (Any car, not specific.)
                    - She is an engineer. (Refers to any engineer, not specific.)
        - Rules:
            - Use “the” when the noun is specific or previously mentioned.
            - Use “a” or “an” for general or non-specific items.
            - “A” is used before words beginning with a consonant sound.
            - “An” is used before words beginning with a vowel sound (e.g., an apple, an hour).
            - Do not use articles with uncountable nouns or plural nouns in a general sense.
                - E.g., She loves music (uncountable), Books are useful (plural).

- Sentence Structure
    - Basic Sentence Structure:
        - In English, sentences follow a basic Subject + Verb + Object (SVO) structure:
            - Subject (S): 
                - The person or thing performing the action.
            - Verb (V): 
                - The action or state of being.
            - Object (O): 
                - The person or thing receiving the action.
        - Exceptions:
            - In questions, the word order changes: Auxiliary Verb + Subject + Main Verb.
            - Example: 
                - Do you like chocolate? (Here, “do” is the auxiliary verb.)
        - Examples:
            - She (S) reads (V) books (O).
            - They (S) play (V) football (O).
    - Complex Sentences:
        - A complex sentence contains an independent clause (main clause) and one or more dependent clauses.
            - Independent Clause: 
                - A complete thought that can stand alone as a sentence.
            - Dependent Clause: 
                - Cannot stand alone and depends on the independent clause for meaning.
        - Examples:
            - I stayed home because it was raining.
            - “I stayed home” is the independent clause.
            - “because it was raining” is the dependent clause.
        - Rules:
            - In complex sentences, the independent clause can usually stand alone, but the dependent clause cannot.
            - Subordinating conjunctions (e.g., because, although, if) are used to connect the dependent and independent clauses.
            - Punctuation: Use a comma before the conjunction if the dependent clause comes first.
            - E.g., If it rains, we will stay home.

- Tenses
    - Tenses indicate the time of an action or state of being. There are three main types of tenses: Present, Past, and Future. Each tense is further divided into simple, continuous, perfect, and perfect continuous forms.
    - Present Tense:
        - Simple Present: 
            - Describes habitual actions or facts.
            - Example: 
                - She walks to school every day.
        - Present Continuous: 
            - Describes actions happening now or around the present time.
            - Example: 
                - She is walking to school right now.
        - Present Perfect: 
            - Describes actions that have been completed at some point before now.
            - Example: 
                - She has walked to school today.
        - Present Perfect Continuous: 
            - Describes actions that started in the past and are still continuing.
            - Example: 
                - She has been walking to school for 10 minutes.
    - Past Tense:
        - Simple Past: 
            - Describes actions that were completed in the past.
            - Example: 
                - She walked to school yesterday.
        - Past Continuous: 
            - Describes actions that were happening at a particular moment in the past.
            - Example: 
                - She was walking to school when it started raining.
        - Past Perfect: 
            - Describes actions that were completed before another action in the past.
            - Example: 
                - She had walked to school before it started raining.
        - Past Perfect Continuous: 
            - Describes actions that were ongoing in the past up to a certain point.
            - Example: 
                - She had been walking to school for 10 minutes when it started raining.
    - Future Tense:
        - Simple Future: 
            - Describes actions that will happen in the future.
            - Example: 
                - She will walk to school tomorrow.
        - Future Continuous: 
            - Describes actions that will be happening at a specific time in the future.
            - Example: 
                - She will be walking to school at 8 AM tomorrow.
        - Future Perfect: 
            - Describes actions that will be completed before a specific time in the future.
            - Example: 
                - She will have walked to school by 9 AM tomorrow.
        - Future Perfect Continuous: 
            - Describes actions that will be ongoing up until a specific time in the future.
            - Example: 
                - She will have been walking to school for 10 minutes by the time it starts raining

- 10. Punctuation (Brief Overview)
    - Common Punctuation Marks:
        - Full stop (.): 
            - Used to mark the end of a declarative sentence.
        - Comma (,): 
            - Used to separate items in a list or clauses in a sentence.
        - Question mark (?): 
            - Used at the end of a direct question.
        - Exclamation mark (!): 
            - Used to express strong emotion or surprise.
        - Apostrophe ('): 
            - Shows possession or contraction.
        - Examples:
            - Sarah's book (possession)
            - Don't (contraction of do not)

- Conditionals (Brief Overview)
    - Definition:
        - Conditionals are sentences expressing “if” or “when” something happens, or is imagined to happen.
    - Types of Conditionals:
        1.	Zero Conditional: 
            - Describes general truths.
            - If + Present Simple, Present Simple.
            - Example: 
                - If you heat water, it boils.
        2.	First Conditional: 
            - Describes real and possible situations in the future.
            - If + Present Simple, will + base verb.
            - Example: 
                - If it rains, I will stay home.
        3.	Second Conditional: 
            - Describes hypothetical situations in the present or future.
            - If + Past Simple, would + base verb.
            - Example: 
                - If I were rich, I would travel the world.
        4.	Third Conditional: 
            - Describes hypothetical situations in the past.
            - If + Past Perfect, would have + past participle.
            - Example: 
                - If I had studied harder, I would have passed the exam.
